## Answers: Compounds

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)

    The one ending is on *-upāyāsā*, and it is a masculine nominative plural. It is the last member that carries it, as *ekavibhattitā* says it must, and it therefore settles three things at once for a word of twenty-two letters: the case, the number and the gender of the whole. Everything to its left stands in the bare stem. So the compound is an *itaritara dvanda*, declined as a plural in the gender of its last member, and it is a subject of *sambhavanti*.

    The other two words are compounds as well, of two further classes. *Jātipaccayā* is *jāti* + *paccaya*, a *tappurisa* standing in the ablative of cause, 'with birth as condition'. *Jarāmaraṇaṁ* is *jarā* + *maraṇa*, a *dvanda* of the other kind: collective, neuter singular, the two treated as one thing. Three compounds, three classes, four words.

    The five members fail to look up because a stem is not a word. The compound contains *soka*, *parideva*, *dukkha*, *domanassa*, *upāyāsa*; a page of canon has *soko*, *paridevo*, *domanassaṁ*, *upāyāso*. Only *dukkha* has an independent life, because it is also an adjective: *dukkha* has 303 root-text occurrences where the other four have almost none outside the Visuddhimagga. A search for a member spelled as the compound spells it will usually find nothing, and the search is not at fault.

2. 'sambādho gharāvāso rajopatho, abbhokāso pabbajjā. (MN 27 — M i 179)

    Two compounds, one word that looks like a third, and no verb anywhere. *Gharāvāso* is a locative *tappurisa*, 'dwelling in a house'. *Rajopatho* is a *kammadhāraya*, 'a path that is dust', standing here in apposition to *gharāvāso*.

    The trap is *abbhokāso*. It looks like *abbha* 'cloud' plus *okāsa* 'space', which would make it a compound and a rather beautiful one. DPD gives *abhi* > *abbh-* + *ava* + √*kās*, Sanskrit *abhyavakāśa*: a root with two prefixes on it, Chapter 14's material and not this chapter's at all. A plausible split may not be a real one, and only the dictionary can tell them apart.

3. Tirokuṭṭesu tiṭṭhanti, sandhisiṅghāṭakesu ca; Dvārabāhāsu tiṭṭhanti, āgantvāna sakaṁ gharaṁ. (Kp 7)

    *Tirokuṭṭesu* is an *avyayībhāva*, here inflected as a locative plural instead of frozen in the usual *-aṁ*; item 23 asks for the same word cold. That shows the class is defined by its first member and not by its ending. An *avyayībhāva* is a compound whose governing member is an indeclinable, and the usual frozen accusative is the normal result of that, not its definition.

    *Sandhisiṅghāṭakesu* is a *dvanda*; *dvārabāhāsu* a genitive *tappurisa*. Note the spelling: Collins gives this word as *tiro-kudda*, and both romanised editions consulted print *kuṭṭa* with the cerebral. That is an orthographic question and was checked against editions, not against a corpus, as it must be whenever the disagreement is about spelling.

4. Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacittaṁ uppādesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)

    Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacitto āmasi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)

    ***Theyyacittaṁ*** is a genitive *tappurisa*, neuter accusative singular, the object of *uppādesi*: it names a thought, and *citta* is neuter. ***Theyyacitto*** is the same two members standing masculine and nominative, agreeing with *bhikkhu*: a *bahubbīhi* by use, and it names no thought at all. It describes the monk who has one. Inside the compound nothing changed. Only the ending did, and there the canon demonstrates the whole of the *bahubbīhi* rule on itself.

    The second sentence has no word for a thought because it does not need one: the thought has been absorbed into an adjective and hung on the man. This is what the chapter means by saying the class points outwards. DPD records the pair as two entries under one construction (the neuter noun 'intention to steal' and the adjective 'intending to steal, with theft in mind', both of them *theyya* + *citta*), so the same compound is entered twice: the same point, reached from a dictionary's side. *Theyyacitto* has 87 root-text occurrences and *theyyacittaṁ* 2, and the masculine can only be the adjectival use.

    The Vinaya then does what a grammar cannot: it attaches a consequence to the difference. The first case ends *anāpatti, bhikkhu, cittuppāde*, "there is no offence, monk, in the arising of a thought" (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56). The second, where the monk touched the cloth with theft in mind, ends *āpatti dukkaṭassā* (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56), an offence of wrong conduct. One ending on one compound is the difference between a thought that is not an offence and a man who has committed one.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna sāsanaṁ. (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

    Four compounds, three classes. *Sabbapāpassa* is a *kammadhāraya* governed by the genitive relation inside *sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ*, which is itself an uncompounded genitive *tappurisa* written as two words, and the question asks for that relation. The same relation could have been written *sabbapāpākaraṇaṁ* and was not. So compounding is a choice, not an obligation: an *anicca samāsa* is assembled for the occasion, so the occasion may equally decline to assemble one, and a two-word genitive means nothing by staying two words.

    *Akaraṇaṁ* is the negative prefix compounded with an action noun. *Sacittapariyodapanaṁ* is three members deep, and its first member is the reflexive possessive *sa-* (DPD's Sanskrit is *svacetas*), so *sacitta* is 'one's own mind' and the whole is a genitive *tappurisa* over it. *Buddhāna* is a metrical genitive plural for *buddhānaṁ*, which is the verse's business, not this chapter's.

6. svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko paccattaṁ veditabbo viññūhī'ti. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

    Only two of the four have a compound underneath, and the dictionary is the only thing that will tell you which.

    - ***Akāliko***: *akāla* + *ika*, and *akāla* is *na* > *a* + *kāla*, the negative prefix compounded with a noun. A compound, and the suffix has made an adjective of it as the chapter says.
    - ***Ehipassiko***: *ehi* + *passa* + *ika*, two second-person imperatives with the suffix on the end. A compound, and of the strangest kind in the chapter.
    - ***Sandiṭṭhiko***: DPD gives *saṁ* + √*dis* + *ti* + *ka*, that is *sandiṭṭhi* + *ka*. A prefix, a root and two suffixes: no compound anywhere in it.
    - ***Opaneyyiko***: *upaneyya* + *ika*, where *upaneyya* is *upa* + √*nī* with a gerundive suffix. Again no compound, and this one shows the vowel change: *upa* strengthens to *opa* before the suffix, which is the same process that turns *punabbhava* into *ponobhavika*.

    This does not make the chapter's statement wrong; it makes it narrower than it sounds. The claim is that *-ika* turns a finished compound into an adjective, and both compounds here confirm it. The other two add that the suffix does not belong to compounds alone: it takes a prefixed root-derivative just as readily, and *svākkhāto* at the head of the line is a third such word without the suffix at all, *su* > *sv* + *ā* + √*khā* + *ta*. So the suffix is evidence of nothing on its own. A word ending in *-ika* is a word to look up, not a compound to split.

    *Ehipassiko* fits no class because its members are finite verb forms, and all six classes assume nominal members. Collins files it under compounds built out of sentences, a heading he takes from Western scholarship, not from the tradition, and the tradition's silence is itself informative: this is a word the language made and the grammarians did not provide for. The Dhamma is a 'come-and-see!' thing.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Recall.**

7. ***Ekapadatta***, 'the fact of being one word', and ***ekavibhattitā***, 'the fact of having one inflection'. A reader uses the second, because it says where to look: the ending is on the last member alone, so the last member gives the case, the number and the gender of the whole.

8. **Number and case.** The uninflected members carry neither. *Buddhasāsanaṁ* is 'the teaching of the Buddha' or 'of the Buddhas' indifferently, and the genitive relation that makes it 'of' is nowhere written; it has to be recovered from sense and context. A third thing could fairly be added: a compound does not always keep its first member to itself, since a word elsewhere in the sentence may construe with it.

9. **Copulative and determinative.** In a copulative compound, the *dvanda*, the members are equals and would have been joined by *ca*: *devamanussā* 'gods and humans'. In a determinative compound the first member qualifies the second in some way, and everything else in the chapter is a subdivision of how.

10. Because a *bahubbīhi* is a **use**, not a formation. Any compound of any class becomes one the moment it is used as an adjective describing something outside itself. *Bahu* + *vīhi* is an ordinary descriptive compound meaning 'much rice'; said of a field it means 'having much rice', and the rice is no longer what the word is about. Collins, Bodhi and Thānuttamo all say this outright, and Duroiselle demonstrates it by running each of the other classes in turn under the heading 'used relatively'.

11. A *nicca samāsa* has a dictionary entry and an *anicca samāsa* does not. A permanent compound is one whose members do not occur apart in that sense (*kammakāro*, *paṭiccasamuppādo*, the name *Arindamo*), so it is a lexical item and is listed. An impermanent compound was assembled for the occasion and could always have been said as separate words; these are the vast majority, and looking for one in a dictionary is a waste of time. When a long word fails to resolve, do not first suspect the dictionary.

**Split the compound.**

12. *vijjā* + *caraṇa* + *sampanna*. No sound change: the *-ā* of *vijjā* stands, and the join before *sampanna* is clean.

13. *pubbe* + *nivāsa* + *anussati* + *ñāṇa*, with *nivāsa* + *anussati* → *nivāsānussati* by ordinary vowel sandhi. The trap is *pubbe*. It is a locative singular and it has kept its ending inside the compound, an *alutta* compound, standing in the middle of a long word where it looks like an error.

14. *appa* + *rajas* + *akkha* + *jātika*, with *rajas* → *raja-* by the *-as* stem rule. Four members, not three; DPD's construction field gives this.

15. *mahā* + *phala*, with the initial *ph-* doubled at the join and the *ā* of *mahā* shortened in consequence, since a long vowel and a following double consonant cannot both stand. Reading it as *mahap* + *phala* and hunting for *mahap* is the mistake this item exists to prevent.

16. *bahula* + *kata*, with the stem-final *-a* becoming *-ī-* before a form of √*kar*. This is a pattern, not an exception, and so many canonical words end in *-īkata* and *-ībhūta*. Meeting an unfamiliar one, split it there.

17. *hatthin* + *go* + *assa* + *vaḷavā*, a four-member *dvanda*: 'elephants, cattle, horses and mares'. The *-in* stem drops its nasal to give *hatthi-*, and *go* becomes *gav-* before the vowel of *assa*.

18. **Not a compound.** *Ekamantaṁ* 'to one side' is the sandhi of two separate words, *ekaṁ antaṁ*, which the editions happen to print joined. Nor is this an isolated oddity: Oberlies points out that words in close syntactical juncture are quite generally treated as though they were members of a compound, and gives *na-ccaje* and *tatra-ssu*, where the negative particle doubles its verb's initial as a compound join would. Compounding and habitual sandhi are two points on one scale, and where a given text falls on it is often an editor's decision.

**Name the class.**

19. ***Dvanda***: 'the teaching and the training'. Separated it would be *dhammo ca vinayo ca*. It is declined as a singular in the gender of its last member, which is one of the three patterns a *dvanda* may take.

20. ***Tappurisa*, genitive**: *bhikkhūnaṁ saṅgho*, 'the assembly of the monks'. The genitive is much the commonest relation, so an unfamiliar *tappurisa* should be tried as a genitive first.

21. ***Kammadhāraya***, adverb plus noun: *sammā* 'rightly' modifying *diṭṭhi* 'view'. Separated, the two would not have stood in different cases, because the first is an indeclinable. Every factor of the noble eightfold path is of this shape.

22. ***Digu***, and specifically a collective (*samāhāra*) *digu*: *cha* + *āyatana*, with *cha* in its compounding form *saḷ-*. The six are collected into one concept and the compound is a neuter singular: 'the sixfold sense base', not 'six sense bases'. Had it been an *asamāhāra digu* it would have stood in the plural.

23. ***Avyayībhāva***: *tiro* 'across, beyond' plus *kuṭṭa* 'wall'. Collins resolves it as *kuṭṭānaṁ tiro*. It is an adverb by formation, and the form here is a locative plural, not the usual frozen *-aṁ*, one of the ways this class varies.

24. ***Bahubbīhi***, from a *tappurisa*: *khīṇa* 'exhausted' + *āsava* 'corruption', hence 'one whose corruptions are exhausted'. Note that it stands alone, with no *bhikkhu* beside it, the second of the three consequences the chapter draws from the *bahubbīhi* being a use.

25. The trap is that the case ending looks like a mistake and is not. *Manasikāro* is an *alutta* compound: *manasi* is a locative singular of *mano*, kept intact, plus *kāra* 'making'. 'Work in the mind', hence 'attention'. It occurs a hundred and thirty-seven times in the root texts, so it is an ordinary word, not an oddity, built on a class many coursebooks pass over.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Build the form.**

26. *Dukkhassa samudayo*, 'the origin of suffering': a genitive *tappurisa*. If you supplied a different case, say which and why; several relations are formally possible here and the sense settles it.

27. *Sīlaṁ eva dhanaṁ*, 'the wealth which is virtue itself'. The two members are in **apposition**, not in a genitive relation: virtue does not possess the wealth, it *is* it. Rendering it 'the wealth of virtue' turns an identity into a possession, as rendering turns *avijjāpaccayā* 'with the condition that is ignorance' into 'the condition of ignorance', and *satisambojjhaṅgo* 'the awakening factor that is mindfulness' into 'the awakening factor of mindfulness'. Neither rendering is quite wrong, and neither is what the compound says.

28. ***Subhāvitacitto***, masculine nominative singular. The point of the exercise is the gender: *cittaṁ* is neuter, but a *bahubbīhi* agrees with the noun it describes and not with its own last member, so a neuter noun at the end of the compound comes out masculine here. Six occurrences in the root texts, and the same thing happens in reverse with a feminine last member. Almost all *bahubbīhis* follow the *-a* declension whatever they end in.

29. The canon has *hatthacchinno*. *Chinnahattho* occurs nowhere in any layer of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; *hatthacchinno* occurs five times in the root texts, in the Mahāvagga's list of those who may not be ordained, beside *pādacchinno* and *hatthapādacchinno*. Oberlies names this as a general phenomenon, "compounds with an inversion of the expected word-order", and gives *akkhacchinno*, *aṅgulicchinnaṁ*, *veṇikatā* and *vaṅkaghastā* alongside it. The implication needs stating carefully: a grammar's rules and its illustrations are not equally reliable. Duroiselle's seven-way scheme of *bahubbīhi* case-relations is sound and is taught in this chapter; not one of the five compounds he illustrates it with occurs in the canon, because they are the standing examples of the Sanskrit grammatical tradition reproduced without a mark to say so. Take the rule, check the form.

**Read the sentence.**

30. - Saḷāyatanañcidaṁ, bhikkhave, kiṁnidānaṁ kiṁsamudayaṁ kiṁjātikaṁ kiṁpabhavaṁ? (MN 11 — M i 67)
    - *saḷāyatanañcidaṁ* < *saḷāyatanaṁ* the sixfold sense base + *ca* and + *idaṁ* this · *bhikkhave* monks · *kiṁnidānaṁ* having what as its source · *kiṁsamudayaṁ* having what as its origin · *kiṁjātikaṁ* having what as its birth · *kiṁpabhavaṁ* having what as its production
    - And this sixfold sense base, monks — what is its source, what its origin, what its birth, what its production?

    Four *bahubbīhis* in a row on the interrogative pronoun, each agreeing with *saḷāyatanaṁ*. The trap is that they look like nouns: *kiṁnidānaṁ* means 'having what as its source?', not 'what source?', so the whole phrase is a question about *saḷāyatanaṁ*, not a list of four questions. The Pāli answers itself two segments later in the same form, *saḷāyatanaṁ nāmarūpanidānaṁ nāmarūpasamudayaṁ*, where the same compounds are built on *nāmarūpa* instead.

31. - Santi sattā apparajakkhajātikā, assavanatā dhammassa parihāyanti. (SN 6.1 — S i 137)
    - *santi* there are · *sattā* beings · *apparajakkhajātikā* < *appa* little + *rajas* dust + *akkha* eye + *jātika* of the kind · *assavanatā* through not hearing · *dhammassa* of the teaching · *parihāyanti* they fall away
    - There are beings with little dust in their eyes who are falling away through not hearing the teaching.

    This is one of the chapter's examples, chosen on purpose, and doing the analysis unaided differs from following it. Four members, the *-as* rule operating in the middle, and the whole a *bahubbīhi* describing *sattā*: nothing inside the compound names a being. Look again at *assavanatā* too: DPD parses it as *na* + *savana* + *tā*, an abstract noun in the instrumental, so the causal sense is carried by the case and not by anything in the compound.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

32. Idaṁ kho pana, bhikkhave, dukkhasamudayaṁ ariyasaccaṁ (SN 56.11 — S v 421). *Dukkhasamudayaṁ* is a genitive *tappurisa*; *ariyasaccaṁ* is a *kammadhāraya*; and the first qualifies the second, so a compound serves as an adjective, a *bahubbīhi* by use, inside one of the most familiar sentences in the canon.

33. **Tirokuṭṭesu tiṭṭhanti** (Kp 7).

**Read at length.**

34. The passage is MN 27 — M i 178: the arising of a Realised One, his teaching, and a householder's going forth. It recurs, with small variations, in dozens of suttas, so the twenty minutes are well spent.

    - The compound whose first member keeps its ending is ***majjhekalyāṇaṁ*** 'good in the middle', at 11.3. *Majjhe* is a **locative** singular, kept intact, and it stands between *ādikalyāṇaṁ* and *pariyosānakalyāṇaṁ*, neither of which keeps its ending, so the contrast is visible on one line. It is Perniola's example of the *alutta* compound, and here it is in a formula every reader knows.
    - They are ***bahubbīhis*** built with ***saha*** 'with' reduced to *sa-*: 'having gods', 'having Māras', 'having Brahmās', 'having ascetics and brahmins', 'having gods and humans'. Each agrees with the noun it describes (*lokaṁ* for the first three, *pajaṁ* for the last two), and two of them have a *dvanda* inside the *bahubbīhi*. This is the *sa-* possessive that `04-gender-and-adjectives` meets from the other end.
    - Because *vā … vā* is 'or' and a *dvanda* is 'and'. Compounding *gahapati* and *gahapatiputta* would have said that a householder **and** a householder's son heard it; the passage means either one. A *dvanda* can occasionally be read disjunctively, but where the disjunction is the point Pāli writes *vā*.
    - An *itaritara dvanda* would have given ***kesamassūni*** or similar, a plural in the gender of the last member, and the sense would have shifted from 'hair-and-beard' as one thing to be shaved to hairs and beards counted separately. The collective is the right choice and the canon makes it a hundred and forty-four times.

**The canon's compound list.**

35. "'Kings' means: a king of the earth, a king of a region, a ruler of a district, a ruler of a border area, judges, government officials, or whoever passes sentence of mutilation and execution. That is what 'kings' means."

    ***Pathabyārājā*** is *pathabyā* 'of the earth' + *rājā* 'king', a **tappurisa** in the genitive relation: a king *of* the earth. Its first member is the genitive of *pathavī*, not the stem, in the irregular form *pathabyā* that DPD gives beside *pathaviyā*, so it is also an ***alutta*** compound: the first member keeps the ending it would have as a separate word. ***Padesarājā*** is the same class on the same second member: *padesa* 'region' + *rājā*, a king of a region. Both keep the gender and declension of *rājan*, their last member, as a *tappurisa* does.

    ***Chejjabhejjaṁ*** is a **dvanda**, not a *tappurisa*: *chejja* 'what is to be cut' and *bhejja* 'what is to be broken', two gerundives joined by an understood 'and'. It is the collective kind, taking a neuter singular ending for the pair instead of agreeing with either member, and so it is *chejjabhejjaṁ* and not a plural. The test is whether the members stand in a relation to each other or merely side by side, and here neither cuts the other: they are two punishments named together.

    The relative clause at the end, *ye vā pana … anusāsanti*, does what a list cannot: it catches whoever else holds the power, without naming them. Chapter 17 treats the construction; here it is enough that the canon reaches for a relative clause where its compounds run out.

**Reaching back.**

36. All three are compounds; two of the three are *bahubbīhis* and one is disputable, and that makes the item useful. *Manopubbaṅgamā* is *mano* + *pubbaṅgama* 'going before', a *tappurisa* used as an adjective describing *dhammā*, hence 'having mind as their forerunner'. *Manoseṭṭhā* is the same shape: *mano* + *seṭṭha* 'chief', describing *dhammā*, 'having mind as their chief'. Both are *bahubbīhis* because the mind they name is not what the word is about; *dhammā* is. *Manomayā* is different: DPD parses it as *mano* + *maya*, 'made of mind', where *-maya* is a suffix meaning 'consisting of', not an independent word, so it is an adjective by formation and does not need the *bahubbīhi* analysis at all. How you know, in every case: ask whether the thing the compound names is the thing the compound is about. If it is not, the compound is exocentric.

    Chapter 2's point still holds alongside all of this. The *-o* of *mano* stands unchanged before each of the three following consonants; no rule applies and none did.

37. **A noun's work.** *Sammāsambuddho* stands in a list of epithets, all nominative singular masculine, in apposition to *tathāgato*: 'a Realised One arises in the world, worthy, fully awakened, …'. The past participle *sambuddha* is here a substantive, the title, not a predicate saying that something has been awakened. The syntax decides it, not the form: there is already a finite verb, *uppajjati*, and *sammāsambuddho* is not it. Chapter 10 makes the general point that a past participle may serve as adjective, as noun, or as the verb of its sentence, and that only the sentence shows which.

38. Because *avyayībhāva* is a statement about how the word was **made** and 'indeclinable' is a statement about what it now **is**. The name says so itself: *avyayībhāva* is 'becoming indeclinable'. Chapter 12 is interested in *yathābhūtaṁ* as a word a reader meets, a fixed adverb, nearly two thousand times in the root texts, modifying a verb of knowing; this chapter is interested in where its form came from.

**Say it aloud.**

39. The odd compound is ***maggāmaggassa***, *magga* 'path' compounded with its own negation *amagga* 'non-path'. Read as an ordinary *dvanda* it says 'of path and non-path', which is close to meaningless; read as the type Oberlies calls the *phalāphala* type, it names the whole question of the path: one skilled in what is the way and what is not. The canon has others of the shape: *kusalākusalaṁ* 'the wholesome and the unwholesome', *piyāppiyaṁ* 'the liked and the disliked'. Each names a field, not two items.

    Sound out two more compounds in the verse as you read. *Gambhīrapaññaṁ* is a *bahubbīhi*, 'of deep wisdom', accusative because it agrees with the *taṁ* of the last line. And *uttamatthamanuppattaṁ* hides a sandhi as well as a compound: *uttamattham* + *anuppattaṁ*, 'who has attained the highest goal', where *uttama* + *attha* is itself a *kammadhāraya*. Three of the verse's four lines carry a compound, which is a fair sample of the Dhammapada.
