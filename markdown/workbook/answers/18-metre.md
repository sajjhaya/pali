## Answers: Metre

Seven items carry a trap: **8**, **17**, **19**, **20**, **26**, **27** and **35**. Each is named where it falls.

Every scansion below has been produced by rule and checked against the line as the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints it, so where your marks differ from these, chase the difference instead of shrugging at it.

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. The five are *dā*, *ve*, *pā*, *van* and *dham*, and they fall into three groups. ***Dā*** and ***pā*** are heavy because the vowel is **long**, the plainest of the three conditions and the only one most readers expect. ***Van*** and ***dham*** are heavy because a **conjunct** follows: *-nt-* in *pātubhavanti*, *-mm-* in *dhammā*. Neither of those four syllables is a surprise.

***Ve*** is the fourth, and it catches many readers. Its vowel is written with a single letter, its syllable is open, and it is heavy, because *e* is **long by origin**. A reader working from "one letter, one short vowel" marks it ⏑, and the line then has a light syllable where the Tuṭṭhubha's opening will not take one. Item 11 states this fact as a rule; here it is met before it is stated, the right order.

2. All but one of the heavy syllables are heavy because the vowel is long. *Ā*, *tā*, *jhā* and *brāh* have *ā*, and nobody misreads those. The two that would have caught you are ***no*** and ***to***, whose vowel is *o*: written with a single letter, standing in an open syllable, and long by origin as the *e* of item 1 is. The exception is ***ṇas***, heavy because of the conjunct *-ss-* of *brāhmaṇassa*. So this line is almost entirely a line of long vowels, where item 1 divided its weight three ways.

    *——⏑—¦—⏑—¦—⏑—×*, and against item 1 the marks differ at **position 1** and **position 7**. The first does not matter: the opening syllable of a Tuṭṭhubha is anceps, free to be either, and its being heavy here and light there has no consequence. The second matters. Positions five to seven are the **break**, and item 1 has the normal —⏑⏑ where this line has —⏑—. That is neither a fault nor a variation to be explained away; it is the commonest of the alternatives, and item 27 turns the same difference into a trap by putting it where a reader will read it as damage.

3. It is evidence that the two lines are in the same metre, and that is all it is. Eleven positions, the same cadence, the same break: a metre is a pattern of weights, and weight is indifferent to which words carry it. Line four has the same eleven marks with a third set of words again, so three of the four lines of this verse are metrically identical and lexically unrelated.

It is *not* evidence about how the lines are **cut**. The **¦** in these scansions marks the Tuṭṭhubha's own division (opening, break, cadence), not a word break, and the two do not coincide. Item 1 divides its words after the fourth syllable, *Yadā have* ¦ *pātubhavanti dhammā*; this line divides after the fifth, *Athassa kaṅkhā* ¦ *vapayanti sabbā*. Identical marks, different cuts. Nor is it evidence that the lines sound alike: they share a rhythm and nothing more. Item 36 is the case where that difference between marks and cuts decides an analysis.

4. The unmarked syllable is ***ti***, the quotation marker closing the udāna. It is outside the metre and is not counted: the fact item 26 asks you to find for yourself, met here as given information. Note also that the canon does not have the word before it as *sahetudhammaṁ* standing free: the niggahīta has assimilated to *n* before the *t* of *ti*, which is sandhi and has nothing to do with the metre.

Eleven syllables to each of the four lines, once *ti* is set aside. That settles the family at once: the count is fixed, the cadence is fixed, and the middle of the line varies: the **flexible syllabic** family, *vaṇṇacchandas* in its first sense. Eleven syllables within that family makes it **Tuṭṭhubha**. Item 35 is the same metre with one line of twelve in it, and item 15 is the family list this answer has just used.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. *⏑———¦⏑——×* — eight. *Ma-no-pub-baṅ-ga-mā-dham-mā*. *Pub-* is heavy for *-bb-*, *-baṅ-* for *-ṅg-*, *dham-* for *-mm-*.

6. *⏑———¦⏑—⏑×* — eight. Identical to item 5 in the first half and different in the seventh syllable, which is the whole difference between a Siloka odd line and an even one.

7. *⏑⏑——¦⏑——×* — eight. *Na* and *hi* are both light, which is the ⏑⏑ opening the metre normally avoids in second and third position but tolerates in first and second.

8. **Trap.** *—⏑——¦⏑—⏑×* — eight. The trap is the first syllable: *esa* begins with *e*, which is heavy although the syllable is open and the vowel is written with one letter. A reader working from "short vowels are light" marks it ⏑ and then cannot make the line fit.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Recall.**

9. Of a vowel: *rassa* short, *dīgha* long. Of a syllable: *lahu* light, *garu* heavy. The first pair is about **length**, the second about **weight**, and they do not track each other: a syllable with a short vowel is heavy whenever a conjunct or the niggahīta follows it, which is most of the time. Duroiselle glosses *lahu* and *garu* as "short" and "long" syllables, and that single slip makes the subject unlearnable.

10. Heavy if the vowel is **long**; or if a **conjunct** follows it, in the same word or the next; or if the **niggahīta** follows it. Light if none of the three holds, that is, a short *a, i, u* with at most one consonant after it.

11. *E* and *o* are long by origin, so they make a syllable heavy in an open syllable; where a conjunct follows they are pronounced short, but the syllable is then heavy twice over, once for the vowel and once for the conjunct. A syllable containing *e* or *o* is never light by rule. It can be scanned light by licence, as in the chapter's Ratanasutta case.

12. *Vaṅka*, "bent", for the heavy syllable; *uju*, "straight", for the light (Vuttodaya 7). The European convention is the reverse (a bent **⏑** for light and a straight **—** for heavy), so the same pattern printed under the two systems looks like its own mirror image. Nothing in the marks says which system is in use.

13. A **word break**. The Vuttodaya glosses it *padaccheda*, "the cutting of words" (v. 10). It is **not a pause**: reading it as one produces a recitation no tradition uses.

14. DN 20 — D ii 255, the Mahāsamaya Sutta, *Silokamanukassāmi*. The commentary glosses *siloka* there as *akkharapadaniyamitaṁ vacanasaṅghātaṁ*, "an assemblage of words regulated by syllable and by line": as a metre, that is, and not in the word's commoner sense, "fame, praise".

15. Flexible syllabic (*vaṇṇacchandas* 1), fixed by the **number of syllables** with the cadence fixed and the rest free. Measure (*mattāchandas*), fixed by the **number of measures**, the syllable count varying. Bar (*gaṇacchandas*), the measures grouped into bars of four. Fixed syllabic (*vaṇṇacchandas* 2), fixed by the weight of nearly every syllable.

16. The last syllable of a line counts as **two measures** whatever its actual weight. It matters in the **measure metres** and the bar metres, where the arithmetic is the definition of the metre: without it every even line of every *mattāchandas* metre comes out one measure short.

**Scan the line.**

17. **Trap.** *——⏑—¦—⏑⏑×* — eight. Two traps in one line, in fact. *Dham-mas-sa-va-naṁ* has its heavy syllables from *-mm-* and *-ss-*, not from any long vowel; and the cadence is **—⏑⏑×**, which is not the *pathyā*'s ⏑——× and not the even line's ⏑—⏑×. The line is neither broken nor an even line: it is a named variation, and item 34 asks for its name.

18. *——⏑—¦———×* — eight, and every syllable of the cadence heavy.

19. **Trap.** *——⏑⏑—⏑—⏑×* — **nine**, and the question is the wrong one. This is the opening of the Jarāsutta, a **Vetālīya**, and Vetālīya is a measure metre: the syllable count varies from line to line by design and tells you nothing. Count the measures instead and the line is fourteen, as the metre requires of an odd line. Counting syllables in a *mattāchandas* metre is the commonest way to conclude that a sound line is corrupt.

20. **Trap.** *——⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×* — eleven, a textbook Tuṭṭhubha. The trap is *-bhi-* in *bahunābhichanno*: it is followed by *ch*, which is a **digraph and one sound**, so the syllable stays open and light. Read *ch* as a conjunct and the syllable comes out heavy, the break reads —⏑— instead of —⏑⏑, and the line looks like a variation when it is the normal form.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Now say what disturbed the count.**

21. Nine as written. *Cariyā* carries a **sarabhatti** vowel; drop it and the word is *car-yā*, restoring the conjunct that makes position. *——⏑—¦⏑——×* — eight, and a *pathyā* odd line.

22. Nine as written, and the same accommodation: *kiriyā* is *kir-yā*, from Sanskrit *kriyā*. *——⏑—¦⏑——×* — eight, *pathyā* odd line. Note that the two lines end up with identical scansions, as a fixed cadence ensures.

23. Nine as written, and this one needs **two** accommodations, not one. Release *br-* from making position, so that *ca* stays light; *brahma-* is on Ānandajoti's list of words where *br* regularly fails. Then drop the sarabhatti vowel of *cariya*. *⏑—⏑—¦⏑——×* — eight.

24. Nine as written, and again two. The sarabhatti vowel of *ariya* goes, and the line has **already** lost the niggahīta of *ariyasaccānaṁ*: the editor's apostrophe marks it. *—⏑——¦⏑—⏑×* — eight, an even line. Each accommodation has a separate effect: keep the vowel and the line has nine syllables; keep the niggahīta and it has eight but closes ——⏑× instead of the required ⏑—⏑×.

25. Nine as written, and nothing has been altered: this is **resolution**, not licence. The first syllable is realised as two light ones, which the Siloka's profile allows in that position: *[⏑⏑]—⏑—¦⏑——×*. Read that way the line is a *pathyā* odd line of eight positions carried by nine syllables.

26. **Trap.** Ten as printed, against the refrain's eight, and the two extra syllables have two different explanations. The quotation marker ***ti* is outside the metre** and is not counted, which removes one. The other is not a licence at all: *Taṁ tesaṁ* simply has one syllable more than the *Etaṁ* of the refrain, and the line runs to nine by resolution as item 25's does. The trap is expecting a single cause. A line can be long for two unrelated reasons at once, and finding one of them is not finishing.

27. **Trap.** Eleven syllables, the right number, and the line scans *——⏑—¦—⏑—¦—⏑—×*. Note two points. The division is *pa-gā-ḷho* and **not** *pa-gāḷ-ho*: *ḷh* is a digraph and one sound, so it cannot close the preceding syllable. Getting that wrong happens to give the right total here, and that makes it a trap: the count checks out and the analysis is wrong. And the break is —⏑— where the Tuṭṭhubha's normal break is —⏑⏑. That is no fault: Ānandajoti lists —⏑—, ⏑⏑— and ⏑⏑⏑ as patterns that occur in this position, the normal one accounting for about eighty-five per cent of cases and no more.

**Metre as the cause.**

28. In full the line is *etaṁ buddhānaṁ sāsanaṁ*, which scans *——————⏑×*. Its last four positions are ——⏑×. The Siloka's even line requires the cadence **⏑—⏑×** and admits no other, so the full form does not scan. Drop the niggahīta and *-na* is light: *————¦⏑—⏑×*, as required. One nasal is the difference between the best-known verse in the Dhammapada being in metre and being out of it.

29. ***Ghātvā*** should not be taught. It does not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all, in any text, and neither does any word containing *ghātv*, while the ordinary *ghāyitvā* stands eighty-eight times in the root texts. *Daṭṭhu* is sound, with sixteen root-text occurrences. The canon's usage settles it, and the point of checking is that fidelity to a source cannot catch a source being wrong: everything around this sentence in Ānandajoti is correct.

30. Lengthen or shorten a **vowel**; double or simplify a **consonant**; add or drop the **niggahīta**. The commonest is the change of niggahīta to labial *-m* at the end of a word, and it is the hardest to demonstrate for the reason that makes it common: the same change happens in prose, where the metre is not a consideration, so no individual instance can be shown to have happened *because of* the metre.

31. In *upekhā* the *e* stands in an open syllable and is heavy because *e* is long by origin. In *upekkhā* the *e* is pronounced short, but the syllable is closed by the conjunct *-kkh-* and is heavy for that reason instead. The weight is the same and the cause is different, so the four terms in answer 9 have to be kept apart.

**Name the pattern.**

32. The **seventh**. The odd line ends ⏑——× and the even ⏑—⏑×. If the two were identical the verse would be four equivalent lines with nothing marking where a unit ended, and the pairing that makes a Siloka read as couplets, not as a list, would disappear. The Vedic Anuṭṭhubha was that four-identical-line metre; the Siloka is what it became when the two lines diverged.

33. Both scan *——⏑—¦———×*, and both are ***mavipulā***: positions two to four are —⏑—, and the cadence is four heavy syllables. Both also have the word break after the fifth syllable that the chapter says the *mavipulā* normally takes: *Ākaṅkhamānā | sotthānaṁ*, *Sabbattha sotthiṁ | gacchanti*.

34. *——⏑—¦—⏑⏑×* — ***bhavipulā***. Positions two to four are —⏑—, and the cadence is —⏑⏑×. Someone who had learnt only the *pathyā* would find that three lines of a sutta they have recited for years (two *mavipulā* and this *bhavipulā*) end in patterns the rule forbids, and would have to conclude either that the text was corrupt in three places or that the rule was wrong. Both conclusions are available and both are wrong, so learn the table of variations, not only the norm.

35. **Trap.**

    Yathindakhīlo pathavissito siyā  *⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—⏑×*  twelve — **Jagatī**\
    Tathūpamaṁ sappurisaṁ vadāmi    *⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×*   eleven — **Tuṭṭhubha**

    The difference is one light syllable in penultimate position, and that is the whole difference between the two metres: opening and break are identical and the cadence differs by that syllable alone. It is not a fault. A Jagatī line inside an otherwise Tuṭṭhubha verse was always acceptable and in the middle period is so ordinary that it is better to speak of a single Tuṭṭhubha-Jagatī. The trap is that only the count marks the switch, so a reader scanning for a fixed eleven concludes the first line has a syllable too many.

36. ***Ravipulā***, or a ***pathyā*** with the sixth syllable resolved. The marks cannot decide it, because both analyses produce the same weights. The caesura, the word break, decides it. If it falls after the fourth syllable the line is *ravipulā*; if after the fifth, the run of three light syllables is a resolution inside a *pathyā*. No case in the chapter shows more clearly that the *yati* is evidence, not punctuation.

**What the evidence licenses.**

37. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *Chaccābhiṭhānāni **abhabba** kātuṁ*, and its own apparatus records *abhabbo* against the siglum *sī.*, the Sinhalese edition. The Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print *abhabbo*. **The licence disappears.** With *-a* the syllable is light by nature and the line is an ordinary Tuṭṭhubha needing nothing; the first syllable is anceps, so both readings scan. The general lesson is that a claim about a metrically shortened vowel is always also a claim about which edition is in front of you, and the apparatus should be read before the licence is asserted.

38. **The tradition calls it *Vatta***, and Duroiselle, transmitting the Vuttodaya, calls it *Vatta* too. This book calls it the Siloka because that is the name in universal modern use, and because *siloka* is the word the Buddha uses at DN 20 — D ii 255, Ānandajoti's reason for changing to it between his first and second editions. Both names are defensible; they answer different questions, one about the canon and one about the tradition that analysed it.

39. The name in universal modern use is not a Pāli word at all. *Tuṭṭhubha* is a modern Pālicisation of Sanskrit *Triṣṭubh* found in no Pāli text; the tradition's form of the name has a different vowel. The chapter uses the modern name anyway, for the same reason it uses Siloka, and says so.

40. *—⏑——¦⏑—⏑×* — identical to item 8's, mark for mark. Nothing has changed: *e* is long by origin, *sa* is light, *dham* is closed by *-mm-*, *mo* is long, and none of that depends on where the space goes. *Dh* is a digraph and one sound, so it makes no position on either analysis, and no conjunct straddles the join.

So the metre cannot decide it. Scansion settles weight, and weight is a fact about vowels and about the consonants that follow them, not about word boundaries. It would have something to say if a conjunct straddled the join, since the rule counts a conjunct "in the same word or the next", so a division that put two consonants together would make position and one that separated them would not. Here it does not arise.

The one feature where a division becomes metrical evidence is the **yati**, and item 36 is the case where it decides between two analyses the marks cannot separate. Here it falls both ways at once. The Siloka wants its break after the fourth syllable, and both divisions give one there, *esadhammo* ¦ *sanantano* and *esa dhammo* ¦ *sanantano* alike. The divided form has a further break after the second syllable, and the metre neither asks for one nor forbids it.

The division is therefore an editor's decision and not a metrical fact. *Esadhammo* has 1 root-text occurrence, and that one is this line of the Vinaya; everywhere else the root texts print the two words apart. The lesson is item 37's, about licences, in a smaller key: before asking what the metre proves, find out what it is capable of proving.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

41. The operation is **vowel sandhi**: *sammanti* + *idha*, final *-i* and initial *i-* coalescing into a single long *ī*. The line as the canon has it is

    Sammantīdha kudācanaṁ; (Kd 10 — Vin i 349)  *———⏑¦⏑—⏑×*

Eight syllables, and the even line's required cadence ⏑—⏑× at the end of it.

It is none of the three. Item 30's three changes (vowel length, consonant doubling, the niggahīta) each alter the **weight** of a syllable and leave the count where it was. This alters the **count**, by making two syllables into one, and it does so by a rule that operates throughout the language and takes no notice of verse: the same coalescence happens in prose, where no metre is asking for it.

Take that point away. The metre did not make this line; it selected it. Pāli offers the poet a resolved form and a joined form of the same two words, and the metre's whole contribution was to rule out the one that came to nine syllables. A great deal of what looks like metrical shaping is the ordinary language, chosen from, not bent, and the licence lies in the residue that will not yield to that explanation, as at item 28.

**The reading passage.**

42. The six lines scan:

    ```
          ⏑    —    —    —  ¦  ⏑    —    —    ×
          Ma   no   pub  baṅ   ga   mā   dham  mā

          ⏑    —    —    —  ¦  ⏑    —    ⏑    ×
          ma   no   seṭ  ṭhā   ma   no   ma   yā

          ⏑    ⏑    —    —  ¦  ⏑    —    —    ×
          Ma   na   sā   ce    pa   duṭ  ṭhe   na

          —    ⏑    ⏑    —  ¦  ⏑    —    ⏑    ×
          bhā  sa   ti   vā    ka   ro   ti   vā

          ⏑    —    —    —  ¦  ⏑    —    —    ×
          Ta   to   naṁ  duk   kha  man  ve   ti

          —    —    ⏑    ⏑  ¦  ⏑    —    ⏑    ×
          cak  kaṁ  va   va    ha   to   pa   daṁ
    ```

    Eight syllables to the line, every line. That fixes the family at once: it is a flexible syllabic metre, and eight syllables makes it Siloka.

    Lines 1, 3 and 5 are odd lines; 2, 4 and 6 are even, and the marks say so without reference to position. Every odd line closes ⏑——× and every even line closes ⏑—⏑×, and that opposition alone identifies them. All six are *pathyā*; there is no variation anywhere in the verse.

    The verse has six lines, not four, and the chapter's description of the metre accounts for it. A Siloka is dissimilar pairs ("an odd or *prior* line and an even or *posterior* line, the pair repeated"), and here the pair has been repeated three times instead of twice, and because the pair is what the metre is built on, nothing about the structure has to change to accommodate it.
