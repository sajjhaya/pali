## Answers: Absolutives and Infinitives

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Idhāvuso, bhikkhu satthāraṁ upanissāya viharati … (DN 34 — D iii 284)

    *Upanissāya* is the word, an absolutive of *upanissayati* that no longer behaves like one. An ordinary absolutive is adverbial: it names a completed action and hands its agent to the main verb. This one is a postposition. It governs *satthāraṁ*, an accusative, and means 'depending on, close to'.

    So the form has crossed word classes without changing a letter, and the tell is that it has an object. *Nissāya* at item 18 is the same word without the prefix, and *ārabbha* 'concerning' and *paṭicca* 'dependent on' are two more of the class. What an absolutive-as-postposition buys is compression: one word carries both the relation and its object, where English needs a preposition and then a noun.

    Parse *upanissāya* as an ordinary absolutive and you will look for the monk to have done some depending and then done something else, and will not find the second verb until *viharati*, four words later, with *satthāraṁ* stranded.

2. Kicchena me adhigataṁ, halaṁ dāni pakāsituṁ; Rāgadosaparetehi, nāyaṁ dhammo susambudho. (SN 6.1 — S i 136)

    *Pakāsituṁ* is the infinitive, of *pakāsati* 'reveals, makes known': DPD's *pa* + √*kās* + *ituṁ*. It completes *halaṁ*, which DPD resolves as *hi* + *alaṁ*, 'there is indeed no need'. So the construction is impersonal, like *sakkā* + infinitive at item 19 and *sukaraṁ* + infinitive at item 4: a predicate of possibility, ease or sufficiency with no grammatical subject, completed by an infinitive.

    What English has to supply is the agent. "There is no need to reveal it" leaves the revealer unnamed and English tolerates that; a translator who wanted to name him would have to add "for me", and the Pāli has not. *Pakāsituṁ* is indeclinable and carries no person, and the impersonal *halaṁ* has no subject to lend it one.

    *Adhigataṁ* in the line above does have its agent, in the instrumental: *me*, 'by me'. That is the ordinary construction for a passive past participle, which set 09 taught: a transitive verb's participle is passive, and its agent stands in the instrumental. So the verse holds both patterns one line apart: an instrumental agent where the form can take one, and no agent at all where it cannot.

    The verse itself is the hesitation that the Brahmā request answers, so if you worked item 28 of set 09 you have now met both halves of that exchange. *Adhigataṁ* is from *adhigacchati*, whose aorist *ajjhagā* closed the verse in set 08's item 2.

3. Atha kho te bhikkhū sāvatthiyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā … ekamantaṁ nisīdiṁsu. (AN 10.27 — A v 49)

    The subject is *te bhikkhū*, and it is expressed once. Everything else in the sentence, three absolutives and two finite verbs, takes its agent from that one nominative plural. *Caritvā*, *upasaṅkamitvā* and *abhivādetvā* have no agent of their own and cannot have one, because they are indeclinable and have nothing to agree with; the reader supplies the monks and is right.

    That is the default the chapter states, and it makes a sentence of this length readable at speed. A chain of absolutives is Pāli's ordinary way of stringing actions together, and once you see that they all belong to the one subject you can run the whole chain without stopping. Set 08 took this same sentence for its aorists and asked which type each was; the three words between them are this chapter's.

    Notice also *piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā*, a past participle agreeing with *bhikkhū* in the nominative plural. Its function is like that of the absolutives around it, but it works differently: it *agrees*, and so it says something about the monks, not about the action. Item 30 is that difference stated as a rule.

4. Na dāni sukaraṁ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke pariccajituṁ. (MN 76 — M i 524)

    **Instrumental**, *amhehi*, 'by us', and not nominative, because *sukaraṁ … pariccajituṁ* is an impersonal construction with no expressed grammatical subject of its own. There is nothing nominative for *amhehi* to compete with, and the instrumental steps in to mark the one who would do the giving-up.

    It is the same case set 09's passive uses for its agent, now with the equivalent function beside an **active** infinitive: *pariccajituṁ* means 'to give up', not 'to be given up'. So the instrumental here marks an impersonal predicate, not the passive, and if you take it for a passive you will find nothing to be passive.

    Item 2's *halaṁ* is the same construction with a different predicate, and item 19's *sakkā* the same again.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Sakkā bhoge ca bhuñjituṁ puññāni ca kātuṁ. Yannūnāhaṁ … (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)

    ***Bhuñjati* and *karoti* appear four times between them and never twice in the same form. The first sentence has the two infinitives, *bhuñjituṁ* and *kātuṁ*, completing the impersonal *sakkā*. The second has the two optatives**, *bhuñjeyyaṁ* and *kareyyaṁ*, first person singular — Chapter 7's mood, and the one Chapter 8 names for a hypothetical the speaker is entertaining about himself.

    The facts stay the same; who is in them changes. The first sentence states a bare possibility with no person in it at all: it *can* be done. The second puts a first person singular in the middle of that same possibility and wishes it. Nothing in the world has moved between the two sentences; the grammar has moved from an impersonal predicate to a personal mood, and that is all the slide consists of.

    Both sentences are **the same man's thought**, and the second follows the first immediately in the sutta. This is a monk reasoning himself out of the robe, and the passage is said aloud because the reasoning sounds so reasonable: the possibility is stated first, as a fact about the world, and the wish is then hung on it. Two absolutives carry the actual renunciation, *paccakkhāya* and *āvattitvā*, and they are the two words he says fastest.

6. Bhagavāpi, bhante, etarahi … abhisambuddho'ti. (DN 16 — D ii 83)

    The predicate is *abhisambuddho*, a past participle, and there is no finite verb in the sentence at all. The copula is understood, as it regularly is with a nominative predicate. So the whole is held together by a chain of three non-finite forms, the two absolutives *pahāya* and *bhāvetvā* and the participle at the end, all sharing the one nominative subject *bhagavā*.

    In a sentence of thirty words, almost everything comes from this chapter. *Pahāya* is the *-ya* absolutive of *pajahati* and is item 22's build target; *bhāvetvā* is the *-tvā* absolutive of *bhāveti* and is item 20's. Both are tenseless, and item 27 depends on that: the same two words serve a past sentence, a future sentence and a present one without changing.

    *Supatiṭṭhitacitto* is a fourth non-finite element, a compound built on the past participle *patiṭṭhita*, agreeing with *bhagavā* in the nominative singular. Item 27 asks what it contributes.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Recall.**

7. **Indeclinable.** An absolutive takes no case, gender or number ending at all, so it cannot agree with any noun. It does not describe a person or thing as an adjective or participle does; it modifies the *action* of the main verb adverbially: "having done X" attaches to the whole clause, not to any one noun in it.

8. **Default:** the absolutive's understood agent is the same as the main verb's subject (nominative if the main verb is active, instrumental or genitive if it is passive). **The documented exception:** a dative or genitive noun can govern the absolutive on its own, naming an agent distinct from the main clause's grammatical subject — *paññāya cassa disvā āsavā parikkhīṇā honti* (MN 25 — M i 160), where *cassa* ('and for him') governs *disvā* while *āsavā* is the separate nominative subject of *honti*. Item 28 sets the two cases side by side.

9. ***-tvā*** (with the variants *-tvāna* and *-ttā*) and ***-(i)ya***. The sound change: a root ending in a short vowel inserts a *-t-* before the suffix, and *-tya-* then assimilates to *-cca-* (*paṭi* + *i* + *tya* → *paṭicca*). Oberlies counts eight suffixes in all; the rest are in Appendix A.4.

10. The **gerund** is this chapter's absolutive: a completed action, indeclinable, standing before the main verb. The **gerundive** is Chapter 10's future passive participle: an obligation still to be discharged, declined like an ordinary adjective. They share nothing but a look-alike name.

11. Because the cases, chiefly the dative, already cover much of the ground English covers with "to." Where English must reach for an infinitive ("in order to give"), Pāli often has a dative noun in *-āya* in the same role; Chapter 15 gives that overlap its own treatment.

**Identify the formation.**

12. *gantvā* — **absolutive**, *-tvā* type, of *gacchati* 'goes'. 'Having gone.'

13. *paccakkhāya* — **absolutive**, *-(i)ya* type (assimilated), of *paccakkhāti* (*paṭi* + *ā* + √*khā*). 'Having renounced, given up.' Item 5 has it doing the actual renouncing.

14. ***jahetvā***. It does not occur in the root texts and appears once in the commentaries, in a gloss in the Mahāniddesa commentary. Nor can it be hiding behind a sandhi, since it does not begin with a vowel. *hitvā* and *jahitvā* are both solidly canonical.

15. *desetuṁ* — **infinitive** of *deseti* 'teaches'. 'To teach.'

16. *cassa* — sandhi of *ca* + *assa*, 'and for him / and his'. The third-person pronoun's genitive and dative singular are identical (Appendix A.2), so the case cannot be settled from the form, and the sentence does not settle it either. For the construction it makes no difference: whichever it is, it is neither the nominative nor *āsavā*. The whole different-agent reading depends on this word, the tradition's *asamānakattuka*, 'having a different agent'.

17. *amhehi* — instrumental plural of *ahaṁ*, 'by us'. Marks the infinitive's agent in an impersonal *sukara*-type construction, even though *pariccajituṁ* itself reads actively ('to give up', not 'to be given up'). Item 4 is the sentence.

18. *nissāya* — absolutive of *nissayati*, here functioning as a **postposition**, 'depending on, close to', governing the accusative. Item 1 is its prefixed twin in a sentence.

19. *sakkā* — an old optative form, now an **indeclinable** meaning 'possible, one might be able'. Itself neither an absolutive nor an infinitive, it is the impersonal predicate an infinitive most naturally completes (*sakkā … kātuṁ*, 'it is possible to do'). Item 5 opens with it.

**Build the form.**

20. *bhāvetvā* — confirmed at item 6, part of the standard formula for developing the awakening factors.

21. *bhāvetuṁ* — already in the chapter's word list.

22. *pahāya* — *pa* + √*hā* + *ya*, the root taking the suffix directly since it ends in long *ā* (the same rule that gives *vihāya* 'having withdrawn'); the present stem's reduplication (*pajaha-*) belongs only to the finite conjugation and does not carry over, as Chapter 10 already showed for the gerundive *pahātabbaṁ*.

23. *sotuṁ* — √*su* + *tuṁ*, strengthened root, parallel to *netuṁ* from √*nī*.

24. *datvā* — already in the chapter's word list, and set 10's item 27 has it four times in one passage.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Put it back into Pāli.**

25. **Na sukaraṁ paresaṁ dhammaṁ desetuṁ.** Following the word order and vocabulary of AN 5.159 — A iii 184, minus the vocative. Item 4 is the same impersonal frame with its agent expressed.

26. **So taṁ dhammaṁ sutvā tathāgate saddhaṁ paṭilabhati.** DN 2 — D i 62, word for word, and DPD's citation for *paṭilabhati* independently quotes this sentence from a different sutta (the *Attantapasutta*), a second sign that it is fixed, formulaic phrasing and not a one-off composition.

**Read at length.**

27. "Whatever arahants, fully awakened ones, there have been, venerable sir, in the past — all those Blessed Ones, having abandoned the five hindrances, the defilements of the mind that weaken wisdom, with minds well established in the four applications of mindfulness, having truly developed the seven awakening factors, fully awakened to unsurpassed full awakening.

    And whatever arahants, fully awakened ones, there will be, venerable sir, in the future — all those Blessed Ones … will fully awaken to unsurpassed full awakening.

    And the Blessed One too, venerable sir, who is at present the arahant, the fully awakened one — … has fully awakened to unsurpassed full awakening."

    - The three finite predicates are *abhisambujjhiṁsu* (**aorist**, 'awakened': past buddhas), *abhisambujjhissanti* (**future**, 'will awaken': buddhas yet to come), and *abhisambuddho* (**past participle** standing as the predicate, with the copula understood: 'has awakened', read against *etarahi* 'now' as the present case). Three tenses, one sentence template.
    - The absolutives themselves carry no tense at all. *Pahāya* and *bhāvetvā* mean only that the abandoning and the developing are complete before whatever finite verb follows, so the same wording can serve a past-tense sentence, a future-tense sentence and a present-tense sentence without changing a single letter. Only the one finite (or participial) predicate at the end has to change; the whole non-finite chain in front of it is tenseless by nature and so is reusable across all three.
    - *Supatiṭṭhitacitto/supatiṭṭhitacittā* is built on ***patiṭṭhita***, the past participle of *patiṭṭhahati* 'stands firm, is established' (√*ṭhā*), compounded with *su-* 'well' and *-citta* 'mind'. The participle contributes the resultant, already-accomplished sense Chapter 10 taught for the intransitive past participle: "a mind that has become established," not "a mind that establishes", read as a standing state, not an ongoing action.
    - Saying it three times turns a claim about one teacher into a claim about the nature of awakening itself: whichever buddha is in view, past, future, or the one Sāriputta is speaking to, the same five hindrances are abandoned and the same seven factors developed. The repetition makes the argument. Sāriputta's declaration of faith (traditionally called his "lion's roar") rests on a pattern that holds regardless of which buddha fills in the blank, and the sentence's own grammar, one template running through three tenses, embodies that claim before a word of doctrine is drawn from it.

**Whose action is it.**

28. In item 3, *caritvā* is the monks', and you know because they are the only candidate. *Te bhikkhū* is the nominative subject of both finite verbs, no other word in the sentence is nominative, and monks are the sort of thing that can wander for alms. The default holds, and it holds so comfortably that the question does not arise on a first reading.

    In MN 25 — M i 160, *disvā* is the monk's, and the nominative subject of the sentence is not the monk. *Āsavā parikkhīṇā honti*, "the defilements come to an end": *āsavā* is nominative plural and is what *honti* agrees with. So the default reading would make the defilements see with wisdom, which is impossible. The test is plausibility, not word order or a rule about position. Defilements do not see. When a main clause's subject is an abstract noun and the sentence describes a consequence, not a sequence, the absolutive's agent is somebody else.

    The word that supplies him is *cassa*, *ca* + *assa*, 'and for him'. Its case cannot be settled from its form, because the third-person pronoun's genitive and dative singular are identical, and Appendix A.2 gives *assa* for both; the sentence does not disambiguate them either. For the construction it makes no difference. It is **not** nominative and **not** *āsavā*, and the tradition has a name for the pattern: *asamānakattuka*, 'having a different agent'.

    So the rule has two halves, and item 3 and this sentence show them. Ordinarily the agent is the main verb's subject, and to assume so is right nearly every time. Where the subject could not plausibly have done it, look for a genitive or dative, and read the absolutive as giving a cause, not a preceding event.

**Reaching back.**

29. The absolutive *labhitvā* ('having gained') serves as the protasis, read as "if they were to gain" and not "having gained", since the sentence as a whole is hypothetical. The optative *parihareyyuṁ* ('they would carry') carries the apodosis, matching Chapter 8's protasis/apodosis division for a conditional sentence. Here, though, the "if" half is an absolutive and not a verb in the conditional mood, and that is what this chapter adds to Chapter 8.

30. Neither *vatvāna* nor any absolutive carries a voice distinction; *vutta* does. *Vatvāna* is indeclinable. It has no gender, case or number slot to carry agreement in, and so nothing for a voice reading to attach to either; it says only that the saying is complete, and stays the same whether the sentence around it is active or passive. *Vutta*, by contrast, is a verbal *adjective*. Chapter 10's whole voice rule (a transitive verb's participle is passive) applies to it because it agrees with a noun and therefore has to say something about that noun's relationship to the action. The absolutive's freedom from voice follows directly from its freedom from agreement: a form with nothing to agree with has nothing to be active or passive *about*. Item 3 has both kinds standing in one sentence: three absolutives and one agreeing participle, *piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā*.

**Two subordinations in one sentence.**

31. *tena kho pana samayena* now at that time · *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *saṅghassa* by the Sangha (m. gen. sg.) · *cīvare* robe-cloth (nt. loc. sg.) · *bhājīyamāne* being distributed (nt. loc. sg., pres. part. of the passive *bhājīyati*) · *theyyacitto* with theft in mind (m. nom. sg.) · *kusaṁ* the lot (m. acc. sg.) · *saṅkāmetvā* having shifted (absol. of the causative *saṅkāmeti*) · *cīvaraṁ* robe-cloth (nt. acc. sg.) · *aggahesi* took (3 sg. aor.)

    "Now at that time a certain monk, while robe-cloth was being distributed by the Sangha, shifted the lot with theft in mind and took a robe."

    The two constructions are a locative absolute and an absolutive, and the sentence uses each where the rule requires it. *Saṅghassa cīvare bhājīyamāne* is the locative absolute: its subject is *cīvare*, the cloth, and its agent is the Sangha in the genitive, neither of them the subject of the main verb. *Saṅkāmetvā* is the absolutive, and its doer is the monk, who is also the doer of *aggahesi*.

    That is Bodhi's rule, quoted in Chapter 16, applied in both directions in one sentence: where the prior action belongs to the subject of the main verb, the absolutive; where it belongs to someone else, the locative absolute with a participle. Here the distribution is the Sangha's business and the shifting is the monk's, and the grammar keeps the two apart without a pronoun anywhere. It is the same question item 28 asks, answered by the choice of construction, not by inference.

    *Saṅkāmetvā* is indeed a causative, of *saṅkamati* 'goes together, moves across', literally 'having caused to go across'. What he moved was a *kusa*, which DPD gives in this sense as a lot or ticket: the slips drawn to decide who receives which piece of cloth. He shifted his own and took a better robe, so the case is filed under theft and not under greed.
