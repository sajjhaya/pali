## Answers: Script and Pronunciation

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa. (MN 91 — M ii 140)

    na·**mo** **tas**·sa bha·ga·va·**to** a·ra·ha·**to** **sam·mā·sam·bud·dhas**·sa

    *Mo*, *to* and *to* are heavy because *o* is long and their syllables are open. *Tas*, *sam*, *sam*, *bud* and *dhas* are heavy because each is closed by a consonant. *Mā* is heavy because the vowel is long. Every light syllable in the line is a short vowel standing open: *na*, *sa*, *bha*, *ga*, *va*, *a*, *ra*, *ha*, *sa*.

    The longest run of lights is *bha·ga·va*, three of them, and then *a·ra·ha*, three more; the longest run of heavies is the last word, five in a row. Three unhurried words of mostly light syllables, and then the homage lands on its object and stays there. It is built that way.

2. Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

    su·**ṇā**·tu **me** **bhan**·**te** **saṅ**·**gho**

    The two light syllables are *su* and *tu*, and everything else in the line is heavy. Six heavy syllables out of eight is unusually dense for prose, which is part of why the formula sounds the way it does.

    The *e* of *bhante* is heavy because *e* is long by nature and the syllable is open: the reasoning of item 11, met before it is asked for. And *saṅgho* is written with *ṅ* because a group nasal attaches to members of its own group, so a nasal standing before *g* or *gh* can only be the guttural nasal. The line carries the other half of that rule as well: *bhante* has a plain *n*, because *t* is a dental. One rule, two spellings, and nobody chose either.

    This is the opening of every formal act of the Sangha (every ordination, every recitation of the *pātimokkha*, every settlement of a dispute), and a monastic will say or hear it more often than any other sentence in the canon.

3. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

    **sab**·ba·**pā**·**pas**·sa a·ka·ra·**ṇaṁ**, ku·sa·**las**·sa u·pa·**sam**·pa·**dā**

    Seven heavy syllables, and five of them are heavy for reasons the eye catches at once: *pā* and *dā* have long vowels, and *sab*, *pas* and *las* are each closed by the first of a doubled consonant.

    The question is after ***ṇaṁ*** and ***sam***. *Ṇaṁ* is closed by the niggahīta, which is neither a doubled consonant nor a long vowel but closes a syllable as either would. *Sam*, in *upasampadā*, is closed by the *m* of the cluster *mp*: a single consonant, not a doubled one, and it still closes the syllable before it because the break falls between two adjacent consonants. A syllable is heavy if it ends in anything other than a short vowel, and the four ways of ending otherwise are all present in this one line.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

4. Itipi so bhagavā arahaṁ … buddho bhagavā. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

    The chapter prints this line divided and weighed, and comparing your recitation against it is the check. What the writing tests is something else.

    Of the errors a first attempt produces, two kinds are audible and one is not. A missing macron (*bhagava* for *bhagavā*, *sattha* for *satthā*) is audible, and so is a missing doubling: *anutaro* for *anuttaro*, *devamanusānaṁ* for *devamanussānaṁ*. The second of those is the more instructive, because the mistake does not produce a non-word: *sattha* is 'a knife' and 'a caravan' and 'a science', and only the macron makes it the Teacher. If you were reciting properly, your ear had the information and your hand lost it. The failure is the hand's.

    The retroflex of *vijjācaraṇasampanno* is the other case. Most reciters do not distinguish *ṇ* from *n* audibly, and anyone never taught to make the retroflex sounds cannot hear the difference to write it down; that one has to be learned as spelling. Do not conclude from this that the distinction is unimportant. It separates real words, and the chapter's variant box is about what happens when editors disagree over these letters.

5. Ayaṁ itthannāmo itthannāmassa āyasmato upasampadāpekkho. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

    Six doubled consonants and four long vowels, in a line of five words. The doubled ones are *tth* and *nn* in *itthannāmo*, *tth*, *nn* and *ss* in *itthannāmassa*, and *kkh* in *upasampadāpekkho*; the long vowels are the *ā* of each *-nāma-*, the *ā* of *āyasmato*, and the *ā* of *-padā-*.

    Three of the six doublings are aspirates doubled the Pāli way (*tth* is *t* + *th* and *kkh* is *k* + *kh*), item 6's rule twice over. And the line shows well why writing is a different skill from reading: nothing in it is hard to say, and almost every word of it is easy to write down wrongly.

    *Itthannāma* is 'so-and-so', the name-shaped hole in a formula, and it is itself a junction: *itthaṁ* + *nāma*, with the niggahīta assimilating to the *n* that follows it. *Upasampadāpekkho* is *upasampadā* + *apekkha*, where the long *ā* and the short *a* meet and come out as one long *ā*. Both are sandhi, and both are the next chapter's business; they are pointed out here only because they make the line hard to spell.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

6. *dukkha* — **five letters**: *d*, *u*, *k*, *kh*, *a*. Six characters are printed, because *kh* is a digraph and takes two of them. The doubled sound here is *k* followed by *kh*, not *kh* twice, the ordinary way Pāli doubles an aspirate: the first member loses its aspiration. So the spelling is *dukkha* and never *dukhkha*.

7. *amhi* — **four letters**: *a*, *m*, *h*, *i*. Four characters too, and hold this pair beside 6. *Mh* is **not** a digraph, because *m* is not one of the sounds that takes an aspirate partner; it is an *m* followed by an *h*. The chapter's test is exact: every combination with *h* that appears in the alphabet list is a digraph, and every other combination with *h* is two letters. Item 18 is the same test on a different nasal.

8. *saṅgha* — **five letters**: *s*, *a*, *ṅ*, *gh*, *a*. Six characters, *gh* being the digraph. Some works, the PTS dictionary among them, write this word *sangha* with a plain *n*, because *ṅ* occurs only before *k*, *kh*, *g* and *gh* and can therefore be inferred. Nothing is lost either way; this book writes *ṅ*.

9. *daḷha* — four letters, or five, and the disagreement is real. *D*, *a*, *ḷh*, *a* if *ḷh* is one letter; *d*, *a*, *ḷ*, *h*, *a* if it is two. It is written with two characters even in the Asian scripts, and it stands for a single sound. Perniola counts it among his liquids and so gets forty-two letters in the alphabet; the PTS dictionary's list and Kaccāyana's both omit it and get forty-one. Either answer is defensible provided you can say which count you are using. Note that the syllable division does not wait on the answer: *daḷha* divides *da·ḷha* on either view, because *ḷh* is one *sound* whatever it is called.

10. *sammāsambuddho* — **thirteen letters**: *s*, *a*, *m*, *m*, *ā*, *s*, *a*, *m*, *b*, *u*, *d*, *dh*, *o*. Fourteen characters, the digraph being *dh*. The two doubled consonants behave differently on the page and identically in principle: *mm* is a plain geminate, and *ddh* is *d* + *dh*, an aspirate doubled by an unaspirated copy of itself, as in 6.

11. *bhante* — **long**. The *e* is at the end of the word, so its syllable is open and unclosed. *E* and *o* are long by nature and stay long unless something takes the length away.

12. *mettā* — **short**. Divide it *met·tā*: the *e* is closed by the first of the two *t*s, and a closed syllable holding a long vowel would come to three *mattā*, which Pāli does not allow. The vowel gives up its length so the syllable can keep its consonant. This is the two-*mattā* ceiling in its ordinary operation, and *bhante* beside *mettā* is the cleanest minimal pair for it.

13. *soṇḍo* — the first *o* is the disputed case, and the second is not. The final *o* is word-final, so it is long by the same reasoning as 11. The first *o* is followed by the conjunct *ṇḍ*, which by the general rule of 12 should shorten it. Buddhappiya's exception says otherwise: *e* and *o* shorten before *geminate* consonants but not before conjuncts formed with a group nasal, so *soṇḍo* 'drunkard' and *meṇḍo* 'ram' keep a long first vowel where *bhāseyya* does not. Kaccāyana holds that *e* and *o* are simply always long; Moggallāna holds that a short *e* must be a different letter from a long one, and counts forty-three letters in consequence. Say which authority you follow and the answer is settled; assert one flatly and you are taking a side without knowing it.

14. *arahaṁ* — **short**, and the question is a trap worth falling into. *A* is a short vowel and there is no rule that lengthens it here. What is easy to slip into is confusing the vowel with the syllable: the syllable *-haṁ* at the end of the word is **heavy**, because the niggahīta closes it, but the vowel inside it is still short. Vowel length and syllable weight are two different measurements, and almost every error in reading a verse line comes from running them together. Item 17 is the same distinction met where it costs something.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

15. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    ma·**no**·**pub**·**baṅ**·ga·**mā** **dham**·**mā**, ma·**no**·**seṭ**·**ṭhā** ma·**no**·ma·**yā**

    Three points here. *No* is heavy three times over, and each time for the same reason as *bhante*'s *e*: the syllable is open and *o* is long by nature. *Baṅ* is heavy because the niggahīta's own consonant, *ṅ*, closes it, with the weight a written niggahīta would carry. And *pub* is heavy because the *b* of *pubbaṅgama* is doubled, so the first of the two closes the preceding syllable.

    This is also the line where nothing joins: *mano* keeps its *-o* unchanged in front of all three following consonants. The next chapter has a name for that, and sets this same line again to make the point.

16. …poriyā vācāya samannāgato vissaṭṭhāya anelagalāya atthassa viññāpaniyā… (DN 4 — D i 114)

    **po**·ri·**yā** **vā**·**cā**·ya sa·**man**·**nā**·ga·**to** **vis**·**saṭ**·**ṭhā**·ya a·**ne**·la·ga·**lā**·ya **at**·**thas**·sa **viñ**·**ñā**·pa·ni·**yā**

    Check yourself on *ne* in *anelagalāya*. Only a single *l* follows it, so the syllable is open, and an open syllable with *e* in it is heavy. Take *e* as short by default and you will make this light and find the line does not scan.

    That is the same trap as *de-* in *devamanussānaṁ*, and it is the commonest weight error there is. The rule to hold: *e* and *o* are long unless something closes their syllable.

17. Yassa nakkhamati, so bhāseyya. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

    **yas**·sa **nak**·kha·ma·ti, **so** **bhā**·**sey**·ya

    "Whoever does not consent should speak."

    *Nakkhamati* is *na* + *khamati*, 'it is not acceptable'. The negative particle joins, and the aspirate doubles the only way a Pāli aspirate can, by putting an unaspirated copy of itself in front. So *na* + *kh* gives *kkh* and not *khkh*: item 6's rule in a live junction, not a dictionary word.

    *Bhāseyya* is the harder half. The chapter's rule, in item 13, is that *e* shortens before a geminate, and *yy* is a geminate, so the *e* here is short. The weight does not change. The syllable *sey* is closed by the first *y* whatever the vowel inside it, so it is heavy either way, and a scansion of this line cannot show you the shortening at all. Item 14 made that point in the abstract: length and weight are two measurements, and here the first one moves while the second stands still. The shortening is real, it is audible, and it is metrically invisible.

    Every monastic in the tradition has heard this sentence at their own ordination, and it is the moment at which a formal act can be stopped.

18. Yassāyasmato khamati … so tuṇhassa; (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

    tuṇ·has·sa: **eight letters**: *t*, *u*, *ṇ*, *h*, *a*, *s*, *s*, *a*.

    *Ṇh* is two letters and not a digraph, by the same test that settled *amhi* in item 7: the aspirated nasals do not stand in the alphabet, so any nasal followed by *h* is a nasal followed by an *h*. Nothing about the retroflex changes that.

    The division is the interesting part. By the rule as the chapter states it, a break falls between two adjacent consonants, which gives *tuṇ·has·sa*: heavy, heavy, light. But the chapter's box lists *nh* among the clusters that may be divided either way, and if *ṇh* goes with its dental counterpart there, then *tu·ṇhas·sa* is equally allowed and the first syllable is **light**. The chapter does not say whether *ṇh* belongs on that list, and no source read for it says either.

    The consequence is safe. This is prose, and prose does not force the choice; in a verse line the metre would force it, and the division exists for the metre. To want a rule where there is none is to misunderstand what the rules are for.

    The word itself is a junction too: *tuṇhī* 'silent' + *assa* 'would be', 'let him be silent'. The silence is consent, and so item 17 is the alternative to it.

**Put the diacritics back.**

19. Three words, and only one of them cannot be decided.

    (a) *sammāsambuddhassa*: the long *ā* is **audible**, and if you have said the homage aloud at chanting pace you have the information already. Nothing here needs a book.

    (b) *saṅgho*: recoverable **by rule**, without hearing anything at all. A nasal before *k*, *kh*, *g* or *gh* is always *ṅ*, so the dot cannot be anywhere else, and so the PTS dictionary is content to print *sangha* and lose nothing, as item 8 says.

    (c) *anelagalāya*: not recoverable, and this is the undecidable one. The final *-lāya* has an audible long *ā* like (a), but the two *l*s are another matter: the word has two of them, each may be *l* or *ḷ*, and all four resulting spellings are printed in real editions. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *anelagalāya* in its running text and records *aneḷagalāya* for the Sinhalese and PTS editions in a note at that line, with *anelagaḷāya* in a third. So the question "which is correct" has no answer in the abstract; it has an answer for the edition in front of you, and a different one for the edition next to it.

    That is what separates this item from the other two. Vowel length is a fact about the language and you can hear it. The *l*/*ḷ* of this word is a fact about a manuscript tradition, and no amount of careful listening will supply it. Knowing which of the two kinds of question you are facing is most of what this chapter is for.
