## Answers: Numerals

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā rājagahe viharati veḷuvane kalandakanivāpe. (SN 10.9 — S i 212)

    This is the agreement construction, and it is the plainest instance of it in the language. *Eka* is a numeral from one to eighteen, so it may behave as an ordinary adjective; *ekaṁ* and *samayaṁ* are both masculine accusative singular, and they agree in case, number and gender.

    Whether it counts anything is another question, and here it barely does. English says "at one time" and means "once, on an occasion", and Pāli does the same. A numeral in the agreement construction is an adjective, and an adjective meaning 'one' shades into an article without any change of form. The chapter's four constructions are about syntax, not arithmetic, and this item makes the difference visible: nothing in the sentence shows *ekaṁ* used numerically, because it is not.

    That is also why it is here. Almost every sutta a reader opens begins with this phrase, so the first numeral construction anyone meets is met several hundred times before it is ever named.

2. Tena kho pana samayena aññatitthiyā paribbājakā cātuddase pannarase aṭṭhamiyā ca pakkhassa sannipatitvā dhammaṁ bhāsanti. (Kd 2 — Vin i 101)

    *Aṭṭhamiyā* is the odd one out. It is a feminine *ī*-stem in an oblique case, the same *-iyā* ending item 10 meets on *vīsatiyā*, while *cātuddase* and *pannarase* are locatives of a stem that is not feminine. DPD carries *cātuddasa* as a masculine (with an adjective and a neuter beside it) and *pannarasa* as a masculine, and registers *pannarase* separately as an adverb, "on the fifteenth day of the lunar fortnight", locative singular.

    So three consecutive days of one fortnight are named by two different declensions in a single sentence, and no rule lies behind it: these are three words with three histories that happen to have converged on one use. Item 19 sets this passage beside the one the chapter quotes and asks what the pair together settle.

    The sentence matters beyond its grammar. The wanderers of other sects were already gathering on these three days and drawing crowds; the Buddha's fortnightly observance was instituted in answer to it, and so the *uposatha* enters the canon here, in a sentence about someone else's calendar.

3. Abhivādanasīlissa, niccaṁ vuḍḍhāpacāyino; Cattāro dhammā vaḍḍhanti, āyu vaṇṇo sukhaṁ balaṁ. (Dhp 109 — Dhp p. 16)

    The four are *āyu*, *vaṇṇo*, *sukhaṁ*, *balaṁ*: long life, beauty, happiness, strength. Notice that they are not all of one gender and that this does not matter at all: the numeral agrees with *dhammā*, the word for "things", and the four items are then simply listed after it in apposition.

    ***Cattāro dhammā* is agreement**, masculine nominative plural on both words. Compare item 1, which is the same construction in the singular and in a phrase nobody thinks of as counting; and compare item 4, where the numeral is high and the construction collapses. Between the two of them they mark the boundary the chapter draws at eighteen.

    The two genitives in the first two lines agree with each other and with no other word in the verse: *abhivādanasīlissa* and *vuḍḍhāpacāyino*, both masculine genitive singular, naming the person for whom the four things increase. DPD gives Dhp 109 itself as its illustration for both words.

4. Tasmiṁ sahassadhā loke sahassaṁ candānaṁ sahassaṁ sūriyānaṁ … cattāri mahāsamuddasahassāni … sahassaṁ brahmalokānaṁ. (AN 10.29 — A v 59)

    The construction changes at *cattāri mahāsamuddasahassāni*, and it had to. Everywhere else in the passage *sahassaṁ* is a neuter noun in the nominative singular with what it counts standing beside it in the genitive plural ("a thousand of moons", "a thousand of suns"), and nothing agrees with anything. But *four* thousand cannot be said that way. *Sahassa* itself now has to be counted, so it goes into the plural, *sahassāni*; *cattāri* agrees with it, neuter nominative plural; and the thing counted, having lost its place, moves inside the word as the first member of a compound, *mahāsamudda-sahassāni*.

    Three of the four constructions in one sentence, then: the genitive fourteen times, then a compound and an agreement working together to say a number the genitive cannot reach. It shows well that the four are solutions to different problems, not four styles a writer may choose between.

    *Sahassadhā* at the front is a fifth thing and belongs to none of the four: it is a distributive adverb in *-dhā*, indeclinable, "in a thousand ways, thousandfold". It describes the world and counts nothing in it.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Niṭṭhitacīvarasmiṁ bhikkhunā ubbhatasmiṁ kathine dasāhaparamaṁ atirekacīvaraṁ dhāretabbaṁ. Taṁ atikkāmayato nissaggiyaṁ pācittiyan'ti. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196)

    *Dasāhaparamaṁ* has three parts and is in the accusative singular. DPD resolves it as *dasa* + *aha* + *parama* + *aṁ*: ten, day, at-most, and then the ending. So the word is a compound of a numeral with a noun and an adjective, declined as one word: the compound construction, as in item 13's *bhikkhusahassena*, but with an extra member on the end.

    The interesting half is why an accusative is translated "for ten days at most". It is the object of nothing: *dhāretabbaṁ* already has its object in *atirekacīvaraṁ*. This is the accusative used adverbially, the device the chapter on adjectives set out (*dīghaṁ* 'at length', *niccaṁ* 'constantly', *sādhukaṁ* 'well'), and DPD registers *dasāhaparamaṁ* as an indeclinable adverb for that reason. The case is frozen and no longer functions as a case. Look for what the accusative is the object of and you will not find it, and the chapter on indeclinables explains why.

    Two other words deserve a moment. *Ubbhatasmiṁ kathine* is a locative absolute, "the kathina having been withdrawn", and *atikkāmayato* is a present participle in the genitive, "for one exceeding [it]", which is the genitive of the person to whom a rule applies. Neither is a numeral, and both make this rule harder to rebuild than its length suggests.

6. Māse māse sahassena, yo yajetha sataṁ samaṁ; Ekañca bhāvitattānaṁ, muhuttamapi pūjaye; Sāyeva pūjanā seyyo, yañce vassasataṁ hutaṁ. (Dhp 106 — Dhp p. 16)

    Six numeral expressions, and the six are:

    - ***māse māse***: the **distributive**, formed by saying the word twice. "Month by month, every month."
    - ***sahassena***: a higher numeral as a noun, in the instrumental: the means by which the sacrifice is made.
    - ***sataṁ samaṁ***: the higher numeral again, and an accusative of duration. *Samaṁ* is a **singular** feminine accusative, *samā* 'year': a hundred years, with the noun in the singular, because from nineteen upwards what is counted takes no plural.
    - ***ekaṁ***: the low numeral as an ordinary adjective, agreeing with *bhāvitattānaṁ*, masculine accusative singular. The agreement construction, and the only one in the verse.
    - ***muhuttaṁ***: not a numeral at all, included because the whole comparison rests on it: the shortest span the language has a word for, set against a hundred years. DPD registers it as it registers *dasāhaparamaṁ* in item 5, an indeclinable adverb, accusative singular of *muhutta*, so the two words the verse and the Vinaya rule are each built around are the same construction, one measuring ten days and the other a moment.
    - ***vassasataṁ***: a **compound**, *vassa* + *sata*, declined as one neuter word. The same hundred years as in line 2, said the other way, in the same verse.

    The item's real lesson is that the same hundred years is said twice by two different constructions, forty words apart. *Sataṁ samaṁ* and *vassasataṁ* differ in neither quantity nor register: two of the four constructions with an identical function, chosen by what the metre and the line needed. Taught that each construction means something distinct, you will look for a difference here, and there is none to find.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Identify the form.**

7. *tiṇṇannaṁ* — the cardinal *ti* "three". Genitive/dative plural, masculine and neuter. It is asked about here because it carries the ending twice over: the regular form is *tiṇṇaṁ*, and *tiṇṇannaṁ* has had the *-annaṁ* of the *pañca* class added on top of a genitive that was already complete. It is an analogy, not an inheritance: the numeral has been reanalysed as though it belonged with *pañcannaṁ*, *channaṁ*, *sattannaṁ*, one class up.

8. *ubhinnaṁ* — *ubha* "both". Genitive/dative, and the number is where the interest lies: it is neither singular nor plural but the last surviving **dual** in the language. All genders share the one form. A reader who files it as an irregular plural of "two" will have the right translation and the wrong grammar, and will then be puzzled by *ubho*, whose *-o* is a dual ending and not a masculine nominative singular.

9. *catutthī* — the ordinal *catuttha* "fourth". Feminine nominative singular. This is the ordinary feminine of an ordinal from the fourth upwards; the first three are in *-ā* instead, *paṭhamā*, *dutiyā*, *tatiyā*. Three of the *-ī* forms (*aṭṭhamī*, *cātuddasī*, *pañcadasī*) have also hardened into names for days of the lunar fortnight, but that is a lexical fact about those three words and not a rule about the class, and *catutthī* has no such second life. Items 2 and 19 show those three words in both uses.

10. *vīsatiyā* — the cardinal *vīsati* "twenty". Instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative or locative singular: a feminine *i*-stem in *-ti* declines like *jāti*, and the *-iyā* ending is as ambiguous here as it is there. The form alone shows two things. It is **singular**, because from nineteen upwards a numeral is a noun, not an adjective; and so it can tell you nothing about the gender or number of what it counts, which has to be found elsewhere in the sentence.

11. *tikkhattuṁ* — a multiplicative adverb built on *ti* "three", "three times". It has no case and no number, because it is not declined at all: *-kkhattuṁ* added to a numeral stem yields an indeclinable. The accusative *-ṁ* is historical and frozen — the same freezing item 5 found in *dasāhaparamaṁ*. Compare *dvikkhattuṁ* and *dasakkhattuṁ*, formed the same way, and *ekadhā*, *dvidhā*, *sattadhā*, formed differently but equally indeclinable.

Say how the numeral joins its noun.

12. **sahassaṁ** candānaṁ (AN 10.29 — A v 59): the **genitive** construction. *Sahassaṁ* is a neuter noun in the nominative singular and *candānaṁ* is a masculine genitive plural, so the two do not agree in case, number or gender, and cannot be read as a unit. Literally "a thousand of moons". This construction catches anyone told that numerals agree with their nouns: nothing here agrees with anything.

13. saddhiṁ **bhikkhusahassena** (SN 35.28 — S iv 19): the **compound**. The numeral *sahassa* is the last member of a determinative compound whose first member is what is counted, and the whole is then declined as one word, instrumental singular, governed by *saddhiṁ* "together with". The gender is *sahassa*'s, neuter, not *bhikkhu*'s. The canon reaches for it most often when a round number qualifies a noun.

14. **Cattāro** dhammā vaḍḍhanti (Dhp 109 — Dhp p. 16): **agreement**, the construction most readers assume is the only one. *Cattāro* is masculine nominative plural and so is *dhammā*; the numeral behaves as an ordinary adjective, as every numeral from one to eighteen may. Note that the test is shared case, number and gender, not the look of the two words, and 12 fails it.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Read the verse.**

15. Yo sahassaṁ sahassena, saṅgāme mānuse jine; Ekañca jeyyamattānaṁ, sa ve saṅgāmajuttamo. (Dhp 103 — Dhp p. 15)

   *yo* whoever (m. nom. sg.) · *sahassaṁ* a thousand (n. acc. sg.) · *sahassena* by a thousand (n. inst. sg.) · *saṅgāme* in battle (m. loc. sg.) · *mānuse* men (m. acc. pl.) · *jine* should conquer (opt.) · *ekaṁ* one (m. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *jeyya* should conquer (opt.) · *attānaṁ* himself (m. acc. sg.) · *sa* he · *ve* indeed · *saṅgāmaji·uttamo* supreme victor in battle (m. nom. sg.)

   "Though one should conquer a thousand times a thousand men in battle, and another conquer one — himself — it is he who is the supreme victor."

   Three numerals, three different functions. *Sahassaṁ* is the higher numeral as a noun, accusative, with *mānuse* standing in apposition to it, not agreeing. *Sahassena* is an instrumental of multiplication, "by a thousand", giving a million: Pāli has no word for a million here, but builds one out of the syntax. Then *ekaṁ*, the low numeral, behaves as an ordinary adjective agreeing with *attānaṁ*. The verse sets the largest number the language can conveniently say against the smallest, and the grammar tracks the argument: the big number needs apparatus, the small one simply agrees.

16. Māse māse sahassena, yo yajetha sataṁ samaṁ; (Dhp 106 — Dhp p. 16)

    *māse māse* month by month (m. loc. sg., repeated) · *sahassena* with a thousand (n. inst. sg.) · *yo* whoever (m. nom. sg.) · *yajetha* should sacrifice (opt. mid.) · *sataṁ* a hundred (n. acc. sg.) · *samaṁ* year (f. acc. sg.)

    "Though month after month, for a hundred years, one should offer sacrifice with a thousand —"

    The repetition *māse māse* is the **distributive**, and it is the plainest form the distributive takes: say the word twice and it means "each" or "every". The chapter met the same device with *aṭṭha aṭṭha* "eight each". Then *sataṁ samaṁ* is the higher numeral again, and it repays a second look — *sataṁ* is the neuter accusative of duration and *samaṁ* is a **singular** feminine accusative, *samā* "year". A hundred years, with the noun in the singular. DPD cites this very line under *samā*, which is a useful reminder that the dictionary is often the fastest way to settle a form that the paradigms leave ambiguous.

    Item 6 has the whole verse, and the pair to hold beside *sataṁ samaṁ* is *vassasataṁ* in its last line: the same hundred years, said as a compound instead.

17. Sabbampi taṁ na catubhāgameti, Abhivādanā ujjugatesu seyyo. (Dhp 108 — Dhp p. 16)

    *sabbaṁ* all (n. nom. sg.) · *pi* even · *taṁ* that (n. nom. sg.) · *na* not · *catubhāgaṁ* a quarter (m. acc. sg.) · *eti* comes to · *abhivādanā* salutation (f. nom. sg.) · *ujjugatesu* to the upright (m. loc. pl.) · *seyyo* better (n. nom. sg.)

    "All that does not come to a quarter of it; better is salutation offered to the upright."

    *Catubhāgaṁ* is a **fraction**, and it is built as the chapter said fractions usually are: a numeral plus a word for "part", here *bhāga*. It is a compound and declines as one word, masculine accusative singular, governed by *eti*. Resolve the sandhi carefully: *catubhāgameti* is *catubhāgaṁ* + *eti*, and if you take the *-m-* as part of the verb you will look for a root that does not exist.

18. Appamādo kho, mahārāja, eko dhammo, yo ubho atthe samadhiggayha tiṭṭhati. (SN 3.17 — S i 86)

    *appamādo* diligence (m. nom. sg.) · *kho* indeed · *mahārāja* great king (m. voc. sg.) · *eko* one (m. nom. sg.) · *dhammo* thing (m. nom. sg.) · *yo* which (m. nom. sg.) · *ubho* both (m. acc.) · *atthe* benefits (m. acc. pl.) · *samadhiggayha* having secured (absol.) · *tiṭṭhati* stands

    "Diligence, great king, is the one thing that secures both benefits."

    This is the chapter's example, set again on purpose. Meeting it in the exposition, where the surrounding prose names the dual before the sentence arrives, is different work from parsing it cold. Two numerals: *eko*, agreeing with *dhammo* in case, number and gender, the ordinary adjectival use; and *ubho*, which agrees with nothing, because it is a dual and *atthe* is a plural. The mismatch is neither an error nor a licence: it is the residue of a lost grammatical number, and *ubho* alone still shows it. The two benefits are named in the next sentence: the one belonging to this visible life and the one belonging to what comes after.

The same three days, twice, in two genders.

19. Cātuddasī pañcadasī aṭṭhamī ca pakkhassa. (MN 4 — M i 20)

    Set beside item 2, the six day-names are:

    | | MN 4 — M i 20 | Kd 2 — Vin i 101 |
    |---|---|---|
    | fourteenth | *cātuddasī* f. nom. sg. | *cātuddase* loc. sg., not feminine |
    | fifteenth | *pañcadasī* f. nom. sg. | *pannarase* loc. sg., not feminine |
    | eighth | *aṭṭhamī* f. nom. sg. | *aṭṭhamiyā* f. loc. sg. |

    The fourteenth and the fifteenth are the two named in two genders. DPD carries *cātuddasī* and *pañcadasī* as feminines and *cātuddasa* and *pannarasa* as masculines, all four glossed as the day of the lunar fortnight, not as the plain ordinal. So the same day has two words for it, in two genders, and the canon uses both.

    The eighth behaves differently because it has only ever been feminine. DPD gives *aṭṭhama* as the ordinal, 'eighth', and *aṭṭhamī* separately as the feminine, twice over, once for the number and once for the day. There is no masculine day-name for the eighth beside it. The other two have one because they are built on the teens, where the underlying cardinal is itself a masculine-shaped compound.

    What the pair settles is that these three are names, not ordinals. An ordinal agrees with what it qualifies and takes its gender from it. These do not: they carry their own gender, they differ from each other in it, and the fifteenth even appears in two different stems: *pañcadasa* in the Sanskrit-like form and *pannarasa* in the Middle Indic one the chapter's note on the teens calls the genuine development. Words that behave like that are lexical items with histories, not cells in a paradigm. The chapter says as much ("a lexical fact about three words rather than a grammatical one about a whole class"), and here the claim has its evidence set out.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

20. (a) ***Ekaṁ samayaṁ*** (SN 10.9 — S i 212): **agreement**; both masculine accusative singular. The easiest phrase in the set to produce and the easiest to produce without noticing it is a numeral construction at all.

    (b) ***Cattāro dhammā vaḍḍhanti*** (Dhp 109 — Dhp p. 16): **agreement**; *cattāro* and *dhammā* masculine nominative plural, *vaḍḍhanti* third person plural present. The trap is *cattāri*, which is the neuter and is what item 4 uses.

    (c) ***sahassaṁ candānaṁ*** (AN 10.29 — A v 59): the **genitive** construction; *sahassaṁ* neuter nominative singular, *candānaṁ* masculine genitive plural. If this came out with the two agreeing, the commonest error about Pāli numerals has been made in the open, better than making it unnoticed in a translation.

    (d) ***dasāhaparamaṁ*** (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196): a **compound**, three members and an ending, frozen in the accusative and functioning as an adverb.

    This stage exposes which construction comes to hand by default, and for nearly every reader it is agreement, because an English speaker expects a numeral to agree and because (a) and (b) are the phrases most often heard. If (c) and (d) were the hard ones, the chapter has been understood and not yet absorbed, and day 30 settles the difference.
