## Answers: Review — Sounds and Script

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. *Suṇātu* ends in the vowel *-u* and *me* begins with the consonant *m*; *me* ends in *-e* and *bhante* begins with *bh*; *bhante* ends in *-e* and *saṅgho* begins with *s*. All three boundaries are a vowel followed by a consonant, and at this junction alone the rules have nothing to say. The vowel rules need a vowel on both sides, and the consonant rules need a consonant on the left, which no Pāli word can supply, because no Pāli word ends in one. Item 9 is that derivation; this line shows it on the page.

    For a rule to fire, the second word of a pair would have to begin with a vowel. Then *-u a-*, *-e a-* and *-e a-* would each be a candidate for contraction or elision, and the line would come out shorter than it does. As it stands nothing is lost anywhere in it, and so every word of a formal act of the Sangha can be heard at speed.

2. The two are ***sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ***, where a short *a* meets a short *a*, and ***kusalassa upasampadā***, where a short *a* meets a short *u*. Nothing has happened at either, and item 10 asks for the name of that.

    The arithmetic comes out differently for the two, and that is why it is done twice. In the first, the regular treatment of two like vowels is contraction to the long: *-passa akaraṇaṁ* would become *-passākaraṇaṁ*, one syllable instead of two, and the long *ā* is worth two *mattā* where the two short vowels were worth one each. Same *mattā*, one syllable fewer; in the second the vowels are unlike, and the regular treatment is elision of the first: *kusalassa upasampadā* would become *kusalassupasampadā*, where *-ssu-* is a single short open syllable. One syllable fewer and one *mattā* fewer: the elision has thrown a measure away.

    Part I cannot say which of the two spellings this verse requires, and the metre chapter settles it. But the canon settles half of it for us. Both contractions are made elsewhere in the canon, and each time the result is a complete eight-syllable line. *Kusalassupasampadā* has 2 root-text occurrences, both of them whole *pādas* in the Apadāna; and *pāpassākaraṇaṁ* has 1 root-text occurrence, a whole *pāda* in the Jātaka verses. Neither is a form anybody invented for this answer: they are what the same words look like when a poet needed eight syllables instead of nine.

    That observation underlies the item: a junction is neither free nor compulsory. Every one costs a syllable, some cost a *mattā* as well, and the canon makes the same junction in one verse and declines it in another. Think of sandhi as a spelling convention and you will keep being surprised by lines that will not come out to the length they should.

3. The verb is ***upasaṅkami***. It stands inside *tenupasaṅkami*, which is *tena* + *upasaṅkami* with the *a* of *tena* elided before the *u*; and it stands free two words later in the absolutive *upasaṅkamitvā*.

    No junction could form at the second because there is no word in front of it. The sentence ends at *tenupasaṅkami* and *upasaṅkamitvā* opens the next one. Sandhi is a fact about what happens inside a breath group, not about which letters are adjacent on the page, and a full stop is the end of a breath group by definition. Keep this in front of you before the reading items, because the commonest reason a hunted-for junction is not there is that a sentence boundary is.

    *Ekamantaṁ* is the other case and item 15 takes it apart. The difference lies in what became of the result, not in the junction, which is the same niggahīta-before-a-vowel as anywhere else. A junction you are expected to undo leaves both its pieces with their own functions; *tena* is still an instrumental and *upasaṅkami* is still the verb. A junction you are expected to look up has stopped meaning what its pieces mean: *ekamantaṁ* is an adverb, "to one side", and neither *ekaṁ* nor *anta* keeps a recognisable function inside it. The dictionaries list the second kind and not the first, and there is no line between them, only a gradient.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

4. The two are ***bhagavā arahaṁ*** and ***lokavidū anuttaro***. At both a long vowel meets a short one across the boundary and **nothing happens**.

    At the other eleven the word for what has happened is ***pakatisandhi***. Every one of them is a vowel or a niggahīta followed by a consonant (*itipi so*, *so bhagavā*, *arahaṁ sammāsambuddho*, and so on down to *buddho bhagavā*), and, as item 1 showed, that combination has no rule to fire.

    So the most recited formula in the Theravāda world contains thirteen junctions and not one letter has changed at any of them. That is the answer to the expectation the sandhi chapter creates. Rules are written about the cases where something happens, so a chapter of them leaves a reader believing that something usually does; and the ratio in running canonical prose is nothing like that. Recite this formula once with the question in mind, because it is the clearest demonstration in the language that the default is for nothing to occur.

5. *Ādimetaṁ* is *ādiṁ* + *etaṁ*. Apart, the pieces divide **ā·diṁ** and **e·taṁ**, and *-diṁ* is heavy, closed by its niggahīta. Joined, the word divides **ā·di·me·taṁ**, and *-di-* is **light**: open, with a short *i* and nothing after it. The niggahīta has not disappeared: it has surfaced as the *m* that now opens the following syllable, and in moving from the end of one syllable to the start of the next it has stopped adding weight. The junction has turned a heavy syllable light.

    The same thing happens in *mukhametaṁ* and in *pamukhametaṁ*, both from a niggahīta meeting the *e-* of *etaṁ*. It does **not** happen in *pātimokkhanti*, and the reason is that this junction has two steps where the others have one. *Iti* loses its own initial *i* first, as it does everywhere; what the niggahīta then meets is the *t* of the remaining *-ti*, a consonant, so it has nowhere to move to and assimilates in place, as the *n* that a following *t* requires. *-khan-* stays closed and stays heavy.

    So a reciter feels a junction before a reader sees one. On the page these look like letters rearranged. Aloud, three of the four have taken a beat out of the line.

**Recall.**

6. A syllable may not hold more than two *mattā*: a short vowel is one, a long vowel two, and a vowel closed by a consonant two as well. A long vowel with a cluster after it would come to three, so one of the two gives way: either the vowel shortens and the consonant is doubled, or the vowel keeps its length and the cluster simplifies. Both directions occur, and *kattabbaṁ* beside *kātabbaṁ* is one word showing both.

7. Any closed syllable with a short vowel: *at-* in *attā*, *sam-* in *sammā*, *-haṁ* in *arahaṁ*. The vowel is short and the syllable is heavy, because the consonant or the niggahīta that closes it is worth the second *mattā*. Weight is a property of the syllable, not of the vowel, and running the two together is the commonest error in reading a verse line.

8. Either the niggahīta is a **nasalised vowel** (the vowels of French *genre*, *vin*, *un*: Thānuttamo's reading, with Collins, Geiger and Levman behind it) or it is a pure nasal segment after a vowel that is not itself nasalised (Oberlies's reading), which he distinguishes from the truly nasalised *sānunāsika* vowel that no Pāli script has a sign for. Neither disputes that a syllable closed by a niggahīta is heavy. That is a fact about metre, not a reconstruction of a sound, so it can be checked and the rest cannot.

9. Because a Pāli word may **begin** with a vowel or a consonant, but may **end** only in a vowel or the niggahīta; no Pāli word ends in a consonant. Two possible endings times two possible beginnings is four, and there is no fifth.

10. ***Pakatisandhi***, 'natural junction'. A chapter of rules makes a reader worse at it because rules train the expectation that something must have applied: shown two vowels side by side, a reader who has just learned six patterns of change will hunt for the one that fired instead of concluding that none did. Oberlies's way of putting it corrects this: hiatus is "generally permitted", and the question to ask is what would have had to happen for something to, not why nothing happened.

11. They shorten when a **cluster of consonants** closes their syllable, because a closed syllable cannot hold a long vowel: *mettā*, *bhāseyya*. The disputed class is clusters formed with a group nasal: *ṇḍ*, *nd*, *ñc* and the rest. Buddhappiya holds that *e* and *o* stay long before those, so *meṇḍo* and *soṇḍo* have long first vowels; Kaccāyana holds that *e* and *o* are simply always long; Moggallāna holds that if they are ever short they must be different letters, and counts the alphabet differently in consequence.

12. Either a **glide**, a consonant with no history that closes an awkward gap (the *m* of *aññamañña*, the *y* of *diyaḍḍha* and *nayidaṁ*), or a **relic**, an Old Indic word-final consonant reappearing now that the word is no longer final: the *d* of *yāvadeva* from *yāvad*, the *r* of *punareti* from *punar*. No test distinguishes them from inside Pāli, and Oberlies says why: once a language has a stock of words whose *d* or *r* comes back, it extends the habit by analogy to words that never had one. Knowing the older form settles a particular case; nothing settles the class.

13. **Forty-one**, **forty-two** or **forty-three**, and each is counting something different. Forty-one is the PTS dictionary's list and Kaccāyana's: eight vowels, thirty-two ordinary consonants, and the niggahīta counted among the consonants. Forty-two is Perniola's: the same inventory with ***ḷh*** admitted as a letter in its own right and the niggahīta counted separately. Forty-three is Moggallāna's: short *e* and short *o* counted as distinct from long *e* and long *o*, on the argument that one sound cannot have two durations.

14. *natthi* — *na* + *atthi*, a vowel meeting a vowel. The length of the surviving vowel makes it a review question, not a sandhi question. Two identical short vowels contracting should give a long one, and here they do not: the *a* stays short because the syllable *nat-* is closed by the *t* and could not hold a long vowel. The sandhi rule and the *mattā* rule both apply, and the second overrides the first.

15. *ekamantaṁ* — *ekaṁ* + *anta*, with the accusative singular ending. The *m* in the middle is a niggahīta that met a vowel, not a stem consonant, so this extremely common adverb is a frozen junction. DPD's construction is *ekaṁ* + *anta* + *aṁ*: the pieces are *eka* and *anta*, and the *m* belongs to neither. The junction has since hardened into a stem of its own (DPD files the adverb as the accusative of *ekamanta*, and the Therīgāthā declines it, with the locative *ekamante* four times), but the *m* inside it is still the niggahīta.

16. *svākkhāto* — *su* + *akkhāto*, 'well proclaimed'. Two changes at once. The sonant *u* takes its consonantal form *v* before the following vowel, which is *ādeso*; and the resulting first syllable *svāk-* has a long vowel and a closing consonant, the exception to the two-*mattā* ceiling, not an instance of it. Thānuttamo cites this word as his counter-example ("long vowels are usually not followed by conjunct consonants (one exception out of many is: *svākkhāto*)"), so the right answer names the rule *and* says that this form breaks it.

17. *diyaḍḍha* 'one and a half' — *di-* + *aḍḍha*, with a *y* between. The *y* is a glide, not a relic: there is no Old Indic *diy* behind it, and the parallel form *duvaḍḍha* shows the same gap closed with a *v* instead, as a glide can be and a relic cannot. Compare *yāvadeva* in question 12, where the consonant is fixed because it is inherited.

18. *yaṁnūna* — and this item concerns the difference between a rule and a spelling. The rule says a niggahīta before a nasal is absorbed into it, so *yaṁ* + *nūna* is analysed *yannūna*. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *yaṁnūna*, ninety-four times in the root texts, with the niggahīta intact. Both are correct: the rule describes what a reciter does and the spelling records what an editor chose. Expect the two to agree and you will be surprised in both directions.

19. *upekkhā* — or *upekhā*, and the point is that the variation is between editions. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints the double consonant 5,246 times against four; the Pali Text Society prints the single one 460 times against 106; the Buddha Jayanti is divided almost evenly against itself, 565 to 540. Three editors, three policies, and one of them without a policy. The form also shows the *a* + *i* → *e* merger (it is *upa* + *ikkhā*), so it belongs to both chapters twice over.

20. Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassāti. (MN 91 — M ii 140)

    *namo* homage · *tassa* to him · *bhagavato* to the Blessed One · *arahato* to the perfected one · *sammāsambuddhassa* to the fully awakened one · *ti* < *iti*, closing the quotation

    "Homage to him, the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened one — so [he said]."

    na·**mo** **tas**·sa bha·ga·va·**to** a·ra·ha·**to** **sam**·**mā**·**sam**·**bud**·**dhas**·**sā**·ti

    The junction is at the end, and it explains why this passage was chosen instead of the two identical ones before it. Brahmāyu says the homage three times, and the third is closed by *iti*, the quotation particle. *Sammāsambuddhassa* + *iti* gives *sammāsambuddhassāti*: the *i* of *iti* drops and the *a* before it lengthens, which is the regular behaviour of *iti* and one of the most useful junctions in the language to recognise, since *iti* closes nearly every quotation in the canon.

    Note what the lengthening does to the weights. *-ssa* was light in the two earlier repetitions and is heavy here, because it is now *-ssā*. The junction has changed the rhythm of the line, and a silent reader will never notice that.

21. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    *manopubbaṅgamā* < *mano* mind + *pubbaṅgama* going before (m. nom. pl.) · *dhammā* mental states (m. nom. pl.) · *manoseṭṭhā* < *mano* + *seṭṭha* chief · *manomayā* < *mano* + *maya* made of

    "Mind precedes mental states; mind is their chief, they are made by mind."

    ma·**no**·**pub**·**baṅ**·ga·**mā** **dham**·**mā**, ma·**no**·**seṭ**·**ṭhā** ma·**no**·ma·**yā**

    Three junctions and all three are *pakatisandhi*. *Mano* meets *p*, *s* and *m*, and keeps its *-o* every time. The line is the best short answer there is to question 10.

    The weights deserve attention. *No* is heavy three times, each time because the syllable is open and *o* is long: the reason the *ne* of *anelagalāya* is heavy, and the same trap. *Baṅ* is heavy because *ṅ* closes it, question 7's answer in a real line: a short *a* in a heavy syllable.

22. Esevanto dukkhassā. (SN 12.51 — S ii 84)

    *esevanto* < *eso* this (m. nom. sg.) + *eva* just + *anto* end (m. nom. sg.) · *dukkhassā* of suffering (n. gen. sg.)

    "Just this is the end of suffering."

    **e**·**se**·**van**·**to** **duk**·**khas**·**sā**

    Three words in one, joined by two plain elisions: the final vowel of *eso* goes, then the final vowel of *eva*, and *anto* keeps its own. DPD lists the string as a sandhi entry and prints it *es'ev'anto*, the apostrophes marking those two.

    Every syllable in the line is heavy, and that is a fact on its own: after two elisions there is no short open syllable left anywhere. Sandhi does not only change spellings; it changes the weight of the line, and in verse that is the point of it.

    The last word explains why this book's three-layer format exists. *Dukkhassā* is a genitive with its vowel lengthened before the closing quotation mark, and *esevanto* is a word no dictionary lists except as a junction. Both have to be undone before anything can be looked up, and both have to stay as printed on the quotation line, because *eso eva anto* is a sequence no manuscript contains.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

23. **bhikkhūti**, **bhikkhakoti**, **nivāsessāmīti**, **pātimokkhanti**, **panāti**. Every one stands in the canon: *bhikkhūti* has 243 root-text occurrences and *bhikkhakoti* 2.

    Three of them lengthen a short final vowel, which is the regular behaviour of *iti*: *bhikkhu* → *bhikkhū·ti*, *nivāsessāmi* → *nivāsessāmī·ti*, *pana* → *panā·ti*. In each the last syllable of the first word was light and is now heavy, and the *i-* of *iti* has gone.

    *Bhikkhako* has nothing to lengthen. Its final *-o* is long already, so the only visible change is the loss of the *i-*, and the weight of *-ko* is what it always was. A rule that appears not to have applied has applied, with nothing to change.

    *Pātimokkhaṁ* is the fifth and differs from the rest. It ends in a niggahīta, not a vowel, so there is no vowel for *iti* to lengthen. Instead the second half of the same rule applies: the *i-* of *iti* drops as it does in all five, and the niggahīta, now facing the *t* of *-ti*, assimilates to it as *n*. *-khan·ti*, heavy before and heavy after, and the count neither gains nor loses. Item 5 is the same word at the other kind of junction, where a vowel follows and the *m* surfaces instead.

    And this junction matters more than any other because the joined form is not in the dictionary. *Bhikkhūti* is two words, not one: meeting it, strip the *-ti* and then *restore the short vowel* before looking anything up, because *bhikkhū* is not the entry either; *bhikkhu* is. Two operations, and the second gets forgotten, because the form left after the first looks like a perfectly good nominative plural.
