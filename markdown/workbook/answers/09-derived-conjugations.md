## Answers: Passive, Causative and Denominative

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. *ayaṁ* this (f. nom. sg.) · *vuccati* is called (3 sg. pres. pass.) · *jarā* old age (f. nom. sg.)

    "This is called old age."

    Nothing in the form tells you it is passive. *Vuccati* ends in *-ti*, the ordinary third person singular active ending, as *pacati* 'cooks' does. The information is in the **stem**: *vucca-* is a passive stem, built with *-ya-* on the root *vac-* 'say' and assimilated to *-cc-*. That is item 8's answer arriving before item 8 asks for it, and the chapter puts it first in its section on the passive.

    Note the gender of *ayaṁ*: it is feminine because *jarā* is, and to assume the masculine *ayaṁ* would be to read the pronoun of Chapter 5 without its agreement. Item 27 is the same sentence with a neuter subject, a little later in the same analysis.

2. *ruppati* is afflicted (3 sg. pres. pass.) · *ti* (quotation) · *kho* indeed · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *tasmā* therefore · *rūpaṁ* 'form' (n. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation) · *vuccati* it is called (3 sg. pres. pass.)

    "'It is afflicted', monks — that is why it is called 'form'."

    Both finite verbs are passives, and the sentence is set for that reason. You were listening for *vuccati*, not for *ruppati*, but DPD analyses *ruppati* as a passive too. The sutta is explaining the word *rūpa* by the verb it sounds like, and the explanation only works because the thing being defined is something that *has something done to it*. Make *ruppati* active, "it afflicts", and the definition says that form does the damage instead of taking it, the opposite of the teaching the sentence belongs to. Here the grammar carries the doctrine.

3. *mettaṁ* loving-kindness (f. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *sabbalokasmi* in the whole world (m. loc. sg.) · *mānasaṁ* heart, mind (n. acc. sg.) · *bhāvaye* should develop (3 sg. opt., causative) · *aparimāṇaṁ* boundless (n. acc. sg.) · *uddhaṁ* above · *adho* below · *tiriyaṁ* across · *asambādhaṁ* unobstructed (n. acc. sg.) · *avera·masapattaṁ* < *averaṁ* without hatred (n. acc. sg.) + *asapattaṁ* without enmity (n. acc. sg.)

    "And towards the whole world one should develop a boundless heart of loving-kindness — above, below, across, unobstructed, without hatred, without enmity."

    *Bhāvaye* is built on *bhavati* 'is, becomes'. DPD gives the headword as *bhāvayati*, "caus of *bhavati*", from √*bhū*, and glosses it literally as 'causes to become'; the form here is its optative, contracted to *-e*. So the word behind the whole vocabulary of meditative development (*bhāvanā*, *bhāveti*, *bhāvita*) is the verb 'to be' with a causative on it, and it means to bring something into being.

    Some of that literal sense survives. Nobody hearing *bhāvaye* thinks 'causes to become'; the word means 'develops, cultivates', and a translator who reached for the literal would be writing about grammar instead of the mind. But the line goes beyond cultivation. It says *mānasaṁ bhāvaye aparimāṇaṁ*, and what is boundless is a state **made to exist** where it did not, more than a plant being tended. Item 22 asks you to build this form cold, and item 32 sets it against the future of the same root.

    DPD's headword is *bhāvayati*, in *-aya-*, where the chapter and this answer say *bhāveti*, in *-e-*. Both are current and both are the eighth class; Chapter 7's table gives the affix in those two forms.

    Two smaller things. *Sabbalokasmi* is a locative singular with its niggahīta dropped (DPD registers it as an irregular form of *sabbalokasmiṁ*), as verse often treats a syllable it has no room for. And *averamasapattaṁ* is two words, not a compound, *averaṁ* and *asapattaṁ*, joined by Chapter 2's rule that a final niggahīta may become *m* before a vowel.

4. "A brahmin fully quenched always sleeps at ease — one who does not cling to sensual pleasures, cooled, without attachment."

    Count the syllables and each of the four lines has **eight**: *sab-ba-dā ve su-khaṁ se-ti*, and so for the rest. That regularity is the *siloka*, the commonest metre in the canon and Chapter 18's subject, and hearing it before being told the rule is the right order to meet it in. Reading aloud here is *sajjhāya*, the technique the tradition has used to hold this text for two and a half thousand years, and it is spaced retrieval under another name.

    The verb that raises the chapter's question is ***limpati***. On the surface it has a consonant cluster before the *-ati*, the clue the chapter warns you not to trust, and it could be an ordinary present or a passive. DPD carries the headword twice, and analyses the two senses differently: *limpati 1* is `√lip + ṁa + ti`, an active with a nasal infix, 'smears, stains'; *limpati 2* is `√lip + ya + ti`, a true *-ya-* passive, 'is smeared, is stained, is stuck to'. The second entry cites this verse.

    So the question is whether *na limpati kāmesu* says that the brahmin does not smear himself with sensual pleasures or that he is not smeared by them. The form cannot decide, because two different formations have fallen together in it. A stem carries voice, as item 8 says; but where a stem and its own passive have converged, the voice has nowhere left to sit and the sense has to be read off the sentence. There the chapter's rule ends, and that limit is better met in a verse you have just said five times than in a footnote.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. *idha* here · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *anuppannānaṁ* unarisen (m. gen. pl.) · *pāpakānaṁ* bad (m. gen. pl.) · *akusalānaṁ* unwholesome (m. gen. pl.) · *dhammānaṁ* of states (m. gen. pl.) · *anuppādāya* for the non-arising (m. dat. sg.) · *chandaṁ* desire (m. acc. sg.) · *janeti* generates (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *vāyamati* strives · *vīriyaṁ* energy (n. acc. sg.) · *ārabhati* arouses · *cittaṁ* the mind (n. acc. sg.) · *paggaṇhāti* exerts · *padahati* endeavours

    "Here, monks, a monk generates desire for the non-arising of unarisen bad, unwholesome states; he strives, arouses energy, exerts the mind and endeavours."

    One **causative**, *janeti*, from *jan-* 'be born'; the other four verbs are plain actives. The literal 'causes to be born' is still faintly visible, for one brings the intention into being, but the word means 'generates'. The relation is item 3's, and by now it should look like a pattern and not a coincidence: the vocabulary of practice is full of causatives that have stopped announcing themselves.

    This is the right-effort formula and you will meet it many times, and so it is worth having by heart, not merely parsed.

6. *idha* here · *ānanda* Ānanda (m. voc. sg.) · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *sabbasaṅkhāresu* in all conditioned things (m. loc. pl.) · *aṭṭīyati* is troubled (3 sg. pres.) · *harāyati* is ashamed (3 sg. pres. denom.) · *jigucchati* is disgusted (3 sg. pres. desid.)

    "Here, Ānanda, a monk is troubled, ashamed and disgusted by all conditioned things."

    Three derived stems in a row, of two different kinds: *harāyati* is a denominative built on *hiri* 'moral shame', and *jigucchati* is one of the five surviving desideratives. The sources disagree about *aṭṭīyati*: Perniola and Oberlies both print it *attīyati*, a spelling that occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana as a free-standing word. The formula is the one the chapter quotes from AN 3.39 — A i 145, set here in a different frame on purpose: it is a stock phrase, and stock phrases teach vocabulary for nothing.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Recall.**

7. The **passive**, the **causative** and the **denominative**. Productive means the category can be extended to a verb no grammar lists: meet a causative you have never seen, and you can work it out and be right. The other two, desiderative and intensive, are unproductive, so the reverse is true of them, and the difference is practical as well as historical.

8. The **stem**. Say this one cold. A passive verb takes the ordinary active endings, so *vuccati* 'is called' and *pacati* 'cooks' are identical in their last three letters and opposite in voice. Warder and Oberlies state it in almost the same words, which is unusual agreement between a coursebook and a historical grammar. Item 1 is the shortest possible demonstration, and in item 4 the rule runs out.

9. *-ya-* straight onto a consonant-final root, where it assimilates and leaves a doubled consonant (*labbhati*); *-ī-* before *-ya-* on a vowel-final root (*pahīyati*, *dīyati*); and *-iya-* added to the present stem (*karīyati*, *desiyati*).

10. **One more.** An intransitive verb takes no object and its causative takes one; a verb with one object gives a causative with two. Warder works the arithmetic up to three and adds that in practice the three-object case is avoided.

11. Look it up; do not derive it. There are perhaps a dozen of them and each has gone its own way, so the derivation will not give you the meaning: *sikkhati* means 'trains', not 'wishes to be able', and *jigucchati* 'is disgusted', not 'wishes to keep clear of'. This is the opposite of the advice for the other three formations.

**Identify the formation.**

12. *labbhati* — **passive** of *labhati* 'gets', so 'is got, is obtained'. Route one: *labh* + *ya*, with the *y* swallowed by the *bh*. The tell is the doubled consonant in a verb whose root has a single one.

13. *kārāpeti* — **causative** of *karoti*, with the *-āpe-* suffix on a stem that is already causative (*kāreti*). 'Has something made', 'causes to be made'. Note that the second *-āp-* does not reliably add a second causation; Oberlies calls several of these blends.

14. *namassati* — **denominative** from *namas-* 'homage', so 'pays homage to', and it governs an accusative like any ordinary verb. DPD gives it a root √*namass* of its own instead, which the chapter footnotes: a denominative that has been a plain verb throughout the canon's history has, from a dictionary's point of view, become one.

15. *caṅkamati* — **intensive** of *kamati* 'walks', with the root reduplicated. One of only two intensives the canon uses as finite verbs, and the one whose intensive sense is still alive: it means walking to and fro. Item 30 has it in a sentence, beside its own noun.

16. *sādiyati* — **the first trap.** It has the form of a passive in *-iya-* and the sense of an active verb, 'consents to, accepts'. Oberlies's account is that this small class inherited part of the dead middle voice's work. Two hundred occurrences in the root texts, so it is not a curiosity.

17. *titikkhati* — **desiderative**, built on *tij-*, literally 'desires to endure' and meaning simply 'endures, is patient'. Not a trap in the same sense as 16 and 18, and the difference matters: those two mislead about the *form*, while this one is transparent in form and has merely drifted in meaning.

18. *dissati* — **the second trap.** It is the passive of *dassati* 'sees', so 'is seen', and its surface does not show it; it reads as an ordinary present of some root *diss-*. The doubled consonant is the only signal, and it is the same signal as in *labbhati*. Chapter 8's footnote on the aorists of *dassati* says why the active verb, and not this passive, heads that table.

19. *deseti* — **causative** of *dis-* 'point out', meaning 'teaches'. Like 17, transparent in form and drifted in sense: nobody hearing *deseti* thinks 'causes to point out'. Items 3 and 5 are two more of the same kind, so the drift is named, not noted once.

**Build the form.**

20. *labbhati*. *labh* + *ya* → the *y* assimilates to the preceding *bh*, giving *-bbh-*.

21. *pahīyati*. The root is *hā-* under the prefix *pa-*; being vowel-final it takes route two, *pa* + *h* + *ī* + *ya*, and the root's own vowel goes.

22. *bhāveti*. Root *bhū-*, strengthened to *bhāv-*, plus the causative *-e-*. Literally 'causes to be', and in use the ordinary word for developing or cultivating. Item 3 is the same stem in the optative, in a line most readers know by heart.

23. *kāreti* and *kārāpeti*. The root vowel is strengthened to *kār-* in both; the second adds *-āpe-* to the first. *Kāreti* also means, of a king, simply 'rules' — SN 11.12 — S i 229 has *rajjaṁ kāreti*.

24. *pahīyanti*. The passive stem *pahīya-* plus the ordinary third person plural *-nti*, which is the whole point of item 8. It has 135 occurrences in the root texts.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Read the sentence.**

25. *satisambojjhaṅgaṁ* the awakening factor of mindfulness (m. acc. sg.) · *tasmiṁ samaye* at that time (m. loc. sg.) · *bhikkhu* the monk (m. nom. sg.) · *bhāveti* develops (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *satisambojjhaṅgo* the awakening factor of mindfulness (m. nom. sg.) · *tasmiṁ samaye* at that time · *bhikkhuno* for the monk (m. gen. sg.) · *bhāvanāpāripūriṁ* fulfilment of development (f. acc. sg.) · *gacchati* goes (3 sg. pres.)

    "At that time the monk develops the awakening factor of mindfulness; at that time the awakening factor of mindfulness goes to fulfilment of development in him."

    The **causative** *bhāveti*, and beside it the abstract noun *bhāvanā* built from the same stem. Both are 'causing to be'. Seeing the verb and the noun in one sentence is the cheapest possible demonstration that a derived stem is a stem like any other and feeds the whole word-formation machinery.

26. *yo pana* whatever · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *pathaviṁ* the earth (f. acc. sg.) · *khaṇeyya* should dig (3 sg. opt.) · *vā* or · *khaṇāpeyya* should have dug (3 sg. opt. caus.) · *vā* or · *pācittiyaṁ* an offence entailing confession (n. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation)

    "Whatever monk should dig the earth or should have it dug: an offence entailing confession."

    Repeated from the chapter on purpose, for a different use here. The rule depends on the syllable *-āp-* alone: the same optative ending on two stems, one plain and one causative. The Vinaya needs the distinction between doing a thing and getting a thing done, and Pāli supplies it morphologically where English needs a separate construction.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

27. idaṁ vuccati maraṇaṁ. (SN 12.2 — S ii 3)

    The neuter *idaṁ* here where item 1 had the feminine *ayaṁ*, because *maraṇaṁ* is neuter and *jarā* feminine. The two sentences stand a little apart in the same analysis of dependent origination and differ only in that. If you produced *ayaṁ vuccati maraṇaṁ* you have made a real mistake and a useful one.

28. desetu, bhante, bhagavā dhammaṁ, desetu sugato dhammaṁ. (SN 6.1 — S i 137)

    Brahmā Sahampati's request, and the reason there is a dispensation at all. The verb is the **causative** *deseti* in the third person singular imperative, *desetu*, 'let him teach'. The easiest thing to get wrong is *bhagavā*, which is **nominative** and not vocative: it is the subject of the imperative. And the clause is a small lesson in deferential Pāli, because the vocative *bhante* is also here, addressed to the same person. Brahmā speaks *to* the Buddha and *about* him in one breath, saying in effect "Sir, let the Blessed One teach." The third person imperative makes that possible, and English has to reach for *let* to do the same thing.

29. Na ca mamāyetha kiñci lokasmiṁ. (Snp 4.14 — Sn p. 180)

    *Mamāyetha* is the **denominative** *mamāyati*, built on *mama*, the genitive of the first person pronoun, so literally 'to me-ify', in the optative middle. Seven occurrences in the root texts. If you produced an active *mamāyeyya* the grammar is sound and the line is not: this is verse, and the middle is where verse lives, which is Chapter 7's point about the *attanopada* being chosen for its sound.

**Read at length.**

30. "Now at that time the Blessed One, having risen at the time before dawn, was walking up and down in the open air. The Blessed One saw the householder Anāthapiṇḍika coming from a distance. Seeing him, he came down from the walking path and sat on the seat made ready. Seated, the Blessed One said this to the householder Anāthapiṇḍika: 'Come, Sudatta.' Then the householder Anāthapiṇḍika, thinking 'the Blessed One addresses me by name', delighted and elated, fell at the Blessed One's feet with his head right there and said this to the Blessed One: 'I trust, venerable sir, that the Blessed One slept well?'"

    - *Caṅkamati* is the **intensive verb**, 'walks up and down'; *caṅkamā* is the **ablative singular of the noun** *caṅkama*, the walking path itself, so 'having come down from the walking path'. The noun is a physical object with a step down from it, so the verb means a located, constructed activity, the walking meditation the monasteries built paths for, and not vague pacing. Take *caṅkamā* for another verb form and the step is lost, and the seat with it.
    - *Orohitvā* is 'having come down from', an absolutive of *orohati*. *Voropeti* is its causative: it **causes someone to come down from** something, and the thing they are brought down from stands in the ablative. The canon uses it for killing: *jīvitā voropeti*, 'brings down from life'. You can now see how the euphemism is built.
    - *Sukhamasayittha* is *sukhaṁ* + *asayittha*, and *asayittha* is a **middle aorist**, third person singular, Chapter 7's *attanopada* endings on Chapter 8's aorist. It is used here, and not an ordinary *-i* aorist, because this is a formal, deferential enquiry: the middle in Pāli is largely a register and not a reflexive, and Warder's word for what it adds is "dignity". Note how it has to be looked up: *sukhamasayittha* has one occurrence in the root texts and the bare *asayittha* has **none**, because the edition never separates the two words. Look for the verb alone and you will conclude it does not exist.
    - There is no passive here because every action in the passage has a named agent acting on purpose. The Buddha walks, sees, descends, sits, speaks; Anāthapiṇḍika thinks, falls, speaks. Pāli reaches for the passive when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or suppressed on purpose: *pahīyanti*, 'they are abandoned', with nobody abandoning them. Narrative with characters in it is the wrong environment for the construction, and noticing that matters more than being able to form it.

**Reaching back.**

31. Both are true, and they are statements at two different levels. The eighth class (*curādigaṇa*) is a synchronic filing category: it collects the verbs whose present stem ends in *-e-* or *-aya-*, and it does not ask where the stem came from. The causative is a derivational statement: it says *deseti* was built from *dis-*. The class is large *because* the causatives and many denominatives ended up in it, and so Warder says the causative "formally coincides with the seventh conjugation" in his numbering and why he adds that grammarians disagree about how to file individual words. If you can conjugate it, the class is what you need; if you want to know what it means, the derivation is.

32. *Bhavissati* is the future: the ordinary stem, the tense sign *-issa-*, and the ordinary ending, *bhav* + *issa* + *ti*, with no change to the root vowel. *Bhāveti* is the causative present: the root vowel strengthened to its *vuddhi* grade, *bhū* → *bhāv-*, and then *-e-* plus the ending. They can be told apart on sight by where each is marked. The future marks itself in the *suffix* and leaves the root alone; the causative marks itself in the *root* and its suffix is only a vowel. Whenever a verb of *bhū-* has a long *ā* in the root, it is not a future.

33. *Soḷasī* is a **fractional numeral**, 'a sixteenth', formed from the cardinal *soḷasa* 'sixteen' with a feminine *-ī*; *soḷasiṁ* is its accusative singular, agreeing with *kalaṁ*, the accusative singular of *kalā* 'a part, a fraction'. So *kalaṁ nāgghanti soḷasiṁ* is 'they are not worth a sixteenth part', with the thing compared understood from the preceding lines: a hundred elephants, a hundred horses, a hundred chariots and a thousand maidens are not worth a sixteenth of one step towards the Buddha. The same idiom closes Dhammapada 70. Chapter 6 treats the fractions; what this adds is that the construction is an accusative of measure with a negated *agghati*, and that the idiom is fixed at *sixteen* and not at any round number.

34. Not because *me* would be the wrong case. That is the tempting answer and Chapter 5 refutes it in advance: the Pāli enclitics have spread well beyond their Sanskrit case range, and Wijesekera gives *te* doing duty as an accusative outright. The reason is the other property Chapter 5 gives them: an enclitic is unstressed, leaning on the word before it, and the whole content of Anāthapiṇḍika's thought is in the stress. He is not reporting that he was addressed; he is astonished that the Blessed One addressed *him*, by name, having never met him. An enclitic cannot carry that, and *maṁ* can. The position rule does not settle it here, since *maṁ* is not sentence-initial and *me* would have been positionally legal.

**A causative in the case law.**

35. *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *etasmiṁ nidāne* on this occasion (nt. loc. sg.) · *etasmiṁ pakaraṇe* in this connection (nt. loc. sg.) · *bhikkhusaṅghaṁ* the Sangha of monks (m. acc. sg.) · *sannipātāpetvā* having had assembled (absol. of the causative *sannipātāpeti*) · *chabbaggiye bhikkhū* the monks of the group of six (m. acc. pl.) · *paṭipucchi* questioned (3 sg. aor.)

    "Then the Blessed One, on this occasion and in this connection, had the Sangha of monks assembled and questioned the monks of the group of six."

    The causative is ***sannipātāpetvā***, the absolutive of *sannipātāpeti*, which DPD gives as the causative of *sannipatati* 'assembles, comes together', literally 'causes to fall down together'. The formation is the *-āpe-* of this chapter, laid on a verb that already carries two prefixes: *saṁ* + *ni* + *pātāpe* + *ti*.

    The word is there for the difference it makes. A Sangha assembles, *sannipatati*; the Buddha does not. Without the causative the sentence would say that he assembled, as one of those coming together, and the passage would lose its subject. With it, he has the assembling done, and the monks assemble. A causative does not add a doer. It moves the one already there up a step and puts a new one underneath.

    Note also that the second verb, *paṭipucchi*, is not a causative at all despite the length of the word. The *paṭi-* is a prefix and the *-i* is an aorist ending; and the check comes down to this: there is no *-e-* or *-āpe-* between the stem and the ending.
