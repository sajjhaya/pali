## Answers: Review — The Verb

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. *Tasanti* and *bhāyanti* are third person plural presents and belong to **Chapter 7**; *haneyya* is an optative and belongs to **Chapter 8**; *ghātaye* is an optative built on a causative stem, and the stem is **Chapter 9**'s. Four finite verbs and the three chapters of this part, in a verse of sixteen words.

    *Haneyya* and *ghātaye* are the same mood, person and number, and the whole difference between them is the **causative**. *Haneyya* is the optative of the simple verb, "should kill"; *ghātaye* is the optative of a causative stem, "should cause to kill". One affix, and what it moves is the agent: the first forbids the hand, the second forbids the order.

    So the two halves of the line forbid two different things, and only the morphology says so. Take them as elegant variation, Pāli doubling a word for emphasis as it very often does, and you get "one should not kill, one should not kill": the second half of the precept disappears with the affix. The second half catches the one who never touches the rod. A syllable carries the difference between a rule about violence and a rule about violence and its procurement.

2. The finite verbs are *asi*, *kāhasi* and *ajjhagā*. Three clauses have no verb in them at all, and each is a past participle standing as its own predicate: *sabbā te phāsukā **bhaggā***, *gahakūṭaṁ **visaṅkhataṁ***, ***visaṅkhāragataṁ** cittaṁ*. None of the three is inflected for tense. The participle supplies the tense, since a past participle is past by its own form, together with a copula the reader has to understand and the poet has not written.

    The fourth is *diṭṭho·si*, which is the same construction with the copula printed: a past participle plus *asi*, the second person singular present of *atthi*. Note what that does to the tense. The copula is present and the sense is a completed act ("you are seen", meaning "you have been found out"), because the tense of the clause is in the participle and the copula supplies only the person.

    *Kāhasi* is the trap, and Chapter 8 explains it. It is a second person singular future of *karoti* and it has no *-ssa-* in it: in verse the *-ss-* of the future weakens to *-h-*, so that beside the ordinary *karissasi* the canon also has *kāhasi*, and beside *karissati*, *kāhati*. The affix has been worn away, not omitted, and these forms exist because they are a syllable shorter. Meeting a future you cannot cut into a stem and an affix, stop cutting. The *h*-futures are a short closed list for the two commonest verbs in the language, and they are learnt as vocabulary, not derived.

3. *Harāyati* is a **denominative**, from *hiri* 'shame'; *jigucchati* is a **desiderative**, from *gup-*; and *aṭṭīyati* is left unlabelled in Chapter 9's gloss, though the chapter discusses it. Perniola files it among the *-īya-* denominatives, and the chapter's box is about how the word is spelt, not how it is formed. So: two named formations and one the sources leave open.

    All three end in *-ti*, and that ending is Chapter 7's. It is the third person singular primary ending and it says the same thing on all three verbs: third person, singular, present, active endings. The stems are Chapter 9's, and they say something entirely different on each.

    Carry that sentence out of this part. A Pāli verb's ending shows person, number and which of the two series it belongs to, and nothing more. Whether the verb is passive, causative, denominative, desiderative or none of them is a fact about the stem, and no ending anywhere in the language will report it. Item 13 is the same lesson in one word, and item 18 shows what happens when the stem is ambiguous too.

    One thing to notice on the way past: this is a stock phrase, and Chapter 9 prints it from a different sutta. Three words that always travel together, in the canon's usual manner.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

4. *Vijānanti*, *vijānanti* and *sammanti* take the ordinary **active** endings, *parassapada*, third person plural *-anti*. *Yamāmase* is the odd one: first person plural, and its ending belongs to the middle, the *attanopada*.

    The middle does there what it always does: it turns the action back on the people performing it. "Let us restrain **ourselves**": English needs the reflexive pronoun to say what the Pāli says with four letters at the end of the verb. The active first person plural would have given "let us restrain", and the verse would then be about restraining somebody.

    This is voice in the first of item 8's two senses, and the item exists because the word does double duty. Active against middle is a fact about the *endings*, and *yamāmase* is the whole of the evidence for it in this verse. Active against passive is a fact about the *stem*, and no ending will ever report it: item 3's sentence again, reached from the other side. Run the two together and a middle ending starts looking like a passive, which it is not, and a passive stem with an active ending starts looking like an error, which it is not either.

5. "Flung open for them are the doors to the deathless; let those who have ears release their faith. Perceiving trouble, Brahmā, I did not speak the refined, sublime Dhamma among human beings."

    ***Pamuñcantu*** is a **third person plural imperative**, 'let them release', on the stem *pamuñca-*. That stem is Chapter 7's second conjugation class in its purest form. The root is *muc-*, and the *-ñ-* is a nasal pushed inside the root before the class vowel, which is what *rudhādigaṇa* means. So the specimen verb of the class, *muñcati*, turns up prefixed in the most famous verse of the sutta.

    ***Bhāsiṁ*** is an *s*-aorist, first person singular, 'I spoke', negated here, 'I did not speak'. It lacks the augment. Chapter 8's graded rule says the root aorist always takes the augment and the *a*- and *s*-aorists generally do; the augmented form would be *abhāsiṁ*, and Sanskrit has *abhāsīt* for the third singular. The last clause of that rule decides it here: in verse, the metre decides. The line is a *tuṭṭhubha* pāda of eleven syllables, and the augment cannot be accommodated either way. Written out, *na abhāsiṁ* gives twelve. Elided to *nābhāsiṁ* it keeps eleven and breaks the cadence instead: as printed the line closes —⏑—×, which is the Tuṭṭhubha's cadence, and with the long *nā* it would close ———×, which no Tuṭṭhubha does. So the absence is neither a variant reading nor a defective form. The poet has dropped a prefix he is entitled to drop.

    A note on saying it. Hold *apārutā* and *paṇītaṁ* at their length, and sound both halves of the doubled consonant in *pamuñcantu* and *vihiṁsasaññī*. The *ññ* is a held palatal nasal, not an *n*, and rushing it is the commonest fault in recitation. *Sajjhāya*, recitation, is the tradition's instrument for holding a text, and it works for the same reason spaced retrieval works: it makes you produce the thing, not merely recognise it.

6. The present stem is the third person singular of the present indicative with the *-ti* taken off: *gacchati* gives *gaccha-*, *deseti* gives *dese-*, *jānāti* gives *jānā-*. Since a dictionary cites a verb in that third singular, the stem comes free, with no analysis needed. Three forms are built on it, the **present indicative**, the **imperative** and the **optative**, so those three make one chapter. The rest is built on the root instead, or on a stem made from the root: the aorist, the future and the conditional, so they make the next chapter. The division reflects Oberlies's point that in Pāli the centre of the verb has moved from the root to the present stem, so that everything regular is derived from the stem and what is not so derived is a historical relic, and the past tense is full of relics.

7. The **primary** endings serve the present indicative and the future. The **secondary** endings serve the aorist and the conditional. The imperative has its own set. The **optative takes both**. That sentence of Perniola's says more than the tables it summarises: the future is the present endings with *-(i)ssa-* pushed in front of them, not a new paradigm, and the conditional is the future stem with the aorist's endings.

8. **Active against middle**, *parassapada* against *attanopada*, is carried by the **personal endings**: *-ti* against *-te*, *-nti* against *-nte* or *-re*. **Active against passive** is carried by the **stem**: a *-ya-* stem is passive and then takes whatever endings it likes, active-looking ones included. The endings can never tell you the second one. *Vuccati* has an ordinary active *-ti* and means 'is called'; *pacati* has the same ending and means 'cooks'. The ending cannot distinguish them, so Chapter 9's recognition procedure works on stems and not on endings.

9. *Mā* takes the aorist, and the aorist then loses its past sense entirely: *mā bhāyi* 'do not fear', a survival of the old injunctive with nothing to do with the past. *Na* takes the optative for the same English: *na seveyya* 'one should not resort to'. The difference is more than politeness. *Mā* forbids **a particular act now** (stop, don't do that), while *na* with the optative **lays down a precept**, a standing rule about what one ought not to do. Render both as "don't" and you lose the difference between a command and a training rule, which in a monastic text matters a great deal.

10. The **fourth**, the *is*-aorist, is the living one: it is formed from the present stem and, in Perniola's words, can be formed from any present stem ending in *-a*. That is a claim about productivity, and it is useful as well as historical because it runs the other way for a reader: if you know a verb's present stem you already know its most likely aorist, without knowing anything about the verb's history. And it turns the other three types into **a list of exceptions**, not a parallel system to be learnt alongside it. The root aorist in particular is five verbs; it feels large only because those five are among the commonest words in the language.

11. Productive: the **passive**, the **causative** and the **denominative**. Unproductive: the **desiderative** and the **intensive**. Productivity licenses you to meet a causative of a verb no grammar lists, work it out from the rule, and be right. It forbids the reverse move: a word that looks like a desiderative of a verb no grammar lists is almost certainly not one, because Pāli stopped making them before the canon was composed. So meeting a reduplicated stem, look the word up; do not derive it. Oberlies names five surviving forms for the two categories together (*jigucchati*, *tikicchati*, *sussūsati*, *lālappati*, *jaṅgamati*) against the hundreds of causatives in the canon, and each of them has gone its own way.

12. ***Desesi***: two readings, the ambiguity that recurs across the whole *-e-* class. It is either the third person singular *s*-aorist, 'he taught', or the second person singular present indicative, 'you teach'. The *-si* of the *s*-aorist third singular and the *-si* of the present second singular are simply identical. The person of the surrounding discourse settles it: an *s*-aorist third singular sits in narrative about somebody else, and a present second singular sits in speech addressed to someone, usually with a vocative nearby. In practice the aorist reading is far commoner, because *deseti* is a verb of narrative. Note the third thing about this form, which belongs to Chapter 9: the stem is itself a **causative**, *dis-* 'point out' strengthened to *dese-*, so the word is a causative stem in an aorist ending with a present-tense look-alike. Three chapters in six letters.

13. ***Assa***: the ambiguity spans a Part, not a chapter. It is either the third person singular optative of *atthi*, 'it would be, there might be', the *-eyya* ending contracted onto the root *as-*, or the genitive or dative singular of the demonstrative *ta(d)* / *ima-*, 'of him, to him, of this'. Both are extremely common, and the corpus cannot separate them: the string *assa* stands 812 times in the root texts, and that figure is the two readings added together, because no search can tell an optative from a genitive. Take it as a measure of how often the question arises, not of how often the answer is 'optative'. Whether the clause already has a finite verb settles it. If it does, *assa* is the pronoun; if it does not, *assa* is carrying the predicate. A second cue is a hypothetical frame (*sace*, *ce*, *yadi*, or a question about what would follow), which pulls it towards the optative.

14. ***Khomhi***: two words, not one, *kho* + *amhi*, 'I am indeed'. *Amhi* is the first person singular of *atthi*, the doublet of *asmi*, and the junction has swallowed its initial vowel. Context is not needed here, because once resolved the form is unambiguous; the difficulty lies wholly in seeing the junction. The form is not rare (*khomhi* alone stands 134 times in the root texts), and the whole of the first singular behaves this way. Neither *asmi* nor *amhi* is normally printed as a free-standing word.

15. ***Avoca***: the *a*-aorist of *vac-* 'speak', and it is either second or third person singular, since that type has the same *-a* in both. 'You said' or 'he said'. The presence of an addressee settles it: this verb is overwhelmingly the third person of reported narrative, and it is the word that stands at the end of the formula introducing direct speech, usually inside *etadavoca*. Two further things belong to it. Nothing of the root is visible on the surface (Perniola derives it through a reduplication that then weakens and contracts), and the verb has no present in *vac-* at all: its present is *vadati* and its passive is *vuccati*. The three have to be learnt as a set, which is a fair sample of what "the past tense is where the irregularity lives" means in practice.

16. ***Dissati***: the **passive** of *dassati* 'sees', so 'is seen', third person singular present. It is on this list because it looks like an ordinary present of some root *diss-*, and because it is easily glossed 'sees'. The clue is the doubled consonant where the root has a single one, *dis-* + *-ya-* assimilating to *-ss-*; the confirmation is that the simple-consonant active stands beside it in the language and that the sense is one of undergoing. The case of the surrounding words settles it: a passive has the thing acted on in the nominative and the agent, if named at all, in the instrumental. Note also what its own past tense does not show: the aorist of the active verb is *addasā*, from which no reader would ever guess *dissati*.

17. ***Sādiyati***: the trap. It has the form of a passive, an *-iya-* stem on the present stem, and the sense of an **active** verb, 'consents to, accepts'. It is not a rarity: two hundred occurrences in the root texts, and it keeps company with *vediyati* 'feels', *ādiyati* 'takes up', *samādiyati* 'undertakes' and *ruccati* 'is pleased with'. Oberlies's account is that these inherited part of the dead middle's function, an action done for oneself, which is close to what these verbs mean. Only the sense of the sentence settles it, and so Chapter 9 says the form raises a question and does not answer one. Trust the *-iya-* and you will make the Vinaya's *sādiyati* mean the opposite of what a rule needs it to mean.

18. ***Labbhetha***: the most heavily loaded form in this review. It is the third person singular optative middle on the **passive stem** of *labhati* 'gets'. So: *labh-* + *-ya-* assimilating to *labbha-*, which is Chapter 9; then the middle optative ending *-etha*, which is Chapter 7's second column. 'It would be possible, one could obtain.' There is nothing to settle here, for the form is unambiguous, and it is included for the opposite reason. Chapter 7 says the middle survives only in scattered forms and that a reader's practical need is only to recognise it. Here is a form to recognise: seventeen occurrences in the root texts, one of them in the second discourse, with a sense no other form gives.

19. Third singular: *abhavissa*. Third plural: *abhavissaṁsu*. Three steps come before the ending. Take the future stem, *bhavissa-*; prefix the **augment** *a-*; and then use the **secondary** endings, not the primary ones. That is the whole of the conditional. The sense follows from the form: a future seen from a point in the past is something that was going to happen and did not. Both forms are canonical, 143 and 27 occurrences in the root texts, and *bhavati* is the verb to peg this paradigm on because the display paradigms the grammars print (Perniola inflects *labhati*) return nothing at all from the root texts.

20. ***Siyā*** and ***assa***. They come from the two different optative series Chapter 7 sets out. *Siyā* is the *-yā* type, the older ending that survives in a few common verbs beside *vajjā* and *dajjā*. *Assa* is the ordinary ***-eyya*** contracted onto the root *as-*. Both are third person singular and both mean 'it would be, there might be'. *Siyā* has 3,843 occurrences in the root texts, and it is unambiguous, so that is the figure to trust: this is the busiest optative in the language, as it must be, since so much of the Buddha's conditional and hypothetical speech runs through 'would be'. *Assa*'s 812 cannot be read the same way; see item 13, where the same string is also a pronoun.

21. ***Gaccha*** and ***gacchāhi***, and they are the same command. One is the ending *-hi* with the stem vowel lengthened before it, by the rule that lengthens *gaccha-* before *-hi*, *-mi* and *-ma*; the other is the bare stem with no ending at all. Only stems in *-a* can take the shorter form, because in a stem with a long vowel nothing would distinguish the imperative from the stem itself: *dese* and *jānā* would be indistinguishable from the material they are built on, whereas *gaccha* at least sits in a sentence where a command is expected. Both are well attested; so are *karohi* and *suṇohi*, where the *-o-* stem shows the ending clearly.

22. ***Vuccati***. The passive suffix *-ya-* goes straight onto the consonant-final root and is swallowed by it: Perniola takes it through *vac-ya-ti* > *uc-ya-ti* > *uccati*, with a *v-* prefixed to the initial *u-*. The result has no visible *y* at all and looks like an ordinary first-conjugation present. The aorist is no help, because the aorist of this verb is *avoca*, which is a reduplicated and contracted *a*-aorist with nothing of the root left on its surface either. Present *vadati*, aorist *avoca*, passive *vuccati*: three forms, one verb, and none of them predicts another. No case in the book shows more clearly that a verb has to be learnt as a set, not derived.

23. Present third plural: *bhāventi*. Future third singular: *bhāvessati*. The trap is in the second. The causative stem is ***bhāve-***, the root *bhū-* pushed to the *vuddhi* grade and given the causative *-e-*, and the future infix attaches to *that* stem: *bhāve-* + *-ssa-* + *-ti*. So the form is *bhāvessati* with an *e*, and *bhāvissati* is not a Pāli word. It returns nothing from any layer of the corpus except the grammarians' texts, because it has been built on the present stem of the base verb instead of on the causative stem. *Bhāvessati* has 75 root-text occurrences. The general point is Chapter 9's thesis stated negatively: a derived stem adds no new endings, but it does replace the stem, and every later formation must be built on the replacement.

24. ***Mā, bhikkhave, bhāyittha.*** *Mā* with the **aorist**, second person plural, and the aorist has no past sense in this construction. Chapter 7 would have offered the imperative *mā bhāyatha*, which the canon does not use here; the aorist is the standing form after *mā*, and the reason is historical. It is really the old injunctive, never a past tense, which now merely looks like an aorist.

25. Word by word: *rūpaṁ* form (n. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *hi* for · *idaṁ* this (n. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *attā* self (m. nom. sg.) · *abhavissa* were (3 sg. cond.) · *na* not · *ayidaṁ* this · *rūpaṁ* form (n. nom. sg.) · *ābādhāya* to affliction (m. dat. sg.) · *saṁvatteyya* would lead (3 sg. opt.) · *labbhetha* it would be possible (3 sg. opt. mid., passive stem) · *ca* and · *rūpe* with regard to form (n. loc. sg.). "For if form were self, monks, this form would not lead to affliction, and it would be possible with regard to form …"

    The three verbs, in order. *Abhavissa* is the **conditional**, the form built in item 19. *Saṁvatteyya* is an ordinary **active optative**, third singular. *Labbhetha* is the middle optative on a passive stem, item 18. So one sentence carries the rarest finite form in the language, the commonest mood in the canon, and the survival of a voice that is otherwise almost extinct.

    The protasis is *rūpañca hidaṁ … attā abhavissa*, and the apodosis is everything after it. This follows the normal division of labour **exactly**: conditional in the clause carrying the condition, optative in the clause carrying the consequence. It is the textbook instance, and the next item is not.

26. *no* not · *ce* if · *taṁ* that (n. acc. sg.) · *padaheyya* one were to strive (3 sg. opt.) · *na* not · *ayidaṁ* this · *saccaṁ* the truth (n. acc. sg.) · *anupāpuṇeyya* one would arrive at (3 sg. opt.). "If one were not to strive, one would not arrive at the truth."

    The condition is marked by ***ce***, the particle, not the verb, and **both verbs are optatives**. There is no conditional in the sentence at all.

    What the pair teaches is a proportion. Chapter 8 says the conditional is the rarest finite form in the language and that the optative usually stands in for it; item 26 shows what "usually" looks like on the page. You will build the conditional once and meet the optative in conditional clauses constantly, so the form to have cold is *padaheyya*, and *abhavissa* is a form to recognise. Note also *saccamanupāpuṇeyya*, which is *saccaṁ* + *anupāpuṇeyya* with the niggahīta becoming *m* before a vowel. The verb is a *-uṇā-* stem of Chapter 7's fourth class, and if you do not undo the junction you will hunt for a verb *manupāpuṇāti*.

27. *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhū* the monks (m. acc. pl.) · *āmantesi* addressed (3 sg. aor.) · *handa* come now · *dāni* now · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *āmantayāmi* I address (1 sg. pres.) · *vo* you (acc./dat. pl., enclitic) · *vaya·dhammā* subject to decay (m. nom. pl.) · *saṅkhārā* conditions (m. nom. pl.) · *appamādena* with diligence (m. inst. sg.) · *sampādetha* accomplish (2 pl. imper.). "Then the Blessed One addressed the monks: 'Come now, monks, I address you: conditions are subject to decay; accomplish it with diligence.'"

    *Āmantesi* and *āmantayāmi* are one stem in its two shapes. The eighth conjugation class takes the affix ***-e-* or *-aya-***, one affix written contracted and uncontracted, *āmante-* and *āmantaya-*, not two. Chapter 7's table of the classes has both in a single row, against *deseti*. So *āmantesi* is that stem with the *s*-aorist third singular ending, and *āmantayāmi* is the same stem uncontracted with the primary first singular *-mi*, the stem vowel lengthened before it by the rule of item 21.

    *Sampādetha* is a **causative**: DPD gives it as the causative of *sampajjati*, *saṁ* + *pāde* + *ti*, and the root vowel strengthening to *-pāde-* is the visible mark of it. The mood is the **second person plural imperative**, whose ending is identical to the present indicative's, so the form could as easily be read 'you accomplish'; the vocative and the sense settle it. Sit with the fact that the last sentence the Buddha is recorded as speaking ends on a causative imperative: *cause it to be accomplished, by means of diligence*, more than "be diligent".

28. The sense: "In the same way, monks, in a future time there will be monks who, when those discourses spoken by the Realized One — deep, profound in meaning, transcendent, dealing with emptiness — are being recited, will not want to listen, will not lend an ear, will not apply their minds to understand, and will not think those teachings worth learning and mastering."

    Five verbs are in the **future**, formed by inserting *-(i)ssa-* between the stem and the ordinary primary endings: *bhavissanti*, *sussūsissanti*, *odahissanti*, *upaṭṭhāpessanti*, *maññissanti*.

    Now the stems underneath them, and this is the item's point:

    - *bhavissanti*: the plain present stem *bhava-*.
    - *sussūsissanti*: a **desiderative**, *sussūsa-*, 'desires to hear', one of the five that survive.
    - *upaṭṭhāpessanti*: a **causative** in *-āpe-*, from *upaṭṭhahati*, 'causes to stand near'. Note *-essanti* and not *-issanti*, for the reason given in item 23.
    - *maññissanti*: a ***-ya-* stem** of the third conjugation class, *mañña-* from *man-*.

    And a fifth kind is present without being finite: *bhaññamānesu* is a **passive** stem, *bhaṇ-* + *-ya-* assimilated, in a locative absolute. So a desiderative, a causative, a class sign and a passive are all carrying the same tense, with the same endings, in one sentence. That demonstrates Chapter 9's argument (one set of endings, several stems) in Chapter 8's tense, better than any table could.

    *Maññissanti* is **not** a passive. The doubling is the third conjugation's *-ya-* class sign, not the passive's, and those are the same morph, so Chapter 9 demotes the doubled consonant from a test to a reason to look. Two of the three checks fail here: there is no simple-consonant active *manati* standing beside it in the canon, and the sense is not one of undergoing. 'They will think', not 'they will be thought'.

29. *uṭṭhehi* rise! (2 sg. imper.) · *vīra* hero (m. voc. sg.) · *vijita·saṅgāma* victor in battle (m. voc. sg.) · *sattha·vāha* caravan leader (m. voc. sg.) · *anaṇa* debtless one (m. voc. sg.) · *vicara* wander! (2 sg. imper.) · *loke* in the world (m. loc. sg.) · *desassu* teach! (2 sg. imper. mid.) · *bhagavā* Blessed One (m. voc. sg.) · *dhammaṁ* the Dhamma (m. acc. sg.) · *aññātāro* those who understand (m. nom. pl.) · *bhavissanti* there will be (3 pl. fut.). "Rise, hero, victor in battle! Caravan leader, debtless one, wander the world! Let the Blessed One teach the Dhamma — there will be those who understand."

    *Uṭṭhehi* and *vicara* are both second person singular imperatives and they look nothing alike because the imperative has two shapes in that one cell: the ending *-hi*, and the bare stem with no ending. *Uṭṭhehi* takes the ending; *vicara* is the naked stem *vicara-*. Item 21 is this distinction met in the wild, one line apart.

    *Desassu*: the stem is the **causative** *dese-*, from *dis-* 'point out'; the mood is the **imperative**; the person and number are **second singular**; and the voice is **middle**. DPD parses it as *dese* + *ssu*, calling it an irregular form of the expected *desessu*. The middle imperative second singular in *-ssu* is in Chapter 7's second column, where a reader is told they need only recognise such forms, and here is one, in a verse every Buddhist knows, saying what no active form could.

    *Desetu*, the reading of the Syāmese, Cambodian and PTS editions, is the **third person singular active** imperative: 'let him teach'. So the two readings differ in person and in voice at once, and what changes is who Brahmā is talking to. *Desetu* keeps the deference of the prose section three verses earlier, where Brahmā speaks about the Buddha in the third person: *desetu, bhante, bhagavā dhammaṁ*, 'sir, let the Blessed One teach'. *Desassu* addresses him directly and in the middle voice: **teach, thou**. Read with the two imperatives before it, *uṭṭhehi* and *vicara*, which are unambiguously second person, the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's reading is the harder and the more coherent one, for the verse has been urging him on for three lines. No doctrine depends on it. But the editions disagree here about whether a god is addressing the Buddha or speaking about him, not about a spelling, and that is a fact about the apparatus.

30. *ekaṁ samayaṁ* at one time · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *uruvelāyaṁ* at Uruvelā (f. loc. sg.) · *viharati* dwells (3 sg. pres.) · *najjā* of the river (f. gen. sg.) · *nerañjarāya* Nerañjarā (f. gen. sg.) · *tīre* on the bank (n. loc. sg.) · *ajapāla·nigrodha·mūle* at the root of the goatherd's banyan (m. loc. sg.) · *paṭhama·abhisambuddho* newly awakened (m. nom. sg.); *atha kho* then · *bhagavato* to the Blessed One (m. gen. sg.) · *raho·gatassa* gone into seclusion (m. gen. sg.) · *paṭisallīnassa* withdrawn (m. gen. sg.) · *evaṁ* thus · *cetaso* of mind (n. gen. sg.) · *parivitakko* a reflection (m. nom. sg.) · *udapādi* arose (3 sg. aor.).

    *Viharati* is a **present indicative** and *udapādi* an **aorist**, and both describe the same afternoon some centuries ago. This is the **historic present**, not an inconsistency, and Pāli uses it freely where English can manage it only in a consciously colloquial register. Every sutta opens this way, and translators render it as a past for that reason. The event has not moved; only the tense has.

    Look again at *udapādi*, because there the augment can be seen behaving as Chapter 8 says it does. The word is *ud* + *a* + *pad* + *i*: the prefix, then the **augment**, then the root. The augment is **buried in the middle** of the word, not at the front, and there a reader is most likely to lose it and read *-apād-* as part of the stem.

31. Ahañceva kho pana dhammaṁ deseyyaṁ; pare ca me na ājāneyyuṁ. (SN 6.1 — S i 136) Two optatives, one first person singular and one third person plural, and no conditional anywhere: item 26's proportion again. *Ahañceva* is *ahaṁ* + *ca* + *eva*, with the niggahīta assimilating to the following palatal. If you produced *sace ahaṁ dhammaṁ deseyyaṁ* you have written good Pāli and not this sentence, which is the exercise: the condition here is carried by *ca … ca* and the mood, without *sace*.

32. **Mā, bhikkhave, puññānaṁ bhāyittha.** (AN 7.62 — A iv 88) *Mā* with the second person plural aorist, as in item 24. The thing feared stands in the **genitive**, *puññānaṁ*, which is not what an English speaker expects and is the ordinary construction with this verb. The line is worth carrying whole for its sense as much as its grammar: it introduces the Buddha's account of his own past merit, and it is a prohibition against a fear a monastic audience might well have.

33. **Bhārañca vo, bhikkhave, desessāmi bhārahārañca bhārādānañca bhāranikkhepanañca.** (SN 22.22 — S iii 25) The future of the causative *deseti*, first person singular: *dese-* + *-ssa-* + *-mi*, with the stem vowel lengthened before the first-person ending. Note again *-essāmi* and not *-issāmi*, for item 23's reason. *Vo* is the enclitic, on which see item 36, and the four accusatives are strung with *ca* after each, not only after the last, as the canon does and English does not.

34. Brahmā's speech is the argument, and the three verbs are its three moves. ***Parihāyanti*** is a **present indicative** stating what is the case now: there are beings with little dust in their eyes, and they are declining for want of hearing. That is the evidence. ***Desetu*** is an **imperative**, third person singular, and it is the request; the third person makes it deferential, not a command, since Pāli has no politeness marker of its own and the person serves instead. ***Bhavissanti*** is a **future**, and it is the inducement: a flat prediction, not a wish, *there will be those who understand*. Evidence, request, consequence, in three sentences and three moods. It also demonstrates Chapter 7's remark that the third-person imperative covers instruction, invitation, blessing and entreaty alike, and that which one is meant depends on who is speaking to whom. A god entreating a Buddha is at the far end of that range.

    *Pasāreyya* and *samiñjeyya* are optatives in a **simile**, and neither wishes nor supposes anything. This is the optative's second use, the polite or provisional statement: here the hypothetical of comparison, "just as a strong man *might* stretch out his bent arm or bend his outstretched arm". English does the same thing with "would" or "might" in a simile. It matters because, having learnt the optative as the mood of conditions, you will look for a condition, and there is none: the whole simile is a single suspended supposition with no consequent clause at all.

    *Nassati* and *vinassati* are presents used to state what is always the case or what is now unavoidably in train, and Bodhi's "is lost" and "is to perish" render the sense, not the tense. Neither is a present progressive, and the two are not even rendered alike: the first is done and the second is coming, which is the range a single Pāli present covers here. Chapter 7 treats this under what the present tense does: a general truth has no particular time, and Pāli puts it in the present, reaching into the future without any future form. Brahmā is not reporting that the world is at this instant perishing; he is saying that this is how it goes, given what he has just learnt.

    The aorists are *etadahosi*, *pāturahosi*, *etadavoca*, *idamavoca* and *etadavoca* again: five instances, and only two verbs between them. Three of the five are *avoca*. Both *etadahosi* and *pāturahosi* are *ahosi*, the *s*-aorist of *hoti*. A Pāli narrative is built so heavily out of two verbs because the canon was composed for recitation, and its narrative frame is formulaic by design: somebody thinks something, somebody appears somewhere, somebody says something. *Etadahosi*, *pāturahosi*, *etadavoca* are the joints of that frame, and to learn those three phrases whole is to learn the framework of several thousand suttas. Note that all three are junctions (*etaṁ* + *ahosi*, *pātu* + *ahosi*, *etaṁ* + *avoca*), the subject of item 35.

35. *Etadavoca* is *etaṁ* + *avoca*, with the old stem-final *-d-* of *etad* surfacing before the vowel. *Idamavoca* is *idaṁ* + *avoca*, with the same thing happening to the *-m* of *idam*. *Tenañjaliṁ* is *tena* + *añjaliṁ*, the two *a*-sounds merging. *Khomhi* is *kho* + *amhi*.

    ***Khomhi*** defeats a search of a digital text, and the reason needs stating carefully. In the first three the verb survives the junction intact: *avoca* and *añjaliṁ* are still there as substrings, so searching for the word inside a longer word finds them. In *khomhi* the junction has eaten the initial vowel of the second word, so what is left is *-mhi* and not *-amhi*. A search for *amhi* as a word standing alone finds nothing; and the usual remedy of searching for it inside longer words finds nothing either, or worse, finds the pronominal locatives *tamhi* and *yamhi*, which are a different ending on a different word class and make the empty result look like a finding. The rule to carry: when a form begins with a vowel, look for it without that vowel too, because a preceding word ending in a vowel will have absorbed it.

36. *Vo* and *me* are the **enclitic** forms of the second and first person pronouns, *vo* standing for *tumhe*, *tumhākaṁ* or *tumhaṁ*, and *me* for *mayā*, *mama* or *mayhaṁ*. They are preferred because they are unstressed and short: the canon reaches for the enclitic wherever the pronoun is carrying no weight of its own, and reaches for the full form where it is being contrasted or emphasised. That is the principle behind Chapter 5's account of why a pronoun subject appears at all: a full form adds emphasis, an enclitic is merely present. The rule of position they both obey is that an enclitic can never stand first in its sentence. It has no accent to begin a sentence with, and it leans on the word before it, which is what "enclitic" means. So *āmantayāmi vo* and *pare ca me na ājāneyyuṁ* both place them second or later, and to tell whether a *no* or a *vo* is a pronoun or a particle, begin by asking where in the clause it sits.

37. *Cattāro* joins *iddhipādā* by **agreement**, the first of Chapter 6's four constructions, and the one a reader most readily assumes is the only one. *Catu* is a numeral below nineteen, so it behaves as an adjective: no gender of its own, taking the masculine of *iddhipādā*, and standing in the same case and number, nominative plural.

    *Tiṭṭheyya* is the **optative**, and it marks the difference between a claim and an entitlement. An indicative, *tiṭṭhati*, 'he lives on', would assert that the Buddha does in fact live for the full lifespan, which is not what is being said. The optative with *ākaṅkhamāno*, 'wishing', says he **could** if he wished, which leaves the wishing unexercised. The whole of the passage that follows in DN 16, and Ānanda's failure in it, depends on the sentence having been a statement of capacity, not of fact. This is the optative's second use again, the provisional statement, and a good instance of a mood carrying doctrinal weight.

38. The sentence works without a finite verb because *desitā* is a past participle, and a past participle plus a nominative subject is a complete Pāli sentence. Chapter 9 makes the point in an aside: the commonest way of saying in Pāli that something is done is a past participle, not a passive verb, and by a long way.

    *Mayā* is the **instrumental** of the first person pronoun, and it is in that case because it is the agent of a passive construction. That is the whole syntax of the passive: the thing acted on stands in the nominative, and the agent, when it is named at all, stands in the instrumental. Here it is named because it matters who taught them.

    *Desitā* has to agree with ***dhammā***, and it does: masculine nominative plural, as a participle used as an adjective would. That agreement checks the reading, for if *desitā* were not predicated of *dhammā*, it would not carry that ending. "These, monks, are the things taught by me from my own direct knowledge."
