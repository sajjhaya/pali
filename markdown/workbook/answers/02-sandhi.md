## Answers: Sandhi

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Yo panāti yo yādiso … Eso vuccati "yo panā"ti. (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 23)

    The long *ā* comes from the meeting, and it belongs to neither word. *Pana* ends in a short *a* and *iti* begins with a short *i*; the *i* is lost, and what is left lengthens to pay for it. Said at speed the two syllables simply will not stay apart, which is the whole of the explanation.

    This line comes first because here the canon does the exercise itself. Each rule of the Pātimokkha is followed by a word-analysis that quotes the rule's word as the rule prints it, joined, and then gives it back with the join marked instead of written: *Eso vuccati "yo panā"ti*. And the division does not restore the words. The *ā* has to be left standing somewhere, and the editor leaves it on *panā*, where it never was.

2. Bhikkhūti bhikkhakoti bhikkhu, (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 24)

    *Bhikkhūti* is *bhikkhu* + *iti*, and the short *u* lengthens to *ū*. *Bhikkhakoti* is *bhikkhako* + *iti*, and the *o* stays as it is, because *o* is already long. The rule is that a *short* vowel before the quotative lengthens, and there was nothing here to lengthen.

    So the same junction has two outcomes, and neither is a special case: one vowel was short and the other was not. This uses the fact of set 01's item 11, and so the script chapter comes first.

3. "Parimaṇḍalaṁ nivāsessāmīti sikkhā karaṇīyā"ti. (Bu Sk 1 — Vin iv 185)

    *Nivāsessāmi* + *iti* gives *nivāsessāmīti*: the *i* of *iti* is lost and the *i* before it lengthens. Set beside the first two items, the three cover *a* → *ā*, *u* → *ū* and *i* → *ī*, with *o* unchanged. One rule, four vowels, and only the short ones move.

    There is a practical consequence, and it is why these three items are here and not in a footnote. The word's own last vowel has changed, so the form a dictionary files it under is **not even contained** in the printed text. *Nivāsessāmi* has 0 root-text occurrences. *Nivāsessāmīti* has 2 root-text occurrences. Every *sekhiya* rule is built this way, so the whole class answers a whole-word search with silence, and to trust the silence is to conclude that a common form does not exist.

4. Esevanto dukkhassā. (SN 12.51 — S ii 84)

    Three words inside one, and both junctions are the plainest kind: the final vowel of *eso* drops, then the final vowel of *eva* drops, and *anto* keeps its own. Two vowels lost, one surviving. DPD lists the whole string as a sandhi entry with that construction, and prints it *es'ev'anto*, the apostrophes marking the two elisions.

    Take two points from it. Unless you divide this correctly you cannot look up any of the three words, because *esevanto* is in no dictionary as a headword except as a sandhi form. And the resolved sequence *eso eva anto* is a string no manuscript contains, so this book puts the resolution on the gloss line and never inside the quotation.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. …yena mithilā yena brahmāyu brāhmaṇo tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā brahmāyuṁ brāhmaṇaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdi. (MN 91 — M ii 135)

    "…he went to Mithilā, to the brahmin Brahmāyu; and having approached, he bowed to the brahmin Brahmāyu and sat down to one side."

    Four junctions, of three different kinds.

    *Tenupasaṅkami* is *tena* + *upasaṅkami*, the final *a* elided before the *u*. It is the commonest single sandhi in the canon, because it sits inside this formula, and if you do not recognise it you will look up a verb that does not exist. *Brahmāyu*, the man's name, is itself a compound, *brahma* + *āyu*, with two *a*-sounds contracting to *ā*; that is internal sandhi, and so the name is not *Brahmaāyu*. *Ekamantaṁ* is *ekaṁ* + *anta* with the accusative ending, so the *m* in the middle is a niggahīta that met a vowel, as in item 8, and so this stock adverb, one of the most frequent phrases in the canon, is a frozen sandhi.

    And the exercise was fishing for the fourth: *abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ*. A long *ā* stands immediately before an *e*, the two words are said in sequence, and **nothing happens**. That is *pakatisandhi* across a word boundary, and it is the ordinary state of a Pāli sentence. Perniola's point stands behind it: in Pāli, unlike Sanskrit, words in a sentence are kept separate unless habit has run them together.

6. Pātimokkhanti ādimetaṁ mukhametaṁ pamukhametaṁ kusalānaṁ dhammānaṁ. Tena vuccati pātimokkhanti. (Kd 2 — Vin i 103)

    The odd one out is ***pātimokkhanti***, and what decided it is what stood in front of the niggahīta.

    In *ādimetaṁ*, *mukhametaṁ* and *pamukhametaṁ*, the niggahīta of *ādiṁ*, *mukhaṁ* and *pamukhaṁ* meets the vowel of *etaṁ* and becomes the labial *m*. In *pātimokkhanti* it meets *iti*, and *iti* loses its initial *i* first, which brings the niggahīta up against a *t*, where it becomes the dental *n*. Same niggahīta, two outcomes, and the vowel that decided it is no longer there to be seen. It is the two-step of item 8 in a different order, and it explains why the fourth of the chapter's four junction types is kept separate from the third.

    There is the sense of it as well. *Ādi*, *mukha*, *pamukha*, beginning, face, fore: the Pātimokkha is being described as the front of the wholesome qualities, which is the traditional etymology the word is given.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

7. *natthi* — **vowel + vowel**. It is *na* + *atthi*, and DPD lists it under that construction. Two short *a*s meet and one survives, and the survivor is **short**. Two identical vowels contracting ought to give a long one, but the resulting syllable *nat-* is closed by the *t*, and a closed syllable cannot hold a long vowel. The two-*mattā* ceiling from the previous chapter decides it, and so the form is *natthi* and not *nātthi*.

8. *ahampi* — **niggahīta + vowel**, though the *m* on the page makes it look like a consonant junction. It is *ahaṁ* + *api*: the initial vowel of *api* drops, which brings the niggahīta up against the *p*, and the niggahīta then takes the nasal of the labial group. Two changes, in that order, and the second is only possible because the first happened.

9. *tañca* — **niggahīta + consonant**. *Taṁ* + *ca*, with the niggahīta taking the palatal nasal before the palatal *c*. The plainest instance of the rule there is.

10. *nappajānāti* — **vowel + consonant**. *Na* + *pajānāti*, with the *p* doubling. The doubling is historical, not decorative: the *p* had a cluster behind it in Old Indic, the cluster simplified at the beginning of a word, and putting a word in front restores the middle-of-word environment in which it survives. Compare *na* + *khamati* → *nakkhamati*.

11. *tenupasaṅkami* = ***tena*** + ***upasaṅkami***, 'he approached by that [way]'. The final *a* of *tena* is elided before the *u*. This is the commonest single sandhi in the canon, because it sits inside the stock narrative formula *yena … tena upasaṅkami*, and if you do not recognise it you will look up a verb that does not exist.

12. *sveva* = ***so*** + ***eva***, 'that very one'. Two steps. The diphthong *o* is resolved into its parts *a* + *u*; then the sonant *u*, now standing before a vowel, takes its consonantal form *v*, and the *a* drops. That is the chapter's point about *ādeso*: no consonant has appeared out of nowhere; a vowel has changed role.

13. *etadavoca* = ***etaṁ*** + ***avoca***, 'said this'. The *d* is not inserted. *Etad* is what this pronoun looked like in Old Indic, and before a vowel the old final consonant surfaces again; DPD gives the Sanskrit as *etad*. The same *d* appears in *yadeva*, *sammadeva*, *bahudeva* and *sakideva*.

14. *appekacce* = ***api*** + ***ekacce***, 'some'. Two steps again, and DPD spells them out: *api* > *apy* > *app*. The *i* of *api* becomes its semivowel *y* before the following vowel, and the resulting *py* then assimilates: *p* and *y* are of unequal strength, a mute against a semivowel, so by the rule the weaker gives way to the stronger. This is the single most instructive form in the set, because it needs the *ādeso* rule and the strength hierarchy in sequence.

15. *yāvadeva* = ***yāva*** + ***eva***, 'just as far as'. As with 13, the *d* is a relic and not a bridge: Old Indic *yāvad*. This is the item the chapter warns against getting confidently wrong. Dividing it as *yāva* + *d* + *eva*, with *d* as an inserted glide, gives two real words and one invented rule; dividing it as *yāvad* + *eva* explains where the *d* came from and predicts *punareti* from *punar* as well.

16. *evañhi* = ***evaṁ*** + ***hi***, 'for thus'. The niggahīta takes the palatal nasal before *h*, which belongs to no group of its own. The same thing happens in *bhavañhi*, which opens the Soṇadaṇḍa passage quoted in the chapter on script.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

17. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    *manopubbaṅgamā* < *mano* mind + *pubbaṅgama* going before (m. nom. pl.) · *dhammā* mental states (m. nom. pl.) · *manoseṭṭhā* < *mano* + *seṭṭha* chief · *manomayā* < *mano* + *maya* made of

    "Mind precedes mental states; mind is their chief, they are made by mind."

    Every junction in the line is *pakatisandhi*, and that is the point of setting it. *Mano* meets *p*, then *s*, then *m*, and its final *-o* stands unchanged in all three. Nothing has happened, nothing was going to, and a reader hunting for a rule here will invent one.

    The one change is invisible and belongs to the noun chapter, not this one: *mano* is the *-as* stem *manas*, and the *-o* is what *-as* became at the end of a word. That change happened long before the compound was formed.

18. *coro* + *asi* · *bālo* + *asi* · *mūḷho* + *asi* · *theno* + *asi* + *iti*

    "'You are a robber, you are a fool, you are deluded, you are a thief.'"

    Each of the four is a nominative noun or adjective followed by *asi*, the second person singular of *atthi*, 'you are'. The junction is the elision this set's third stage drills: the final *-o* of the noun stands, the initial *a-* of *asi* is lost, and the two words are written as one. *Coro asi* becomes *corosi*, and so for *bālo*, *mūḷho* and *theno*. There is no other change: no vowel lengthens and no consonant assimilates.

    The fourth is longer because it carries a second junction on top of the first. It closes the quotation, so *iti* follows it, and the quotative lengthens the vowel in front of it: *thenosi* + *iti* gives *thenosīti*. Two joins in one word, of different kinds: an elision at the first and a lengthening at the second.

    That second join leaves a mark. *Thenosi* has 0 root-text occurrences as a word standing alone. The edition has *thenosī*, with 2 root-text occurrences, because the form never occurs without the quotative behind it.

    The fully joined *thenosīti* has 1 root-text occurrence, which is the line quoted here. Looked for as it would be spelt in isolation, the form is not found. The word is there and the lookup was wrong, which is what the edition does to any word whose last syllable a following particle has altered.

    *Theno* is 'thief', from √*then*; *coro* is the ordinary word for a robber. The Vinaya prints both because the rule is defining what kind of taking would have a king treat a man as a criminal, and it wants the whole vocabulary of the accusation.

19. The four junctions made, not undone.

    (a) *tenupasaṅkami* (MN 91 — M ii 135): the final *a* of *tena* is elided before the *u*, and no other change occurs.

    (b) *esevanto* (SN 12.51 — S ii 84): two elisions in sequence: the *o* of *eso* goes, then the *a* of *eva*, and *anto* is left with its own vowel intact.

    (c) *corosi* (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 47): the initial *a-* of *asi* is elided and the *-o* of *coro* stands. Note that this is the mirror image of (a): there the first word lost its vowel, here the second word loses its own.

    (d) *nivāsessāmīti* (Bu Sk 1 — Vin iv 185): the *i* of *iti* is lost and the short *i* before it lengthens.

    If you produced (a) and (c) and hesitated over (b) and (d), that is the expected shape of the result. A single elision is a rule you can hold; two in sequence, and a change that alters a vowel you thought you had finished with, are what actually has to be practised. Producing a junction and recognising one are different skills, and this book has drilled the second nine times before asking for the first.

20. *Panā* occurs elsewhere, and it is still not a word.

    The test returns a positive: *panā* has 6 root-text occurrences. So the division *yo panā* + *ti* passes the check that the chapter's phantom-glide test applies, and it should not.

    Look at what the six are. Every one of them is in the Mahāniddesa and every one is in a single verse line, *Saddhammapūjāpi panā tatheva*; DPD carries no headword for *panā* at all. The test has found a string in the edition, not a word the language has.

    So the test rules a division out; it does not rule one in. That qualifies it without making an exception to it, because the test only ever had one purpose: a division whose pieces occur nowhere is certainly wrong, and a division whose pieces occur somewhere is merely not yet refuted. Item 15 is the same point from the other side — *yāva* and *eva* both occur, and the division that uses them is still the wrong one.

    Hold the two together and the chapter's real position appears. A division is a hypothesis about what a speaker said, and it is tested by whether it explains the form, not by whether its pieces can be found in an index.
