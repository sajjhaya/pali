## Answers: The Present System

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Pare ca na vijānanti, mayamettha yamāmase; Ye ca tattha vijānanti, tato sammanti medhagā. (Dhp 6 — Dhp p. 2)

    *Yamāmase* is the odd one out, and its ending marks it: *-āmase*, which the chapter's imperative table gives as a first person plural in the middle voice. The other three verbs, *vijānanti* twice and *sammanti*, are third person plural present indicatives in the ordinary active *-nti*. So the verse holds one form from the column of the table that most readers never need and three from the column they will use every day.

    Look again at two of them. *Vijānanti* shows the chapter's stem rule at work. The verb is *vijānāti*, stem *vijānā-* with the long *-ā-* of the fifth class, and in the third plural that long vowel is absorbed into the ending and comes out short. *Jānāti* but *jānanti*, and here *vijānāti* but *vijānanti*: the same alternation the first-person forms show in the other direction.

    And *sammanti* is the verb of item 18, which is the verse immediately before this one. DPD gives *medhaga* 'argument, dispute' as *√medh* + *aka* > *aga*, and cites this verse for it.

    The chapter quotes the first half of this from MN 128, where the Buddha addresses it to the monks of Kosambī who will not stop quarrelling. Dhammapada 6 is the same two lines with two more after them, and the two more supply the consequence: the quarrels subside. Whoever has met the line in either place has met the *attanopada*, named or not, and so the middle gets half a page here and not a chapter.

2. Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno; Attānaṁ upamaṁ katvā, na haneyya na ghātaye. (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19)

    The two optatives differ only in their endings. *Haneyya* is the full form, the *-eyya* of the chapter's third person singular; *ghātaye* is the contracted *-e*, the alternative the same cell of the same table gives. Same mood, same person, same number; if you do not know that *-e* is an optative ending you will take the second for a present and lose the sentence's whole force.

    Their stems differ too, and that is the second thing to see. *Haneyya* is built on *hana-*, the plain stem of *hanati* 'strikes, kills'. *Ghātaye* is built on *ghātaya-*, the eighth class in its *-aya-* form, not its *-e-* form, the class that gives *deseti*. Whether it is also a **causative** is a question the sources answer differently. The Dhammapada commentary takes it that way, glossing the line *neva paraṁ pahareyya na paharāpeyya*, 'one should neither strike another nor have another struck', where *paharāpeyya* can only be a causative. DPD gives the headword *ghātayati* simply as 'kills, slaughters', from *√ghaṭ*, and cites this line for it. The parsing does not depend on it. *-aya-* is the eighth class's affix and the causative's affix alike, so Chapter 9 has to separate them and this chapter does not.

    The gradation is visible in the first half as well. *Tasanti* is from *√tas*, and *a* is already at the *guṇa* grade, so the stem *tasa-* shows no change at all; *bhāyanti* is from *√bhī*, and its stem *bhāya-* is that root raised. So two verbs of nearly the same meaning, side by side in one couplet, look nothing like each other.

    The verse turns at its middle. Two present indicatives state what is the case; two optatives state what one ought therefore to do. Dhammapada 130 is the same four lines with the second replaced (*sabbesaṁ jīvitaṁ piyaṁ*, "life is dear to all"), so the frame is used twice with two different reasons hung on it, and the mood has the same function both times.

3. Na paro paraṁ nikubbetha, nātimaññetha katthaci na kañci; Byārosanā paṭighasaññā, nāññamaññassa dukkhamiccheyya. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

    *Nikubbetha* and *nātimaññetha* are the middle pair, both in *-etha*, which the chapter's optative table gives as the third person singular of the *attanopada*. DPD parses *nikubbetha* so, "opt, reflx 3rd sg", and *nātimaññetha* is *na* + *atimaññetha*, the negative absorbed into the verb by the sandhi of Chapter 2. The third optative, *iccheyya*, is the ordinary active *-eyya* on the stem *iccha-*.

    So all three are the same mood and the same person, and the difference between them is voice alone. The verse is here to show that the middle is a different column, not a different grammar: see *-etha* against *-eyya* and you have learnt the whole practical content of the *attanopada*.

    A verse is where such forms are found because, as Duroiselle says in the passage the chapter quotes, the choice between the two voices "is mostly determined now by metrical exigencies". *Nikubbeyya* would not scan. There is a second reason here, more interesting than the metre: DPD builds *nikubbati* on *√kar*, the root of *karoti*, *ni* + *kubba* + *ti*, literally 'makes down'. One root, and the chapter's table gives it the stem *karo-* while this verse gives it *kubba-*. The stem is the working unit and the root is not: the chapter's opening claim, in a line of the Mettasutta.

    The two feminine nouns of the third *pāda* are glossed above without a case label, on purpose. The ending *-ā* on a feminine *ā*-stem is the nominative, and the sense every rendering wants is "out of anger, out of thoughts of enmity", which is an ablative. This item does not depend on it and the workbook does not adjudicate it; the item is about the verbs.

4. Rūpaṁ 'netaṁ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā'ti samanupassati. (MN 22 — M i 136)

    *Nesohamasmi* is four words: *na* + *eso* + *ahaṁ* + *asmi*, "not, this, I, am". DPD registers the whole string as a sandhi compound and resolves it into those four, calling it *ind* + *pron* + *pron* + *pr*. Two of its neighbours in the same quotation show the same on a smaller scale: *netaṁ* is *na* + *etaṁ*, and *meso* is *me* + *eso*.

    The verb form buried at the end of it is *asmi*, the first person singular of *atthi*, and the chapter's warning concerns this. Standing alone, *asmi* occurs once in the whole of the root texts. Joined, it is everywhere: this formula alone accounts for hundreds of occurrences, since *esohamasmi* and *nesohamasmi* are how the canon says "I am this" and "I am not this". Search a text for *asmi*, find nothing, and you have learnt a fact about how the editions set sandhi and nothing about the language.

    *Samanupassati* takes the last word. It is an ordinary third person singular present indicative on the stem *passa-*, and DPD gives its root as *√dis*. The stem does not announce the root, and need not: remove *-ti* and you have the stem, and the endings go on the stem. That is the chapter's thesis in one word, a word that occurs several hundred times in the Nikāyas.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Handa dāni, bhikkhave, āmantayāmi vo, vayadhammā saṅkhārā appamādena sampādethā'ti. (DN 16 — D ii 156)

    The two stems are *āmantaya-* and *sampāde-*, and they are the same class in its two shapes. The chapter's table gives the eighth class the affix *-e-*, *-aya-*, with *deseti* as its example; DPD's constructions are *ā* + *mantaya* + *ti* and *saṁ* + *pāde* + *ti*, so the Buddha's last sentence happens to carry one of each. Taught the class by *deseti* alone, you will not recognise the first of them as belonging to it.

    *Āmantayāmi* also shows the lengthening rule twice over if you look: the stem ends in a short *-a*, and before *-mi* it lengthens, as *gaccha-* gives *gacchāmi*. *Sampādetha* is the second person plural imperative, and its ending *-tha* is one of the three the chapter flags as shared with the present indicative, so the form is, on its own, equally "you strive on" and "strive on!", and only the setting decides. Here the setting is a deathbed and there is no doubt at all.

    No pronoun is needed because *-tha* has already said 'you'. *Bhikkhave* is a vocative and is not the subject of anything; it names the people addressed, and the *-tha* marks the subject. The *vo* two words earlier is the object of *āmantayāmi*, not the subject of *sampādetha*, and if you take it for one, the second clause loses its verb's agreement.

6. Attanā hi kataṁ pāpaṁ, attanā saṅkilissati; Attanā akataṁ pāpaṁ, attanāva visujjhati; Suddhī asuddhi paccattaṁ, nāñño aññaṁ visodhaye. (Dhp 165 — Dhp p. 24)

    ***Visujjhati* and *visodhaye* are one verb twice.** DPD gives *visujjhati* as *vi* + *sujjha* + *ti* and *visodhayati* as *vi* + *sodhaya* + *ti*, both from *√sudh*, and calls the second the causative of the first. So the verse says "one is purified" and then "no one purifies another", and the difference between being purified and purifying is carried by one vowel.

    That vowel is the chapter's gradation table. The root vowel is *u*; the *guṇa* grade of *u* is *o*; so *sujjh-* is the root untouched and *sodh-* is the root raised, the same operation that turns *bhū-* into *bhavati* and *jut-* into *jotati*. The doubled *-jjh-* of the first is a second mechanism on top of it, the assimilated remains of the *-ya-* class affix, so the plain verb belongs to the third class and its causative to the eighth.

    *Visodhaye* is then the contracted optative in *-e*, the same ending as *ghātaye* in item 2 and on the same kind of stem. Two verses in this set end on that form, and in both of them it is the form a reader is likeliest to misparse.

    Two smaller things. *Saṅkilissati* is a passive-looking present of the kind item 16 is about, and the editions do not spell it alike: the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society all print *saṁkilissati* with the niggahīta, where this book writes *saṅkilissati* with the velar nasal, as Chapter 2 teaches a niggahīta becomes before a velar. That is the ṅ/ṁ variation of Chapter 1's box, in a key word of the verse, and the book's spelling is a convention, not a report of any one edition. And *paccattaṁ* is an indeclinable adverb, not a case form, which DPD registers as the frozen accusative singular of *paccatta*: the same freezing the numerals chapter found in *dasāhaparamaṁ*.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Identify the form.**

7. *suṇāma* — from *suṇāti* 'hears', stem *suṇā-*. First person plural, present indicative, active. The stem belongs to the class that inserts *-ṇā-* between root and ending (*su-* + *-ṇā-*), so the stem ends in a long vowel, not in *-a*. Note that the stem vowel is already long, so the lengthening before *-ma* has nothing to do; compare *gacchāma*, where it does.

8. *karonti* — from *karoti* 'does, makes', stem *karo-*. Third person plural, present indicative, active. This is the small *-o-* class, and *karoti* is its most irregular member as well as much its most frequent. Item 3 has the same root wearing a different stem.

9. *gacchāhi* — from *gacchati* 'goes', stem *gaccha-*. Second person singular, **imperative**, active: 'go!'. The ending is *-hi*, and the stem vowel has lengthened before it by the same rule that gives *gacchāmi*. The bare stem *gaccha* is an equally ordinary form of the same command.

10. *jāneyyuṁ* — from *jānāti* 'knows', stem *jānā-*. Third person plural, **optative**, active: 'they might know', 'they should know'. The *-eyy-* is the mark of the mood, and *-uṁ* the third plural. Any form carrying *-eyy-* is an optative; the mood has no other disguise, though it does have the contraction of items 2 and 6.

11. *bhavantu* — from *bhavati* 'is, becomes', stem *bhava-*. Third person plural, **imperative**, active: 'let them be', 'may they be'. Nothing about the form says whether it is a command, a wish or a blessing; at Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 25 it is a blessing, and only the setting says so.

12. *yamāmase* — dictionary form *yamati*, stem *yama-*. First person plural, imperative, **middle** (*attanopada*). No other item in the group is not active, and the ending *-āmase* gives it away; it is also, characteristically, a verse form, and item 1 sets the whole verse in front of you. Beyond the parsing, two things about it matter. Its *sense* is disputed (Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi take it as restraining oneself, the commentaries and DPD as dying), and the chapter's footnote sets out who says what. And the dictionary form *yamati* does not itself occur in the root texts: the canon has this verb only as *yamāmase* and in the compound *saṁyamāmase*. A headword does not promise that you will meet the word in that form.

**Resolve the ambiguity.**

13. *gacchāma* is **either** first person plural present indicative, 'we go', **or** first person plural imperative, 'let us go'. The two are identical, and they are identical in every verb, because the imperative's first-person endings are simply the present's. Only the sentence decides, and the usual cues are a following particle of exhortation, an imperative in a neighbouring clause, or a context in which a statement of present fact would make no sense. This overlap covers three cells of the paradigm (first singular, first plural and second plural) and is no defect in the tables.

14. *desetha* is likewise **either** second person plural present indicative, 'you teach', **or** second person plural imperative, 'teach!'. Item 5's *sampādetha* is the third cell of the same overlap on a stem of the same class, resolved by a deathbed. The added difficulty here is the stem: *dese-* is the *-e-* class, and if you take the *-e-* for the mark of an optative you will look for a mood that is not there. An optative of this verb carries the mood-marker visibly (*deseyya* 'he might teach', *deseyyaṁ* 'I might teach'), and neither could be mistaken for the form above.

15. *siyā* is third person singular **optative** of *atthi* 'is', 'it would be', 'it might be', not a present. It belongs to the *-yā* type of optative, not the *-eyya* type, so it does not look like one, and it is among the commonest verb forms in the canon because so much of the Buddha's conditional and hypothetical speech runs through it. Its rival *assa* is the same mood of the same verb, formed the ordinary way. Only knowledge of the paradigm decides the reading: *atthi* has no present in *si-* at all.

16. *vuccati* looks like an ordinary third person singular active present, and it is 'is called', a **passive**. The form makes the point the chapter's voice section exists for: the active-looking ending settles nothing, because active against passive is carried by the **stem**, not by the endings. The stem here is a passive one built with *-ya-* on the root *vac-* 'say', assimilated to *-cc-*. Only knowledge of the verb, or an agent in the instrumental case, shows which voice is in front of you. *Saṅkilissati* and *visujjhati* in item 6 are the same shape and the same problem. The passive stem is Chapter 9's subject.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Read the verse.**

17. **Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1.** *mano·pubbaṅgamā* preceded by mind (m. nom. pl.) · *dhammā* things, mental states (m. nom. pl.) · *mano·seṭṭhā* having mind as chief (m. nom. pl.) · *mano·mayā* made of mind (m. nom. pl.) · *manasā* with a mind (n. inst. sg.) · *ce* if · *paduṭṭhena* corrupted (n. inst. sg.) · *bhāsati* speaks (3 sg. pres.) · *vā* or · *karoti* acts (3 sg. pres.) · *tato* thereafter · *naṁ* him (acc. sg.) · *dukkham·anveti* < *dukkhaṁ* suffering (n. nom. sg.) + *anveti* follows (3 sg. pres.) · *cakkaṁ·va* < *cakkaṁ* the wheel (n. nom. sg.) + *iva* like · *vahato* of the one drawing, of the draught animal (m. gen. sg., present participle) · *padaṁ* the foot (n. acc. sg.)

    "Mind precedes all things; mind is their chief, they are made of mind. If with a corrupted mind one speaks or acts, suffering follows one from that — as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws it."

    All three finite verbs are present indicative and all three are timeless: this is the general-truth use, not a report of anything happening. *Bhāsati* and *karoti* sit in a conditional clause marked by *ce* and take the indicative, not the optative. *Ce* does not require the optative, and a conditional whose condition is stated as a fact keeps the indicative. Look at *anveti*: its stem is *e-*, from the root *i-* 'go' with the prefix *anu-* (*anu* > *anv* + *e* + *ti*), so it belongs to the long-vowel conjugation and not to any class with a visible affix. Two sandhis are resolved in the gloss and neither is optional in the printed line.

18. **Dhp 5 — Dhp p. 1.** *na* not · *hi* indeed, for · *verena* by hatred (n. inst. sg.) · *verāni* hatreds (n. nom. pl.) · *sammant·īdha* < *sammanti* are appeased (3 pl. pres.) + *idha* here · *kudācanaṁ* ever · *averena* by non-hatred (n. inst. sg.) · *ca* and · *sammanti* are appeased (3 pl. pres.) · *esa* this (m. nom. sg.) · *dhammo* law, teaching (m. nom. sg.) · *sanantano* eternal, ancient (m. nom. sg.)

    "For never here are hatreds appeased by hatred; by non-hatred are they appeased. This is an eternal law."

    Both finite verbs are the same form, third person plural present indicative of *sammati* 'is appeased', and both are the general-truth use: the verse states what always holds, as *sanantano* then says outright. Item 1 has the third occurrence of the same form one verse later, with *medhagā*, not *verāni*, as its subject. Look again at *sammati* for its stem: DPD gives the construction as *samma-* + *-ti*, from the root *sam-*, the doubled consonant being the assimilated remains of a *-ya-* class affix. The verb is intransitive and is not a passive, though English needs a passive to render it.

19. **Dhp 167 — Dhp p. 25.** *hīnaṁ* low, inferior (m. acc. sg.) · *dhammaṁ* thing, practice (m. acc. sg.) · *na* not · *seveyya* should resort to (3 sg. opt.) · *pamādena* in negligence (m. inst. sg.) · *na* not · *saṁvase* should dwell (3 sg. opt.) · *micchā·diṭṭhiṁ* wrong view (f. acc. sg.) · *na siyā* should not be (3 sg. opt. of *atthi*) · *loka·vaḍḍhano* one who increases the world, one attached to the world (m. nom. sg.)

    "One should not resort to what is low; one should not dwell in negligence. One should not resort to wrong view; one should not be a perpetuator of the world."

    Three optatives, and for this chapter the verse matters because they take three different forms. *Seveyya* is the full *-eyya* ending. *Saṁvase* is the contracted *-e*, the same mood and the same person, from *saṁvasati* 'lives with', a form you will parse as a present indicative unless you know that the contraction exists. And *siyā* is the *-yā* type belonging to *atthi*. One verse, one mood, three endings, and no other mood in it.

    The sense is the prescriptive one: 'should', not 'might'. All four clauses are negated by *na*, and that is a different thing from the prohibition with *mā* in the next chapter: *na* + optative states a rule about what one ought not do, whereas *mā* forms a direct prohibition and takes the aorist. Both come out as 'don't' in English and they are not the same construction.

**One imperative, two numbers.**

20. *suṇantu* let them listen (3 pl. imper. of *suṇāti*) · *me* to me (1st sg. gen./dat., enclitic) · *āyasmantā* the venerables (m. nom. pl.)

    "Let the venerables listen to me."

    *Suṇantu* is the third person plural imperative, on the present stem *suṇā-* of *suṇāti* 'hears', with the ending *-antu*. Its subject is *āyasmantā*, nominative plural: the venerables are told to listen, and they are the grammatical subject of the verb even though in English the speaker seems to be the one making the request.

    *Me* is the enclitic of the first person, and its form does not show whether it is genitive or dative. Here the sense is dative, 'listen **to me**', but Chapter 5 records that the two have merged in the enclitics and that the ending will not decide it. *Suṇāti* decides it, which takes the person heard in the genitive or the dative and the thing heard in the accusative.

    Between this and *Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho* only the number changes. *Suṇātu* is third person singular with *saṅgho* as its subject, because the Sangha is one body; *suṇantu* is third person plural with *āyasmantā*, because a group of venerables is several people. The formula is otherwise identical, down to the enclitic. Which one a monk says depends on how many are in front of him, and the Vinaya prints both because the same act has to be performable before a Sangha, before a group, and before one person.

**Two verbs for 'is'.**

21. MN 10 — M i 57 uses *atthi* and asserts that something is there. "There are in this body head-hairs, body-hairs, nails, teeth, skin …" — the sentence introduces the parts of the body to a meditator who is about to review them, and what it claims is their presence. SN 5.6 — S i 132 uses *hoti* and asserts that one thing is another. "For one who is born there is death" — nothing is being said to exist; *maraṇaṁ* is being predicated of *jātassa*.

    Swap them and the first sentence becomes a claim that the body *is* its head-hairs and nails, which is not what the meditation says and is a doctrinal error besides; the second becomes a claim that death exists, which is true and is not the line's point. The chapter's rule holds: *hoti* says that A is B, *atthi* says that there is an A. The chapter is careful to add that the distinction is not absolute, and it is right to; these two lines are why it is nonetheless the way to bet.

    The number disagreement is in the first, and it is no error. *Atthi* is third person singular; *kesā lomā nakhā dantā taco* is a string of nominatives, four of them plural. The existential *atthi* standing at the head of a sentence regularly does not agree with what follows it: *atthi devā tāvatiṁsā*, 'there are the gods of the Thirty-Three' (DN 23 — D ii 327), is the same construction with a plural subject and a singular verb, and the negative *natthi* behaves identically in the stock list of wrong views. The chapter's paradigm does give a third plural, *santi*, and it is used, but where the verb is existential and stands first, the singular is ordinary and passes without remark. To correct it is to correct the canon.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

22. Four phrases, and the mood is the whole of the exercise:

    (a) ***bhāyanti maccuno*** (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19): third person plural, present indicative, active. The thing feared stands in the genitive, which English does not lead you to expect; *maccuno* is genitive singular of *maccu* 'death'.

    (b) ***nāññamaññassa dukkhamiccheyya*** (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26): third person **singular**, optative, active. The English has to say "none" or "no one", because Pāli leaves the subject unexpressed and the *-eyya* has already said which person it is. A plural here means you have heard the English, not the Pāli.

    (c) ***appamādena sampādetha*** (DN 16 — D ii 156): second person plural, imperative, active. Identical in form to the present indicative, and the only thing that makes it an imperative is that the Buddha is dying and is not describing anyone's habits.

    (d) ***suṇantu me āyasmantā*** (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 197): third person plural, imperative, active, with its subject expressed because the third person needs one. Compare (c), where the second person does not.

    If (b) and (c) both came out, the two moods have separated. They are the two the chapter says are "syntactically often exchangeable", and the exercise is to produce the right form for the person you mean, not to tell their senses apart, which often nobody can.
