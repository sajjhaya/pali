## Answers: The Past and the Future

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Idamavoca bhagavā. Attamanā te bhikkhū bhagavato bhāsitaṁ abhinandunti. (MN 10 — M i 63)

    The first word is two words. DPD registers *idamavoca* as a sandhi compound and resolves it *idaṁ* + *avoca*, pronoun plus aorist: the junction item 22 meets in *etadavocuṁ*, where the older stem's *-d-* surfaces before a vowel. Here the pronoun is *idaṁ* and the *-m* is simply its own.

    The two present-tense verbs are *vadati* 'says' and *abhinandati* 'delights in, approves'. Neither aorist looks like its present, and they fail to for different reasons. *Avoca* is the *a*-aorist of *vadati*, which has three unrelated-looking stems and has to be learnt whole; it belongs in the chapter's irregular table. *Abhinanduṁ* is the ordinary aorist of an ordinary verb, and DPD gives the construction as *abhi* + √*nand* + *i*, which yields the singular *abhinandi*; the plural here takes *-uṁ* straight onto the root instead. That is the ending the chapter's tables give for types 1 and 2, not the *-iṁsu* of the type the singular belongs to. Whether that makes it a different type or one paradigm with mixed endings is the sort of question the chapter's section on the vanished tenses says matters little.

    This is the last sentence of most suttas in the canon, so it comes first here. Anyone who has listened to much Pāli has heard it hundreds of times, and it consists of two past tenses neither of which is formed the regular way.

2. Gahakāraka diṭṭhosi, puna gehaṁ na kāhasi; Sabbā te phāsukā bhaggā, gahakūṭaṁ visaṅkhataṁ; Visaṅkhāragataṁ cittaṁ, taṇhānaṁ khayamajjhagā. (Dhp 154 — Dhp p. 23)

    The present is *diṭṭhosi*, and it is hidden because it is joined on: DPD resolves it *diṭṭho* + *asi*, past participle plus the second person singular of *atthi*. Chapter 7's warning about the first person applies to the second as well: these forms are printed joined, and a search for *asi* on its own will badly under-count it.

    The future is *kāhasi*, and it has no *-ss-* in it at all. DPD gives it as the second person singular of *kāhati*, the future of *karoti*, from √*kar*; the chapter's footnote traces Perniola's chain at §80e, *karssati* > *kāssati* > *kāsati* > *kāhati*, the intervocalic sibilant weakening to *-h-*; only the endpoint is a form in the root texts. It is a verse form and a syllable shorter than *karissasi*, which largely explains why it exists.

    The past is *ajjhagā*, and you can see its augment, if nothing else of it. DPD's construction is *adhi* > *adhy* > *ajjh* + *a* + √*gam* + *ā*: the prefix *adhi-* palatalises to *ajjh-* before the vowel, and then the augment *a* stands between prefix and root where the chapter's rule puts it, followed by the bare *-ā* ending on the root. Compare *pāvisi* in item 23, where the same rule applies and the augment has vanished into a long vowel. One rule, two outcomes, and you can point at the augment in only one of them.

    DPD labels *ajjhagā* an **imperfect**, not an aorist. The chapter says of the imperfect that where one grammar labels a form so and another calls the same form an aorist, "nothing hangs on which is right". This is that, in the most famous verse in the Dhammapada.

3. Māvamaññetha pāpassa, na mantaṁ āgamissati; Udabindunipātena, udakumbhopi pūrati; Bālo pūrati pāpassa, thokaṁ thokampi ācinaṁ. (Dhp 121 — Dhp p. 18)

    The prohibition is on an optative, not an aorist. *Māvamaññetha* is *mā* + *avamaññetha*, and *-etha* is the third person singular middle optative of Chapter 7's table; DPD gives the verb as *avamaññati* 'thinks lightly of, belittles', from √*man*, *ava* + *mañña* + *ti*. The chapter names this construction and says what it does: with the aorist, *mā* forbids a particular act (*mā bhāyi*, 'don't be afraid'); with the optative it softens towards advice. So the verse is a counsel, not an order, and a whole mood marks the difference.

    The same verb turned up in set 07 with a different prefix. *Ati-maññati* in the Mettasutta is 'to think in excess of', to despise; *ava-maññati* here is 'to think down on', to make light of. One root, two directions, and the prefix is the whole of the difference.

    The future is not the verse's own prediction. *Na mantaṁ āgamissati* is *na* + *maṁ* + *taṁ* + *āgamissati* (DPD registers *mantaṁ* as a sandhi of *maṁ* + *taṁ* and cites this line for it), "it will not come to me", and the edition's own apparatus corroborates the resolution, since the Sinhalese and Pī manuscripts print the two words unjoined. The *pāda* is the content of the thought being warned against, and that makes sense of the *mā* in the line before: the person addressed is the one making the prediction, and the verse denies it. A future stating what will not happen, quoted in order to be contradicted.

    *Ācinaṁ* at the end is a present participle of *ācināti*, not a finite verb, 'accumulates', masculine nominative singular, agreeing with *bālo*. Item 5 has two more of these, and they are the commonest thing in Pāli verse to mistake for a first person singular.

4. Etaṁ satiṁ adhiṭṭheyya, Brahmametaṁ vihāramidhamāhu. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

    The tense is the perfect, and *āhu* is one of a handful of forms left of it. DPD gives *āhu* as the third person plural of *āha*, from √*ah*, construction *a* + *aha* + *u*, and cites this very line. Perniola's judgement, which the chapter reports, is that in the canonical books the perfect amounts to *āha* in the second and third singular and *āhu*, *āhaṁsu* in the third plural, and to no other form that can be named.

    A reader will go looking for a present *āhati*, and there is none. That is the practical content of the whole section: *āha* is among the most frequent verb forms in the literature, it is built on no present stem, and an attempt to work back from it to a dictionary headword will fail. It has to be recognised, not derived. Item 25 has the singular *āha* doing ordinary work in a Vinaya narrative, printed joined to the word before it.

    *Adhiṭṭheyya* belongs to the previous chapter: DPD parses it as the third person singular optative of *adhiṭṭhāti*, *adhi* + √*ṭhā* + *eyya*. So the couplet holds a mood that is everywhere and a tense that has nearly gone, side by side, and the common one carries the instruction.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Anekajātisaṁsāraṁ, sandhāvissaṁ anibbisaṁ; Gahakāraṁ gavesanto, dukkhā jāti punappunaṁ. (Dhp 153 — Dhp p. 23)

    *Sandhāvissaṁ* is the aorist, with a conditional's ending. The chapter's conditional table gives *-(i)ssaṁ* for the first person singular, and there it is. But DPD parses the word as "aor 1st sg of *sandhāvi*, irreg form of *sandhāviṁ*", from √*dhāv*, and the regular first singular of this verb would be *sandhāviṁ*. So the form is a past, not a counterfactual, and its ending is the one most likely to suggest otherwise.

    *Anibbisaṁ* is not a finite verb at all. DPD gives it as the present participle of *na nibbisati*, 'not finding': masculine nominative singular, agreeing with the unexpressed 'I' of the sentence. It looks like a first person singular and is a participle; the word beside it looks like a conditional and is a first person singular. Two adjacent words, each looking like what the other is.

    *Gavesanto* in the next line settles it, because it is the same kind of word in its other form: the same present participle, masculine nominative singular, agreeing with the same unexpressed subject, and nobody could mistake *-nto* for a finite ending. One verse, one participle ending twice, spelled two ways.

    This is the verse before item 2 and the two are one utterance: the search, then the finding. Having rebuilt both, say them together.

6. Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu divā bhaṇḍaṁ passitvā nimittaṁ akāsi—"rattiṁ avaharissāmī"ti. So taṁ maññamāno attano bhaṇḍaṁ avahari. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 57)

    The verb that stands twice is *avaharati* 'steals', once as a future and once as an aorist. *Avaharissāmi* is the first person singular future, formed the wholly regular way (root, *-issā-*, primary ending), and it is what the monk says he will do. *Avahari* is the third person singular aorist, DPD's *ava* + √*har* + *i*, type 4, and it is what he did. The intention and the act, in the two tenses this chapter is about, forty words apart.

    What the difference costs him is the whole case. He marked goods by day meaning to take them at night; in the dark he took his own. The future says *taṁ*, 'that'; the aorist says *attano bhaṇḍaṁ*, 'his own goods'. There is no theft, because there is no owner to steal from, and the Vibhaṅga's rulings on the neighbouring cases depend on this: what the monk perceived against what was in fact there.

    *Akāsi* is the *s*-aorist of *karoti* and is item 7 of this set. Knowing only *karoti*, no rule will get you to it: the *-si* is the type 3 marker, the *kā-* is what is left of the root, and there is no present in the language it resembles. It is also, in this sentence, the ordinary word for making a mental note: *nimittaṁ akāsi*, 'made a sign'.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Name the type.**

7. *akāsi* — **type 3, the *s*-aorist**; third person singular; from *karoti* 'does, makes'. The *-si* is the mark, and it is also why this form is met early: *akāsi* is the ordinary way of saying 'he did' or 'he made' and it looks nothing like its present. Note that *karoti* also has a root aorist, *akā*, so the same verb appears in two of the four types. Item 6 has it in a sentence.

8. *upasaṅkamiṁsu* — **type 4, the *is*-aorist**; third person plural; from *upasaṅkamati* 'approaches', stem *upasaṅkama-*. The *-iṁsu* is the ending on the present stem with its final vowel gone, and this is the type to guess at when no other suggests itself. It is also the commonest single word in the canon's narrative formula for someone going to see the Buddha.

9. *agā* — **type 1, the root aorist**; third person singular; from *gacchati* 'goes', root *gā-*. Everything here is old: the augment, which the root aorist always takes, and the bare *-ā* ending straight on the root. The verb has an *s*-aorist too, *agamāsi*, and the two coexist. *Ajjhagā* in item 2 is the same ending on the same root behind a prefix.

10. *addasaṁ* — **type 2, the *a*-aorist**; first person singular; from *dassati* 'sees'. The root is *dṛś-*, and the form it has arrived at (*a-dras-s-at* with everything assimilated) leaves almost nothing of it visible. This is the type to expect surprises from.

11. *assosi* — **type 3, the *s*-aorist**; third person singular; from *suṇāti* 'hears'. Reading it needs a moment: the double *-ss-* is the augment *a-* meeting the root *su-* with the aorist *-s-*, not a stem in *-ss-*.

12. *avocumha* — **type 2, the *a*-aorist**; first person plural; from *vadati* 'says'. The verb has three unrelated-looking stems, one for each voice and tense system: present *vadati*, aorist *avoca*, passive *vuccati*. They must simply be learnt together, and in item 1 a reader has already met the third singular of the middle one several hundred times.

**Take the tense apart.**

13. *gamissanti* — **future**, third person plural, 'they will go'. Stem *gaccha-* from *gacchati*, but the future is built on the root *gam-*, not on the present stem: *gam* + *issa* + *nti*. Both *gamissati* and *gacchissati* occur; the first is much the commoner.

14. *lacchasi* — **future**, second person singular, 'you will get', from *labhati* 'gets'. The *-ss-* has met the *-bh* of the root and the pair have collapsed: *labh-ssa-ti* > *lacchati*. Nothing on the surface of the word says future; it has to be recognised.

15. *abhavissa* — **conditional**, third person singular, from *bhavati* 'is, becomes'. Three elements are stacked: the augment *a-*, the future stem *bhavissa-*, and a secondary ending. Future stem plus past marking is the form of a counterfactual: something that was going to be and was not. Item 5 is the form this one has to be told apart from.

16. *hehiti* — **future**, third person singular, 'it will be', from *bhavati*. The chain, which is Perniola's at §80e and not the canon's, runs *bhavissati* > *havissati* > *hessati* > *hehiti*, the intervocalic sibilant weakening to *-h-*; only the last two of the four occur in the canon. It is a verse form, and a syllable shorter than the form it replaces, which largely explains why it exists. *Kāhasi* in item 2 is the same weakening on the other of the two commonest verbs in the language.

17. *ñassati* — **future**, third person singular, 'he will know', from *jānāti* 'knows'. This one has a different base, not assimilation: the future is built straight on the root *ñā-*, not on the present stem *jānā-*. The regular *jānissati* also exists and means the same thing.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Decide what the form means.**

18. *bhāyi* is a **second person singular aorist** of *bhāyati* 'fears', and it is not a past tense. After *mā* the aorist forms a prohibition: 'do not fear'. The past sense is gone entirely, and the reason is historical: this is a survival of the Old Indic injunctive, a form that was never past and merely coincides with the aorist in Pāli. The line also shades towards a wish, 'may you not be afraid', and only the setting decides. Vakkali is dying when it is said to him. Item 3 is the same particle on the optative instead, and item 25 is the same particle on the aorist with the wish, not the prohibition, uppermost.

19. *bhavissanti* is a **third person plural future**, and no future is meant. The sense is inferential: 'these cannot be the footprints of a human being'. Doṇa is looking at the marks on the road and reasoning from them, and Pāli puts that reasoning in the future the way English puts it in 'must'. The negated *na … bhavissati* is the frozen idiom for 'it cannot be'.

20. *ñassati* and *dakkhati* are both **third person singular futures**, of *jānāti* and *dassati*, and they are irregular in two different ways, the first built on the root, the second by assimilation. They express neither prediction nor inference but the ordinary future of a consequence: practise in this way, and this is what follows. Notice also that neither has an *-ss-* anywhere in it, and that is the whole difficulty of the section they come from.

    The editions disagree here. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *dakkhati* and records *dakkhiti* as the Sinhalese reading in its own apparatus, and the PTS has *dākkhīti*. The variation is in the ending, not the stem, and it is the same *-ati*/*-iti* alternation that gives *hessati* beside *hehiti*.

**Read the passage.**

21. *no* not · *ce* if · *idaṁ* this (n. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *paṇḍito* the astute person (m. nom. sg.) · *sucintitacintī* one who thinks well-thought thoughts (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *abhavissa* were to be (3 sg. cond.) · *subhāsitabhāsī* one who speaks well-spoken speech (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *sukatakammakārī* one who does well-done deeds (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *kena* by what (n. inst. sg.) · *naṁ* him (m. acc. sg.) · *paṇḍitā* the astute (m. nom. pl.) · *jāneyyuṁ* would know (3 pl. opt.)

    "If, monks, an astute person were not one who thinks well, speaks well and acts well, by what would the astute know him?"

    The **conditional** *abhavissa* stands in the protasis, the clause carrying the condition; the **optative** *jāneyyuṁ* stands in the apodosis, the clause carrying the consequence. That is the normal division of labour, and the forms themselves explain it. The conditional is a future seen from the past, something that was going to happen and did not, which is what a counterfactual condition asserts. The optative states what would follow, the mood's ordinary use. Either can appear on either side, and the conditional is rare enough that the optative usually fills both; this passage is kept because it shows the construction in its full form.

    The three long compounds are *bahubbīhi*s, treated in Chapter 13: each describes the person instead of naming a thing, 'one whose thinking is well thought'.

22. *atha kho* then · *te* those (m. nom. pl.) · *bhikkhū* monks (m. nom. pl.) · *sāvatthiyaṁ* in Sāvatthī (f. loc. sg.) · *piṇḍāya* for alms (m. dat. sg.) · *caritvā* having wandered (absol.) · *pacchābhattaṁ* after the meal · *piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā* returned from the almsround (m. nom. pl.) · *yena … tena* to where · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *upasaṅkamiṁsu* they approached (3 pl. aor.) · *upasaṅkamitvā* having approached (absol.) · *bhagavantaṁ* the Blessed One (m. acc. sg.) · *abhivādetvā* having paid homage (absol.) · *ekamantaṁ* to one side · *nisīdiṁsu* they sat down (3 pl. aor.) · *nisinnā* seated (m. nom. pl.) · *kho* indeed · *etadavocuṁ* < *etaṁ* this (n. acc. sg.) + *avocuṁ* they said (3 pl. aor.)

    "Then those monks, having wandered for alms in Sāvatthī and returned from the almsround after the meal, approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to him, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those monks said this to the Blessed One."

    Two of the three are *is*-aorists (*upasaṅkamiṁsu* and *nisīdiṁsu*, both formed on the present stem with *-iṁsu*), and the third, *avocuṁ*, is an *a*-aorist of *vadati*. The mix is the lesson. The productive type accounts for the two ordinary verbs, and the one verb that does not take it is a high-frequency irregular that has kept its inherited form. So the working method for an unfamiliar past tense is: assume the *is*-aorist and take the present stem off, and if that fails, the verb is probably one of the two dozen commonest words in the language and has to be learnt whole.

    Three of the other verb forms here (*caritvā*, *upasaṅkamitvā*, *abhivādetvā*) are **absolutives**, which are not finite and carry no tense of their own; they belong to Chapter 11. They take their time from the finite verb they hang on, so the whole sentence reads as past.

    *Etadavocuṁ* is one printed word and two: *etaṁ* + *avocuṁ*, with the *-d-* of the old stem *etad* surfacing before the vowel. Unless you resolve that junction you will search for a verb *davocuṁ* and not find one. Item 1 closes the sutta with the singular of the same junction.

**Read the case law.**

23. *tena kho pana samayena* now at that time · *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *ajjhokāse* in the open air (m. loc. sg.) · *cīvaraṁ* robe (nt. acc. sg.) · *pattharitvā* having spread out (absol.) · *vihāraṁ* the dwelling (m. acc. sg.) · *pāvisi* entered (3 sg. aor.)

    "Now at that time a certain monk spread a robe out in the open air and went into his dwelling."

    The finite verb is *pāvisi*, an aorist of *pavisati* 'enters'. The augment is inside the long *ā*, and only that remains of it. The chapter's rule puts the augment between prefix and root, which would give *pa* + *a* + *vis*; and *a* + *a* is *ā*, so the augment and the prefix's own vowel have run together into a single long syllable at the front of the word. DPD's construction field says so (*pa* + *a* + √*vis* + *i*), and that is the last step a reader cannot take by eye, because a lengthened *pa-* and a *pa-* with an augment behind it look identical. *Ajjhagā* in item 2 is the same rule with the augment left standing where you can see it.

    Set it beside the chapter's other note, that the augment is *often dropped* after a prefix ending in *-a*, so *pahāsi* stands without one. The same environment has two outcomes, and the surface of the word does not distinguish them. A derivation is a second claim, and it is settled in a dictionary, not by looking.

24. *tena kho pana samayena* now at that time · *sambahulā* several (m. nom. pl.) · *bhikkhū* monks (m. nom. pl.) · *gijjhakūṭā pabbatā* from the Vulture Peak mountain (m. abl. sg.) · *orohantā* coming down (m. nom. pl. pres. part.) · *sīhavighāsaṁ* a lion's leavings (m. acc. sg., *sīha* + *vighāsa*) · *passitvā* having seen (absol.) · *pacāpetvā* having had it cooked (absol. of the causative *pacāpeti*) · *paribhuñjiṁsu* they ate (3 pl. aor.)

    "Now at that time several monks, coming down from the Vulture Peak mountain, saw a lion's leavings, had them cooked, and ate them."

    Only *paribhuñjiṁsu* is finite: a type 4 *is*-aorist, third person plural, on the present stem of *paribhuñjati*. The other three are not: *orohantā* is a present participle agreeing with *bhikkhū*, and *passitvā* and *pacāpetvā* are absolutives. None of the three carries a tense of its own, and all three take their time from the one finite verb at the end. That is the ordinary pattern of Pāli narrative, and so the last word of a sentence is often the first one to look for.

    *Pacāpetvā* is the absolutive of *pacāpeti*, which DPD gives as the causative of *pacati* 'cooks': 'makes cook, causes to cook'. The monks did not cook; they had the meat cooked. Chapter 9 sets out the *-āpe-* causative and cites the rule against digging, where *khaṇati* stands beside *khaṇāpeti* in the same pair. The distinction matters: what a monk may do himself and what he may have done for him are different questions in the Vinaya, and here the whole of it rests on four letters in the middle of an absolutive.

**What happened next.**

25. *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *mā* let not · *ayaṁ* this (m. nom. sg.) · *patto* bowl (m. nom. sg.) · *nassi* be lost (3 sg. aor.) · *ti* (quotation) · *paṭisāmesi* put it away (3 sg. aor.) · *so* he (m. nom. sg.) · *nikkhamitvā* having come out (absol.) · *taṁ bhikkhuṁ* that monk (m. acc. sg.) · *pucchi* asked (3 sg. aor.) · *āvuso* friend (m. voc. sg.) · *mayhaṁ* my (1 sg. gen.) · *patto* bowl (m. nom. sg.) · *kena* by whom (m. inst. sg.) · *avahaṭo* taken away (m. nom. sg., p.p.) · *evam·āha* < *evaṁ* thus + *āha* said (3 sg. perf.) · *mayā* by me (1 sg. inst.) · *taṁ* him (m. acc. sg.) · *ādiyi* seized (3 sg. aor.) · *a·samaṇo·si* < *a-* not + *samaṇo* an ascetic (m. nom. sg.) + *asi* you are (2 sg. pres.) · *tvaṁ* you (nom. sg.)

    "A certain monk, thinking 'may this bowl not be lost', put it away. The other came out and asked that monk: 'Friend, by whom was my bowl taken?' He said: 'It was taken by me.' He seized him: 'You are no ascetic!'"

    Three aorists of three types, and one form that is not an aorist at all. *Paṭisāmesi* is type 3, an *s*-aorist on an *-e-* stem (DPD gives *paṭisāmeti* as the causative of *paṭisamati*, *pati* + *sāme* + *ti*), so it is formed like the *uppādesi* and *cāvesi* of the chapter's four-case frame, and shares their ambiguity with a second person singular present. *Pucchi* and *ādiyi* are type 4, *is*-aorists in *-i*, the productive type; DPD gives *ādiyi* from *ādiyati*, √*ādā*, and glosses it both 'took, grabbed' and 'stole', and the sentence depends on that.

    The fourth is *āha*, the perfect of item 4, printed joined as *evamāha*: DPD's sandhi entry gives *evaṁ* + *āha*, indeclinable plus perfect. Here the form has its ordinary use, the Vibhaṅga narrative's 'he said', and *evamāha* has 423 root-text occurrences. Item 4 had the plural in a verse; this is the singular in the plainest prose in the canon.

    ***Mā* here governs an aorist, and the sense is a wish. *Nassi* is the third person singular aorist of *nassati* 'perishes, is lost' — DPD's *nassa* + *i*, from √*nas* — so the construction is exactly item 18's, *mā* plus the aorist with its past sense gone. But nobody is being forbidden anything: the monk is not addressing the bowl, and there is no second person in the clause at all. What he is doing is wishing, and the chapter says the construction shades that way — "*mā bhāyi* is as much 'may you not be afraid' as 'don't be afraid'". Here there is no second reading available**, which makes this the cleaner instance of the two.

    The case ends a little later in an acquittal: *anāpatti, bhikkhu, niruttipathe*, "there is no offence, monk, in a manner of speaking" (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 57). The monk who said "it was taken by me" meant that he had picked it up, and *avaharati* is the Vinaya's word for theft; he used the word and not the intention, and the Buddha declines to convict on a word. The Vibhaṅga then runs the same situation once more with a nun in place of the monk (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 57), which is how the case law usually proceeds.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

26. Four phrases, and the tense is the whole of the exercise:

    (a) ***na kāhasi*** (Dhp 154 — Dhp p. 23): second person singular future of *karoti*, in its contracted *-h-* form. *Na karissasi* is correct Pāli and loses the verse's metre, which is all that is wrong with it.

    (b) ***idhamāhu*** (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26): third person plural perfect of *āha*, with *idha* and the junction consonant in front of it. If it came out at all, the rarest tense in the language is in your mouth.

    (c) ***rattiṁ avaharissāmi*** (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 57): first person singular future of *avaharati*, regular in every part: root, *-issā-*, primary ending. Of the four, only this form can be built instead of remembered.

    (d) ***avahari*** (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 57): third person singular aorist of the same verb, type 4. Set (c) and (d) side by side and the whole chapter is in the two of them: same root, same prefix, one syllable of difference, and between them the difference between a plan and an offence.
