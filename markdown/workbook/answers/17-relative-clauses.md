## Answers: Relative Clauses

Six items carry a trap: **15**, **16**, **17**, **19**, **20** and **27**. Each is named where it falls.

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Atha kho bhagavā yena dvārena nikkhami, taṁ gotamadvāraṁ nāma ahosi. (DN 16 — D ii 89)

    *Taṁ*, the nominative subject of *ahosi*. The pair is *yena … taṁ*: same word, same gender and number, different cases, because the gate is what one leaves *by* in the first clause and what *is named* in the second.

    The two halves of the rule divide the labour cleanly. Gender and number come from the antecedent, and both words point at the same gate, so both are neuter singular and there the sameness ends. Case comes from each word's function where it stands: instrumental in the relative clause, because the going out was by that gate; nominative in the main clause, because there the gate is the subject. Sameness is the antecedent's doing and difference is the clause's.

2. Yo, bhikkhave, rāgakkhayo dosakkhayo mohakkhayo — idaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, asaṅkhataṁ. (SN 43.12 — S iv 362)

    *Idaṁ*. It is not from the *ta-* stem at all, and that is ordinary, not irregular: any demonstrative can answer.

    *Yo* is masculine singular from *rāgakkhayo*, the first of the three nouns it opens; *idaṁ* is neuter singular from *asaṅkhataṁ*, the word it is equated with in the main clause. Each half agrees with its own clause's noun and not with the other half, which is the general rule that the fixed pair *yo … so* conceals by usually satisfying it twice over. Item 4 is the same fact again with the genders the other way round.

3. Yāni kānici, bhikkhave, bhayāni uppajjanti sabbāni tāni bālato uppajjanti, no paṇḍitato. (AN 3.1 — A i 101)

    *Sabbāni tāni*. The generalised relative *yāni kānici* is closed by *sabba* with the demonstrative attached to it, which is the standard completion for a generalisation.

    The two additions are ***kānici*** on the relative and ***sabbāni*** on the correlative: the first turns 'which dangers' into 'whatever dangers', the second turns 'those' into 'all those'. A generalised relative needs its answer generalised because the correlative states the scope of the claim. *Yāni kānici … tāni* would leave the main clause saying something about some dangers, not about every one, and the sutta insists that there is no exception.

4. **Yā** kho, bhikkhave, imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ saṅgati sannipāto samavāyo, **ayaṁ** vuccati cakkhusamphasso. (SN 35.93 — S iv 68)

    *Yā* takes feminine singular from *saṅgati*, the **first** of the three nouns in the series, even though *sannipāto* and *samavāyo* after it are masculine; it takes nominative from being the subject of its own clause. *Ayaṁ* takes masculine singular from *cakkhusamphasso* in the main clause, and nominative from being the subject there. The two pronouns refer to the same thing and differ in gender, and the rule has been obeyed in both.

    **Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    Generalisation. *Yaṁ kiñci … sabbaṁ taṁ*, "whatever … all that". There is no finite verb in the sentence; the whole content sits in the relation between the two halves.

    In place of a verb, the pairing itself holds it together: two nominatives, one opened by the relative and one closed by the correlative, and the relation between them is the predication. It is item 3's construction, *kiñci* generalising the relative and *sabbaṁ* generalising the answer, in the singular instead of the plural and with the verb dropped as well. The two sentences are built alike, and a reader is more likely to have the shorter by heart already.

6. Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 57)

    Manner, generalised by doubling. *Yathā yathā … tathā tathā*, "in whatever way … in just that way". The doubling matters, for the instruction is that the knowing tracks the posture whatever the posture is.

7. Yasmā ca kho, ānanda, suññaṁ attena vā attaniyena vā tasmā suñño lokoti vuccati. (SN 35.85 — S iv 54)

    Cause. *Yasmā … tasmā*, "because … therefore". Note that the reason clause comes first and the conclusion second, which is the ordinary order and the reverse of the English habit of stating the claim and then defending it.

    **Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

    **Recall.**

8. Number and gender from the antecedent; case from its function in its own clause. The first half identifies what the pronoun points at, and nothing else lets a reader find the antecedent when it stands several words away, or in the clause on the other side. The case half says nothing about the antecedent at all; it gives the relative's function where it stands.

9. "Of undetermined meaning": undetermined, that is, until the correlative determines it. It is the better name because it says what the word *does*, not what it is related to: it opens a term and leaves it open, and the sentence is not readable until something closes it. "Relative" names a relation; *aniyamattha* names the state the reader is left in.

10. Both facts have one cause. *Yad-* is the stem with its old final *d*, and that *d* only survives where a following vowel gives it somewhere to attach. So it is a real element of the paradigm and never a free word: the corpus has *yadidaṁ*, *yadā*, *yadeva*, *yadaniccaṁ* and never *yad*. A junction form *should* return zero occurrences.

11. *Yaṁ* first, then *yathā*. It is a difficulty ranking because a relative that means "where" or "when" announces what kind of clause is coming and what to look for; a relative that means nothing shows only that a clause has begun and leaves the relation to be reconstructed. The emptiest word is the hardest, and it is much the commonest.

12. *Paccattavacane* nominative, *upayogavacane* accusative, *karaṇavacane* instrumental, *bhummatthe* locative. Together they amount to the claim that the empty relative is never actually empty: it always stands for some case, the case is recoverable from the sentence, and recovering it is what construing the sentence consists of. That is considerably stronger than saying the ambiguity does not matter in translation.

13. *Yato* and *yasmā*. In the root texts *yato* leads 1,370 to 374; in the commentaries the ratio reverses to 3,317 for *yasmā* against 590, and in the sub-commentaries 1,605 against 327. *Yasmā … tasmā* is the exegetes' connective and *yato* is the canon's.

14. Any three of: a participle agreeing with the noun; a *tappurisa* compound; a *kammadhāraya* compound; a *bahubbīhi* compound.

**Find the answering word.**

15. **Trap.** There is no answering word, and there is no relative pronoun either: the trap is that the missing word is the *relative*, not the correlative. *Bhikkhu* serves at once as the complement of *vuccati* in the main clause and as the subject of *aggahesi* in the subordinate one, and the two are simply set down side by side. Wijesekera §18.d records the construction and supplies the missing *yo* in brackets in his own rendering. English cannot do it without one.

16. **Trap.** Nothing answers *yāvakīvañca*. The expected *tāva* is absent, and the main clause, *vuddhiyeva … pāṭikaṅkhā*, simply states the consequence. The construction is complete as it stands; the correlative is optional and is very often dropped. Note also *bhavissanti*: a future in a sentence that is not about the future, which item 39 returns to.

17. **Trap.** This is *yena* the connective, in the sense "whereby, in that", and **not** the *yena … tena* idiom of motion taught in the chapter on case syntax. Two signs show it: there is no verb of motion, and the Buddha's reply at AN 4.80 — A ii 83 closes the sentence with *yena* again, not with *tena*, so the word attaches a clause to *hetu* and *paccayo*, not marking a destination.

**What agrees with what.**

18. *Yo* takes masculine singular from *satto* and nominative from being the subject of *upapanno*. *Tassa* takes masculine singular from the same being, and **genitive** because of *tassa evaṁ hoti*, "to him it occurs", the idiom that puts the one who thinks in the genitive or dative. *Yo so* together is the emphatic combination, "that very being who".

19. **Trap.** *Yāvatakaṁ* is a relative **adjective**, not a pronoun, so the item is not asking about the *ya-* paradigm at all. *Taṁ* is neuter singular agreeing with *yāvatakaṁ* and with the neuter *pattabbaṁ*; it is nominative because the main clause is passive (*anuppattaṁ taṁ mayā*, "that has been attained by me"), so the thing attained is the grammatical subject and the attainer stands in the instrumental.

20. **Trap.** The concord rule is broken here, and nowhere else in the chapter. *Vinicchayā* is picked up by neuter plural *yāni* in one line and by masculine plural *te* in the next. Oberlies's explanation is that the masculine and neuter nominative and accusative plural endings had already fallen together through dialect mixture, so the distinction the rule depends on was not being maintained. DPD shows the same thing lexically: it carries *vinicchaya* as masculine at this verse and as neuter at Snp 4.12. Do not emend.

21. *Yā* takes feminine plural from *kulitthiyo kulakumāriyo* and nominative from being the subject of its own clause; *tā*, the second one, takes feminine plural from the same nouns and is the object of *vāsessanti*: accusative, identical in form to the nominative in this declension, so the sentence needs reading, not pattern-matching. The first *tā*, standing immediately after *yā*, is the emphatic combination of the kind seen at item 18.

Supply the case of the empty relative.

22. Locative. *Yaṁ vipassī* = *yasmiṁ kappe vipassī*, "in which eon Vipassī [arose]". This is Buddhaghosa's resolution, and it makes the sentence cohere: *ekanavutikappe* is the antecedent, not a floating adverbial, and the *yaṁ* clause says which eon it was.

23. Instrumental, on Buddhaghosa's reading: *kāraṇatthe paccattaṁ*, glossed *yena kāraṇena*, "by which reason". Resolved: *aṭṭhānaṁ etaṁ … yena kāraṇena ekissā lokadhātuyā dve arahanto … uppajjeyyuṁ*. Wijesekera reads it instead as an adverbial accusative; see item 31.

24. Accusative. The *yaṁ* clause is the content of the demonstrative accusative *etaṁ*: "this is your misdeed, namely that you did not entreat the Realised One". Resolved, the *etaṁ* and the *yaṁ* are the same thing named twice, once forward and once in full.

25. Locative again, and here the sentence supplies the antecedent itself: *so samayo yaṁ mahāvātā vāyanti* = *yasmiṁ samaye mahāvātā vāyanti*, "that is the time at which the great winds blow". This is the construction from which the *ekaṁ samayaṁ* of every sutta opening descends.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Name the clause and its sense.**

26. Time, shading into cause: *yato … atha*, "when, once … then". The answering word is *atha*, not *tato*, as is common with *yato*.

27. **Trap.** *Kittāvatā* is **interrogative**, not relative, and it opens no subordinate clause: this is a question, and it is the main clause. It belongs beside the others because the reply at 1.4 answers it with *yasmā … tasmā*, so the three shapes of one stem stand within two segments of each other: *kittāvatā* asking, *yasmā* subordinating, *tasmā* concluding.

**What the counts license.**

28. He is right about the root texts, and the margin is wide: *yato* 1,370, *yasmā* 374. What the counts add is that the claim does not hold of Pāli generally: in the commentaries *yasmā* leads 3,317 to 590 and in the sub-commentaries 1,605 to 327. So "rare" is not sayable without naming a layer, and Warder noticed because he was reading the Dīgha. It also explains how other grammars come to teach *yasmā … tasmā* as the ordinary causal pair: a grammar written from commentarial Pāli is describing the language it read.

29. *Yāvatak-* 117 root-text occurrences and *tāvatak-* 78; *yattak-* 12 and *tattak-* 15, of which four are *tatta-* "heated" in a compound and not the pronoun at all, leaving eleven. Learn *yāvataka*/*tāvataka* and learn *yādisa*/*tādisa*; recognise *yattaka* if it appears and do not spend time on it. None of the four ever occurs in its bare stem form, so the counts had to be taken on *yāvatak-*, not on *yāvataka*: the form a word is looked up in belongs to the grammar, and not to the text.

30. Seven occurrences, all in the later literature, and none in the root texts, commentaries or sub-commentaries. Six of the seven are declension runs in Kaccāyana, Padarūpasiddhi and the *Saddanīti*, grammars listing a paradigm, and the seventh is a verse in the Thūpavaṁsa, a mediaeval chronicle. That licenses you to say it is a grammarians' cell, completed by rule and used by nobody, the verdict Oberlies reaches by hand for *amuyo* and *amūsānaṁ*. It does not license you to say the ending is unattested: *katamesānaṁ* stands twelve times in the root texts, and *sabbesānaṁ* and *etesānaṁ* once each. What is unattested is this stem carrying that ending.

**Adjudicate.**

31. Buddhaghosa reads the *yaṁ* as a nominative used in the sense of an instrumental (*kāraṇatthe paccattaṁ*) and glosses it *yena kāraṇena*, "by which reason", which fits the sense of the sentence well: it is impossible that two Buddhas arise at once, and the *yaṁ* clause states the thing that is impossible. Wijesekera holds that it is instead an adverbial accusative on the Sanskrit model, and adduces sentences where the *yaṁ* clause is clearly the content of a demonstrative accusative: *tuyhevetaṁ dukkaṭaṁ … yaṁ tvaṁ … na tathāgataṁ yāci*, DN 16. Follow Wijesekera, for economy, not authority: his analysis accounts for all four of Buddhaghosa's uses by one mechanism, where Buddhaghosa needs a separate case-substitution for the third. Both give the same translation, so counting cannot settle it: the disagreement is about the grammar behind an agreed reading.

32. The canon has ***yadaniccaṁ*** solid, everywhere the formula occurs, so *Yad 'aniccaṁ* is a form no text prints and does not belong on a quotation line. The impulse is a teacher's and is sound: the reader does need to see that the word is *yad* + *aniccaṁ*, and that the *d* is the stem's old final surfacing before a vowel. The resolution belongs on the gloss line, which exists for it.

33. The sentence does not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; it is Perniola's own construction. That shows the *illustration* is invented, which is his habit, and every form taken from him is checked against the canon first for that reason. It shows **nothing** about the construction: the *kammadhāraya* used attributively is ordinary Pāli, and the right response is to replace his sentence with a canonical one, not to doubt the category. An unattested example is evidence about a grammarian, not about a language.

**Read the sentence.**

34. Literal: "Easy are the things not good and unbeneficial to oneself; but what is beneficial and good, that is supremely hard to do." Idiomatic: "It is easy to do what is bad and harmful to yourself; what is good and beneficial is the hardest thing of all." The relative works like an English cleft: *yaṁ … taṁ* isolates the thing being talked about and holds it up for the predicate that follows, which in English needs "what … is" or "it is … that".

35. Literal: "Of whom the utter unvirtuousness, spread over like a *māluvā* creeper over a sal tree, he does to himself what an enemy wishes for him." Idiomatic: "One whose immorality overspreads him as a creeper overspreads a sal tree does to himself what an enemy would wish." Two relatives, nested: *yassa … so* carries the whole verse, and inside the main clause a second pair, *tathā … yathā*, is inverted so that the demonstrative comes first: *tathattānaṁ* is *tathā* + *attānaṁ*. The verse also has no finite verb in its first half, the participle *otthataṁ* standing where one would be.

The relative with nothing to hold on to.

36. "Whoever should rub, there is an offence of wrong-doing."

    *Yo* agrees with nothing, and nothing answers it. There is no noun in the relative clause for it to modify and no *so* in the main clause to close it: the main clause is *āpatti dukkaṭassa*, 'an offence of wrong-doing', which has no verb either and names an offence, not a person. So the rule that governs every other relative in this chapter, that the pronoun takes its gender and number from its antecedent, has nothing here to work on.

    What is left is the masculine singular itself, and it carries the sense: a bare *yo* means 'whoever', and in the Khandhaka it means whichever monk. The reader supplies *bhikkhu* from the book they are holding, where the Pātimokkha's own rules supply it in words. This is the limiting case of the construction, a relative clause with no antecedent, no correlative, and no finite verb in the clause it depends on. It is set here because anyone who has learnt *yo … so* will look for three things and find none of them.

37. "Whoever should have it made, there is an offence of wrong-doing."

    *Kārāpeyya* is a causative, an optative of *kārāpeti* 'has made, causes to be made', where *ugghaṁseyya* is a plain optative of *ugghaṁseti* 'rubs up', an *-e-* stem of Chapter 7's eighth class with a causative's vowel but not a causative. The difference decides whom the rule catches: *yo kārāpeyya* is the one who arranges for the thing to be made, not the one who makes it. A causative in a rule moves the offence up a step, from the hand to the instruction. So the Vinaya reaches for one often, and Chapter 9's rule against digging pairs *khaṇati* with *khaṇāpeti* instead of stating either alone.

**The reading passage.**

38. The three run *ye te vajjīnaṁ vajjimahallakā, te sakkarissanti …*; *yā tā kulitthiyo kulakumāriyo, tā na … vāsessanti*; *yāni tāni vajjīnaṁ vajjicetiyāni …, tāni sakkaronti …*. In each, the outer relative is *yāvakīvañca* and the inner is the *ya-* pronoun doubled with its own demonstrative. The answering word in a different case is *tesañca*, genitive plural, in the first and third: *tesañca sotabbaṁ maññissanti*, "and they will think them worth listening to", and *tesañca … baliṁ no parihāpessanti*, "and they will not neglect the offerings to them". It is a **second** correlative to the same relative, in the case its own clause requires, which is the concord rule applied twice over to one antecedent.

39. Ānanda is reporting a present fact, that the Vajjians do these things, so the present is right. The Buddha is stating a rule about a particular case whose outcome is certain if the condition holds, and by the tense rule that takes the future in **both** clauses: *sakkarissanti* in the condition and *pāṭikaṅkhā* with a future sense in the consequence. "As long as they honour" leaves out that certainty: the English present reads as a general truth, and the Pāli future makes a commitment about this case.

40. The main clause closes it: *vuddhiyeva, ānanda, vajjīnaṁ pāṭikaṅkhā, no parihāni*, "growth alone is to be expected for the Vajjians, not decline". The construction is not defective because the correlative is optional throughout the system: Warder records that the demonstratives are "fairly free, and quite frequently they are omitted altogether", and *tadā* after *yadā* is the clearest case. The omission is safe here because *yāvakīvañca* is unambiguous on its own: an extent clause needs no answering word to be recognised as one.
