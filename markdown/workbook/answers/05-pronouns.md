## Answers: Pronouns

**Sotānugata — met by ear.**

1. Tvaṁ, samma medakathālike, mamaṁ rakkha, ahaṁ taṁ rakkhissāmi. (SN 47.19 — S v 168)

    First person: *mamaṁ* and *ahaṁ*. Second person: *tvaṁ* and *taṁ*. They stand in the sentence as *you, me, I, you*, so the line is easier to hold as a rhythm than as a list.

    The two accusatives look least like their own nominatives, and that is a fact about both paradigms, not an accident of this sentence. *Ahaṁ* gives *maṁ* or *mamaṁ*; *tvaṁ* gives *taṁ*, *tvaṁ* or *tuvaṁ*. Read the two tables down their columns and the pattern is plain: the nominative singular shares nothing with the obliques beside it, the singular obliques run on *ma-* and *ta-*, and the plural runs on *amha-* and *tumha-* throughout. Three stems to a paradigm, and only the nominative cannot be predicted from the rest.

    The acrobat is speaking to his assistant on the pole, and *taṁ* here is the difficulty the chapter opens with. It is the second person accusative singular, "you"; it is also the third person and demonstrative accusative singular, "him, her, it". Nothing in the form separates them. *Ahaṁ* settles it: a first-person speaker addressing someone directly, in a sentence whose other half is *tvaṁ … mamaṁ*, is not switching to a third party in the last two words.

2. Evaṁ me sutaṁ. (DN 1 — D i 1)

    ***Me* is instrumental**, first person singular, and the sentence is literally "thus [it was] heard by me". There is no finite verb in it at all: DPD parses *sutaṁ* as the past participle of *suṇāti*, and gives this very sentence as its illustration.

    The commentary declines to settle it. Buddhaghosa separates three senses in which *me* is met (instrumental, dative and genitive) and then says of this place that both "heard by me" and "my hearing" fit. So the standard English rendering takes a side the tradition does not. The enclitics' second property thus appears in the first sentence a reader ever meets: *me* has spread beyond the case range it had in Sanskrit, and an enclitic is often ambiguous by nature, not by accident.

    That the three commonest words in the canon contain an unsettled parse, and that most readers pass over them for years without noticing, is the reason this item is in stage 1 and not stage 4.

3. Ayaṁ te patto, ayaṁ saṅghāṭi, ayaṁ uttarāsaṅgo, ayaṁ antaravāsako. (Kd 1 — Vin i 94)

    ***Saṅghāṭi* is the odd one out: it is feminine**, and the other three are masculine. DPD gives *saṅghāṭi* as a feminine *i*-stem from *saṅghaṭeti*, 'joined together', and *patta*, *uttarāsaṅga* and *antaravāsaka* as masculine.

    The item is set for what this shows about *ayaṁ*. The nominative singular of *ima-* is *ayaṁ* in the masculine and *ayaṁ* in the feminine alike; only the neuter differs, as *idaṁ*. So this sentence agrees a single form with two genders four times over, and if you have learnt *ayaṁ* as "the masculine one" you will misgender the robe. The chapter's table and Appendix A.2 both print the two cells identically, which is easy to read past and hard to forget once a sentence has made use of it.

    *Te* is the second person enclitic, genitive or dative singular. It stands second in its clause, which is where an enclitic has to stand, and it is not repeated in the three clauses that follow: Pāli says "your" once and lets it govern the list.

4. Ko āpanno, kiṁ āpanno, kismiṁ āpanno, kathaṁ āpanno, kaṁ bhaṇatha, kiṁ bhaṇathā'ti? (Bu Pc 12 — Vin iv 35)

    *Kathaṁ* is not a pronoun. It is an interrogative adverb, 'how', the only word in the series that does not decline and does not stand for a noun. The chapter footnotes it for this reason: in a run of five interrogatives it is invisible unless someone points at it.

    *Kismiṁ* is built on the second stem. Beside *ka-* the interrogative has a stem *ki-*, and three oblique cases admit forms built on it: ablative *kismā*, genitive *kissa*, locative *kismiṁ* and *kimhi*. These are the same pronoun in its other stem, not a separate set to be learnt, and so item 11 below can be worked out instead of memorised.

    The passage is a monk under cross-examination, and the six questions run through subject, object, location, manner, and then object twice more. Three cases are represented: *ko* nominative, *kismiṁ* locative, and *kiṁ* and *kaṁ* accusative. Look at the two accusatives at the end: *kaṁ* is masculine and asks about a person, *kiṁ* is neuter and asks about a thing, and one letter makes the whole difference.

**Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.**

5. Nagaraṁ yathā paccantaṁ, guttaṁ santarabāhiraṁ; Evaṁ gopetha attānaṁ, khaṇo vo mā upaccagā. (Dhp 315 — Dhp p. 45)

    *Attānaṁ* is singular and *gopetha* is second person plural. This is neither a mismatch nor a licence of the verse, for the reflexive has no number of its own to contribute. *Attā* serves all three persons and both numbers, taking both from the verb, so English must render three words of Pāli, *evaṁ gopetha attānaṁ*, with a "yourselves" that Pāli never says. To make *attānaṁ* agree with *gopetha* is to look for a plural that does not exist.

    *Vo* could not have stood at the head of its clause. An enclitic is never the first word of its sentence, without exception, and that positional rule makes several of the ambiguities in this set decidable at a glance. Here *vo* is second, leaning on *khaṇo*, where an enclitic belongs.

    The verse is also the chapter's cleanest instance of the reflexive used plainly as a reflexive (the object of *gopetha* is the subject of *gopetha*), against the emphatic instrumental *attanā* of item 10, which is used differently.

6. Yo vo, ānanda, mayā dhammo ca vinayo ca desito paññatto, so vo mamaccayena satthā. (DN 16 — D ii 154)

    Five pronouns, four cases: ***yo*** and ***so*** nominative singular masculine, the relative and its correlative; ***mayā*** first person instrumental singular; ***mama*** first person genitive singular, inside the compound *mamaccayena*; and ***vo*** twice, the second person enclitic.

    What an English translation of *vo* throws away is the case. It is a dative, and Wijesekera's name for it is the *sympathetic dative*: the person with a stake in the action, not the person who owns a thing. So *so vo … satthā* means "the Teacher for you, in your interest, now that I am going", not "the Teacher belonging to you". English has only "your", which flattens a dative into a possessive and loses the whole sense of the case.

    And the sentence has both halves of the rule in one clause. The division Wijesekera supplies is that the dative is used with the first and second person pronouns and the genitive with everything else: here *vo* dative for the second person, *mama* genitive inside *mamaccayena*, ten words apart. The chapter records that neither source states both halves and that the two were put together for this book, so treat it as a well-attested tendency, not a rule without exceptions.

    If the sentence was rebuilt with one *vo* instead of two, that is the ordinary failure; repeat the sentence until it is not. The first *vo* belongs to the relative clause and the second to the main clause, and Pāli does not carry it across.

**Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.**

**Identify the form.**

7. *amūsaṁ* — stem *asu*/*amu-*, the remote demonstrative "that one yonder". Genitive/dative plural, and the same form serves all three genders. Note that the singular of this pronoun keeps traces of an older stem in the *ad-* forms, *aduṁ* and *adussa*, while the plural is built on *amu-* throughout, so the plural looks more regular than the singular.

8. *tuyhaṁ* — stem *tumha-*, the second person. Genitive or dative singular, "your, to you"; the second person does not inflect for gender. Its enclitic counterpart is *te*, which stands in the same two cases and several more besides — as item 3 above shows it doing.

9. *enaṁ* — stem *ena-*, the anaphoric. Accusative singular, all three genders, and the anaphoric has no other singular case and no nominative at all. Distinguish it from *eta-*, the proximal demonstrative it was built to: *enaṁ* points back at what has already been named, *etaṁ* points out at what is present.

10. *attanā* — the noun *attā* "self", serving as the reflexive. Instrumental singular, and also ablative singular; the person and number come from the verb, not from the pronoun, so this one form can mean "by myself", "by yourself", "by himself" or "by themselves". In the instrumental it very often carries the emphatic sense "personally, of one's own doing".

11. *kismā* — stem *ka-*/*ki-*, the interrogative. Ablative singular, masculine and neuter, "from what?". It is built on the second stem *ki-*, not *ka-*, the same alternation that gives *kissa* in the genitive and the *kismiṁ* heard in item 4.

**Resolve the ambiguity.**

12. *taṁ* — either the second person accusative singular, "you", of *tumha-*; or the third person and demonstrative accusative singular, "him, her, it, that", of *ta(d)*. Both are common and the form does not separate them. The person of the verb and of the other pronouns in the clause decides: a first-person verb with an *ahaṁ* beside it points to a second-person object, as in item 1.

13. *no* — either the first person plural enclitic, "us, our, by us"; or the negative particle *no*, "not". Position settles it, because an enclitic is never the first word of its sentence. A sentence-initial *no* is the negative; anywhere else, weigh the sense.

14. *assa* — either the genitive or dative singular of *ima-*/*ayaṁ*, "of this, to this"; or the third person singular optative of *atthi*, "it would be". Here the test is syntactic, not positional: as a pronoun it will have a noun or participle depending on it, and as a verb it will be the only finite verb in its clause.

**Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.**

**Read the sentence.**

15. Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbesaṁ jīvitaṁ piyaṁ. (Dhp 130 — Dhp p. 19)

    *sabbe* all (nom. pl. m.) · *tasanti* they fear · *daṇḍassa* the rod (gen.) · *sabbesaṁ* to all (gen./dat. pl. m.) · *jīvitaṁ* life · *piyaṁ* dear

    "All tremble at the rod; to all, life is dear."

    Both pronouns are the pronominal adjective *sabba*, and the verse displays two of the three diagnostics in one line: *sabbe* in the nominative plural masculine, where a noun would give *-ā*, and *sabbesaṁ* in the genitive plural, where a noun would give *-ānaṁ*. There is no personal or demonstrative pronoun in the verse at all, and none is needed: the verb carries its own subject.

16. Ko imaṁ pathaviṁ vicessati, yamalokañca imaṁ sadevakaṁ. (Dhp 44 — Dhp p. 7)

    *ko* who (nom. sg. m.) · *imaṁ* this (acc. sg. f.) · *pathaviṁ* earth · *vicessati* will discern · *yamalokañca* < *yamalokaṁ* the world of Yama + *ca* and · *imaṁ* this (acc. sg. m.) · *sadevakaṁ* with its gods

    "Who will discern this earth, and this world of Yama with its gods?"

    Two classes here. *Ko* is the interrogative, nominative singular masculine of *ka-*. *Imaṁ* is the proximal demonstrative, and it occurs twice in different genders, feminine with *pathaviṁ* and masculine with *yamalokaṁ*. The accusative singular of *ima-* is *imaṁ* in the masculine and the feminine alike, and only the noun it agrees with tells them apart. That is the same collapse item 3 found in the nominative, one case further along.

17. Tumhehi kiccamātappaṁ, akkhātāro tathāgatā. (Dhp 276 — Dhp p. 40)

    *tumhehi* by you (2nd instr. pl.) · *kiccamātappaṁ* < *kiccaṁ* to be done + *ātappaṁ* the effort · *akkhātāro* those who tell, proclaimers · *tathāgatā* the Realised Ones

    "The effort is to be made by you; the Realised Ones are those who tell of it."

    *Tumhehi* is the second person instrumental plural, the full form and not the enclitic *vo*. That matters, because the enclitic could not stand at the head of the sentence and the full form can. The instrumental names the agent with a gerundive, *kiccaṁ*: the effort is a thing to be done, and *tumhehi* names who must do it. The line places the pronoun first for that emphasis.

18. Tato naṁ dukkhamanveti, cakkaṁva vahato padaṁ. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    *tato* thereupon · *naṁ* him (acc. sg., any gender) · *dukkhamanveti* < *dukkhaṁ* suffering + *anveti* follows · *cakkaṁva* < *cakkaṁ* wheel + *va* like · *vahato* of the draught ox (gen. sg.) · *padaṁ* foot

    "Then suffering follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws it."

    *Naṁ* is the anaphoric, accusative singular; *tato* is the ablative singular of *ta(d)* used adverbially, "from that, thereupon", one of a whole family of adverbial uses the oblique cases of *ta(d)* have: *tasmā* "therefore", *tahiṁ* "there", *yena … tena* "where … there". The verse was quoted in the chapter, and it is set again here on purpose: it is the first verse of the Dhammapada, and most readers will have known it by heart for years without ever having parsed the two pronouns in it.

A demonstrative welded to a personal pronoun.

19. *imāhaṁ* < *imaṁ* this (nt. acc. sg.) + *ahaṁ* I (nom. sg.) · *saṅghassa* to the Sangha (m. dat. sg.) · *nissajjāmi* I relinquish (1 sg. pres.)

    "I relinquish this to the Sangha."

    Two pronouns of different kinds, in different cases, doing different jobs, printed as one word. *Imaṁ* is the proximal demonstrative in the neuter accusative, agreeing with the *cīvaraṁ* named in the sentence before; it is the object. *Ahaṁ* is the first person nominative and it is the subject. The junction is the ordinary elision: the niggahīta of *imaṁ* goes and the *a* of *ahaṁ* is absorbed into the preceding vowel, which lengthens.

    The surface does not show that the word contains a subject and an object. The clue is that the sentence otherwise has neither, and a first person verb with no nominative in sight is the signal to look inside the long word at the front.

    The verb will not be found in the shape given here. *Nissajjāmi* has zero root-text occurrences as a word standing alone. The edition has *nissajjāmīti*, with 26 root-text occurrences: the quotative lengthens the final vowel, and this verb happens never to be used except inside something a monk is quoted as saying.

    The half-resolved *nissajjāmī* has 22 root-text occurrences of its own, the *ti* separated off by an editor, the lengthening left standing. A form can be common and still be absent from every search that spells it correctly.

Four ways of saying "self", in four lines.

20. Yo attano sīlavatāni jantu, anānupuṭṭhova paresa pāva; Anariyadhammaṁ kusalā tamāhu, yo ātumānaṁ sayameva pāva. (Snp 4.3 — Sn p. 153)

    *yo* who (nom. sg. m.) · *attano* his own (gen. sg.) · *sīlavatāni* precepts and practices · *jantu* person · *anānupuṭṭhova* < *anānupuṭṭho* unasked · *va* just · *paresa* to others (dat. pl. of *para*) · *pāva* told · *anariyadhammaṁ* an ignoble nature · *kusalā* the skilled · *tamāhu* < *taṁ* that (acc. sg.) + *āhu* they call · *ātumānaṁ* himself (acc. sg.) · *sayameva* < *sayaṁ* of his own accord · *eva* just

    "The person who tells others of his own precepts and practices unasked — the skilled call that an ignoble nature, in one who speaks of himself unprompted."

    **The three reflexive expressions are *attano*, *ātumānaṁ* and *sayaṁ***, and they stand within four lines of one another in a verse about the vice of talking about oneself. *Attano* is the genitive of *attā*, the ordinary reflexive. *Ātumānaṁ* is the accusative of *ātuma*, the same Sanskrit *ātman* arrived by a different route, and rare enough that Duroiselle calls it "very rarely used".

    *Sayaṁ* alone does not decline. It is an indeclinable, 'oneself, of one's own accord', and it agrees with nothing and carries no case; it simply intensifies. DPD derives it from *sa*, which puts it with the possessive *saka-* and not with *attā*. So the verse holds two reflexives from one family and one from another, and only the dictionary shows which is which.

    The fourth pronoun is *paresa*, and its ending is a pronominal one. *Para* is a pronominal adjective, so its genitive and dative plural is *paresaṁ*, where a noun of the same stem class would give *-ānaṁ*: the second of the three diagnostics, and the same ending as *sabbesaṁ* in item 15. The verse has *paresa*, the same form with its niggahīta dropped, one of the standard metrical accommodations. So one short word carries a pronominal ending and a verse-final shortening on top of it. The editions do not all agree here, and the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's own apparatus records *parassa* against the siglum *ka.*, 'in some manuscripts'.

    This is the hardest reading in the set because nothing in it is irregular. Every form is what the chapter's tables predict. The difficulty is density, four pronouns of three kinds in four lines, two of them rare, and no table can prepare you for density.

**Put it back into Pāli.**

21. (a) ***ahaṁ taṁ rakkhissāmi*** (SN 47.19 — S v 168): *ahaṁ* first person nominative singular, *taṁ* second person accusative singular. If this came out as *maṁ* or *mamaṁ*, the two persons have been crossed; if the pronouns were left out altogether on the grounds that the verb carries its person, the emphasis of the line has been lost with them.

    (b) ***Evaṁ me sutaṁ*** (DN 1 — D i 1): *me* first person singular, instrumental here and, on the commentary's reading, possibly genitive. Three words, and only the one nobody looks at can go wrong.

    (c) ***Ayaṁ te patto*** (Kd 1 — Vin i 94): *ayaṁ* nominative singular of *ima-*, *te* second person enclitic in the genitive or dative singular. The trap is word order: *te* is second and cannot be first.

    (d) ***Evaṁ gopetha attānaṁ*** (Dhp 315 — Dhp p. 45): *attānaṁ* accusative **singular**, against a plural imperative. A plural here is the natural mistake, and this item exists to provoke it.

    The failure this stage exposes is a particular one. Enclitics are recognised effortlessly and produced badly, because recognising one asks only what it means and producing one asks where it may stand. If (c) came out with *te* at the head, the positional rule is not yet yours, and the error is more than a slip of memory.
