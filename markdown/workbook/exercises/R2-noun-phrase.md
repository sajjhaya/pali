## Review: The Noun Phrase

A review is not a recap. The exercises after each chapter test that chapter, and they are the right instrument for that. This tests what only becomes visible once several chapters are in place: where they overlap, where a form belongs to both, and where the habit one chapter builds is the habit the next one breaks. Nothing here can be answered from a single chapter.

It covers only chapters that have been verified in full. It now covers the whole of Part II: **Nouns**, **Gender and Adjectives**, **Pronouns** and **Numerals**. It was first written when only the last two had been through the verification passes, and it has been extended, not rewritten, as the first two caught up, as the format was designed to allow.

The four stages run as they do in the chapter sets, with the review's own constraint on the first two: every line in stages 1 and 2 has already been worked in an earlier set, and is here to be asked a question that set could not ask.

### Sotānugata — the noun phrase

Nothing is withheld. Each line is given with its gloss and its rendering. Say it aloud, then answer.

1. Dīghā jāgarato ratti, dīghaṁ santassa yojanaṁ; Dīgho bālānaṁ saṁsāro, saddhammaṁ avijānataṁ. (Dhp 60 — Dhp p. 9)

    *dīghā* long (f. nom. sg.) · *jāgarato* for one lying awake (m. gen. sg., pres. part.) · *ratti* the night (f. nom. sg.) · *dīghaṁ* long (n. nom. sg.) · *santassa* for the weary (m. gen. sg., p.p.) · *yojanaṁ* a league (n. nom. sg.) · *dīgho* long (m. nom. sg.) · *bālānaṁ* for fools (m. gen. pl.) · *saṁsāro* wandering on (m. nom. sg.) · *saddhammaṁ* the true teaching (m. acc. sg.) · *avijānataṁ* of those not understanding (m. gen. pl., pres. part.)

    "Long is the night for one lying awake; long is the league for the weary; long is the wandering on for fools who do not understand the true teaching."

    You met this verse in the adjectives set, where the question was how one stem takes three genders. Here the question is what it takes them from. Name the three nouns that hand *dīgha* its three endings, give the stem class of each, and say for each whether the adjective's ending is the same ending the noun is carrying or merely the same case. One of the three is not a match, and the item turns on it.

2. Ayaṁ te patto, ayaṁ saṅghāṭi, ayaṁ uttarāsaṅgo, ayaṁ antaravāsako. (Kd 1 — Vin i 94)

    *ayaṁ* this (nom. sg.) · *te* your (2nd gen./dat. sg.) · *patto* bowl (m. nom. sg.) · *saṅghāṭi* outer robe (f. nom. sg.) · *uttarāsaṅgo* upper robe (m. nom. sg.) · *antaravāsako* inner robe (m. nom. sg.)

    "This is your bowl, this your outer robe, this your upper robe, this your inner robe."

    A newly ordained monk being handed his requisites. The pronouns set asked which noun is the odd one out. Here the question is what the nouns show and what they do not. Take the four nouns one at a time and say whether the ending alone fixes the gender; then say the same of *ayaṁ*, four times over. To learn the gender of *saṅghāṭi* you have two words in front of you, and neither will say.

    Then the general point, which is the review's: name the only place in this sentence where gender is visible at all, and say what a reader must do when it is visible nowhere.

3. Tasmiṁ sahassadhā loke sahassaṁ candānaṁ sahassaṁ sūriyānaṁ … cattāri mahāsamuddasahassāni … sahassaṁ brahmalokānaṁ. (AN 10.29 — A v 59)

    *tasmiṁ* in that (m. loc. sg.) · *sahassadhā* thousandfold · *loke* world (m. loc. sg.) · *sahassaṁ* a thousand (n. nom. sg.) · *candānaṁ* of moons (m. gen. pl.) · *sūriyānaṁ* of suns (m. gen. pl.) · *cattāri* four (n. nom. pl.) · *mahāsamuddasahassāni* thousands of great oceans (n. nom. pl.) · *brahmalokānaṁ* of realms of divinity (m. gen. pl.)

    "In that thousandfold world there are a thousand moons, a thousand suns … four thousand great oceans … a thousand realms of divinity."

    The numerals set asked you to find where the construction changes. Here the question is about one word wearing two hats. *Sahassa* stands four times in this line and is not the same kind of thing each time. Say what it is in *sahassaṁ candānaṁ*, what it is in *mahāsamuddasahassāni*, and what *cattāri* is agreeing with in the second (it is not the oceans). Then say what the two constructions together show about how Pāli builds a number larger than eighteen, and why the noun being counted keeps ending up in the genitive.

### Vacasā paricitā — the noun phrase

Recite each until it is fluent, then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

4. Tvaṁ, samma medakathālike, mamaṁ rakkha, ahaṁ taṁ rakkhissāmi. (SN 47.19 — S v 168)

    *tvaṁ* you (2nd nom. sg.) · *samma* dear · *medakathālike* Medakathālikā (voc.) · *mamaṁ* me (1st acc. sg.) · *rakkha* protect! · *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *taṁ* you (2nd acc. sg.) · *rakkhissāmi* I shall protect

    "You protect me, dear Medakathālikā, and I shall protect you."

    An acrobat speaking to his assistant on the pole. Rebuild it from the gloss, aloud, until the four pronouns come in the right order without thinking.

    Then the review question, which is item 6's rule turned on a real sentence. Both verbs already carry their person, so *tvaṁ* and *ahaṁ* add nothing you did not already know from *rakkha* and *rakkhissāmi*. Say why they are there. Then take them out, say the sentence again without them, and say what has been lost: the answer is a single word, and the sentence is a chiasmus built to make it audible.

5. Abhivādanasīlissa, niccaṁ vuḍḍhāpacāyino; Cattāro dhammā vaḍḍhanti, āyu vaṇṇo sukhaṁ balaṁ. (Dhp 109 — Dhp p. 16)

    *abhivādanasīlissa* for one whose habit is to bow (m. gen. sg.) · *niccaṁ* constantly · *vuḍḍhāpacāyino* honouring elders (m. gen. sg.) · *cattāro* four (m. nom. pl.) · *dhammā* things (m. nom. pl.) · *vaḍḍhanti* increase · *āyu* long life · *vaṇṇo* beauty · *sukhaṁ* happiness · *balaṁ* strength

    "For one in the habit of bowing, always honouring the elders, four things increase: long life, beauty, happiness and strength."

    Rebuild it from the gloss, then say the four things without looking. The numeral agrees with *dhammā* and the four things it counts are not all of *dhammā*'s gender. Take the four in turn, give the gender of each, and say what the numeral would have had to be had it agreed with the list instead of with the word that heads it. Then say what a reader should do, in general, when the members of a list disagree in gender with the word that names them.

    One of the four is not an *a*-stem at all, and the class it belongs to is the subject of the recall group's last question. Find it, and say what the two classes have in common.

### Manasānupekkhitā — the noun phrase

**Recall.** Answer from memory, in a phrase. If a question needs the book open, that is the answer to it.

6. A Pāli verb already carries its person. What follows for pronoun subjects, and what does it predict about where you will actually meet *ahaṁ* and *tvaṁ*?
7. Numerals from one to eighteen behave one way and those from nineteen upwards another. Name the difference in one sentence, and say what changes at nineteen, not at twenty.
8. Which numeral declines on the pronoun pattern instead of as an ordinary adjective, and which one form of it goes further and follows the *ima-* type?
9. Name the four ways a numeral can be joined to what it counts. Which of the four is a reader most likely to assume is the only one?
10. One word in the language still inflects as a dual. Name it, say what its *-o* is not, and say why 'two' has no proper paradigm of its own.
11. Masculine and neuter nouns are declined identically except in three cases. Name them, and say what follows when you meet an oblique ending and want to know the gender.
12. A noun in *-a* and an adjective in *-a* form their feminines with different suffixes. Give both, and say why the difference is a grammatical fact, not a phonetic one.
13. Some grammars call the *-as* stems neuter and others call them masculine. Say what is actually happening to those words, and name the one sense-base in the standard list whose adjective gives the game away.

Say what the form is, and what decides it. Each of these is either ambiguous or belongs to more than one chapter at once. Give the analysis, and (the point of the group) name what in a sentence would settle it.

### Coming back — the noun phrase

The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

**Day 3.** Items 1 to 5 aloud. Five lines, and between them the whole of Part II: three genders on one adjective, one pronoun over four nouns, a numeral that counts by taking a genitive, four pronouns in a chiasmus and a numeral agreeing with the head of a list, not its members. If these five come back, the recall group will mostly answer itself.

**Day 10.** Items 14 to 19. Six forms, each ambiguous or belonging to two chapters at once, and in this group a second attempt shows what a first cannot: the first time you are working out what a form *could* be, and the second you find out whether you remember what settles it.

**Day 30.** Items 20 to 25. The five reading sentences and the production item. If *paṭhamaṁ* at item 22 still refuses to resolve and you can say why it refuses instead of reaching for one reading, the review has done its work: some questions close and some do not, and knowing which is which is most of the skill.
