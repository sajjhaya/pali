## Review: Sounds and Script

A review is more than a recap. The exercises after each chapter test that chapter. This set tests what only becomes visible once several chapters are in place. Nothing here can be answered from a single chapter.

It covers **Script and Pronunciation** and **Sandhi**, the whole of Part I. The two chapters have one idea in common, and this review is built around it: the length of a syllable is a quantity that the language is constantly balancing, and a great deal of what looks like arbitrary spelling turns out to be that balancing. Almost every item below joins a fact about sounds to a fact about junctions.

The four stages run as they do in the chapter sets. What is different is that every line in stages 1 and 2 has already been worked in an earlier set, and is here to be asked a question that set could not ask.

### Sotānugata — sounds and script

Nothing is withheld. Each line is given as it stands, divided, and glossed. Say it aloud first and answer afterwards.

1. Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

    su·**ṇā**·tu **me** **bhan**·**te** **saṅ**·**gho**

    *suṇātu* let it listen (3 sg. imper.) · *me* to me (dat./gen. sg.) · *bhante* venerable sirs (voc.) · *saṅgho* the Sangha (m. nom. sg.)

    "Let the Sangha listen to me, venerable sirs."

    You met this line in the first set, where the question was about its weights. Here it is a sandhi question. There are three word boundaries in it and nothing has happened at any of them. Name what each boundary consists of (what ends the word before, what begins the word after), and then say, for each, what would have had to be different for a rule to fire. Item 10 asks for the name of this situation; here you are being asked to recognise it before you have the name.

2. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

    **sab**·ba·**pā**·**pas**·sa a·ka·ra·**ṇaṁ**, ku·sa·**las**·sa u·pa·**sam**·pa·**dā**

    *sabbapāpassa* of all evil (n. gen. sg.) · *akaraṇaṁ* the not-doing (n. nom. sg.) · *kusalassa* of the wholesome (n. gen. sg.) · *upasampadā* the taking up (f. nom. sg.)

    "Not doing any evil; taking up what is wholesome."

    Say it aloud. Twice in these two lines a vowel meets a vowel across a word boundary, and twice nothing happens. Find both. Then do the arithmetic the review is built on: for each of the two, count the *mattā* of the two syllables as they stand, and count what they would have come to had the two vowels contracted in the ordinary way. Say what the difference is, in syllables and in *mattā*, and say which of the two spellings a reciter would find easier to keep in time.

3. …yena brahmāyu brāhmaṇo tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā brahmāyuṁ brāhmaṇaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdi. (MN 91 — M ii 135)

    *yena* by which (m. inst. sg.) · *brahmāyu* Brahmāyu · *brāhmaṇo* the brahmin (m. nom. sg.) · *tenupasaṅkami* he approached by that way · *upasaṅkamitvā* having approached · *brahmāyuṁ* Brahmāyu (acc.) · *brāhmaṇaṁ* the brahmin (acc.) · *abhivādetvā* having bowed · *ekamantaṁ* to one side · *nisīdi* he sat down

    "…he went to the brahmin Brahmāyu; and having approached, he bowed to the brahmin Brahmāyu and sat down to one side."

    Say it aloud twice; you will meet this formula hundreds of times. The same verb stands twice in it, once inside a junction and once free. Find both, take the first apart, and then say what it is about the position of the second that meant no junction could form there. Then answer the harder half: *ekamantaṁ* is also a junction, and unlike the first it has been frozen into a word the dictionaries list. Say what makes the difference between a junction a reader is expected to undo and one a reader is expected to look up.

### Vacasā paricitā — sounds and script

Recite until the line is fluent, then rebuild it with the Pāli covered. Both of these are formulas, and so they are the ones to have by heart.

4. Itipi so bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho vijjācaraṇasampanno sugato lokavidū anuttaro purisadammasārathi satthā devamanussānaṁ buddho bhagavā. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

    "Such is the Blessed One: perfected, fully awakened, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, holy, knower of the world, supreme guide for those fit to be trained, teacher of gods and humans, awakened, blessed."

    Recite it, then write it out from memory and check it letter by letter. Then the review's question: thirteen word boundaries, and at two of them a vowel meets a vowel. Find the two. Say what has happened at each, and then say what has happened at the other eleven. The answer is one word, the same for all eleven. If you have just learnt the vowel-junction rules you will expect this formula to be full of them, and it is one of the least contracted stretches of Pāli there is.

5. Pātimokkhanti ādimetaṁ mukhametaṁ pamukhametaṁ kusalānaṁ dhammānaṁ. (Kd 2 — Vin i 103)

    *pātimokkhanti* the Pātimokkha, so called · *ādimetaṁ* this is the beginning · *mukhametaṁ* this is the face · *pamukhametaṁ* this is the fore · *kusalānaṁ dhammānaṁ* of wholesome states (m. gen. pl.)

    "'Pātimokkha': this is the beginning, this is the face, this is the fore of wholesome states."

    Rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud, junctions and all. You were asked in the sandhi set which of these junctions behaves differently from the others. Here the question is about weight. Take *ādimetaṁ* and *mukhametaṁ*: write out the two words each was made from, divide both the pieces and the joined form into syllables, and say what the junction has done to the weight of the syllable at the join. Then say whether the same thing happens in all four, and what that suggests about why a reciter feels a junction before a reader sees one.

### Manasānupekkhitā — sounds and script

**Recall.** Answer from memory, in a phrase. If a question needs the book open, that is the answer to it.

6. State the two-*mattā* ceiling in one sentence, and then say what a long vowel does when a cluster of consonants arrives after it.
7. Vowel length and syllable weight are two different measurements. Give a single syllable that has a short vowel and is nonetheless heavy, and say what makes it so.
8. Name the two accounts of what the niggahīta sounded like, and then name the one fact about it that neither account disputes.
9. Why are there four kinds of junction between two Pāli words, and no more? Derive the number; do not list them.
10. The tradition has a name for the case where two sounds meet and nothing happens. Give the name, and say why a chapter of sandhi rules makes a reader worse at recognising it.
11. *E* and *o* are long by nature. Give the condition under which they shorten, and name the one class of following cluster before which the grammars disagree about whether they do.
12. A consonant appears between two vowels at a junction. Name the two quite different things it may be, and say what test, if any, tells them apart.
13. How many letters are in the Pāli alphabet? Give every defensible answer and say what each one is counting.

Say what the form is, and what decides it. Each of these belongs to both chapters at once. Give the analysis, and name what in the form itself decides it; that is the point of the group.

### Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — sounds and script

**Read the line.** Give a word-by-word gloss and an idiomatic rendering; divide every syllable, mark the heavy ones, and account for every junction, including any place where two sounds meet and nothing has happened.

20. Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassāti. (MN 91 — M ii 140)
21. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)
22. Esevanto dukkhassā. (SN 12.51 — S ii 84)

**Put it back into Pāli.**

23. The quotative is the junction this book will ask you to undo more often than any other, so make five of them instead. Here are five pairs, every one of which stands joined somewhere in the canon:

    *bhikkhu* + *iti* · *bhikkhako* + *iti* · *nivāsessāmi* + *iti* · *pātimokkhaṁ* + *iti* · *pana* + *iti*

    Write the joined form of each. Then divide the last two syllables of each joined form and mark their weights, and say what the junction has done in every case. Three of the five lengthen a vowel; one has nothing to lengthen; the fifth has no vowel there at all and changes a nasal instead. Sort them, and say what decided each.

    Then the reason this matters more than any other junction: say what happens when a reader meets one of these joined forms and looks it up in a dictionary, and what they must do first.

### Coming back — sounds and script

The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

**Day 3.** Items 1 to 5 aloud. All five are lines you will meet again (three of them are formulas recited daily somewhere in the Theravāda world), and the reason to come back to them first is that the junctions in them are the ones you will be undoing for the rest of the book.

**Day 10.** Items 14 to 19. Six forms, each of which needs a fact from both chapters at once, and the second visit is where you find out whether you were reciting the analysis or making it.

**Day 30.** Items 20 to 23. The three reading lines and the production item together are the whole of Part I in about forty words of Pāli: if the junctions come apart cleanly, the weights fall out right, and you can build the five quotatives, not merely recognise them, then Part I has done its work and the noun chapters can start.
