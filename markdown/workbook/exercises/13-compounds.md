## Exercises: Compounds

In compounding, alone of the topics in this book, the drill and the reading are the same activity. Every long word in a sutta is an exercise set by the text itself, and the only way to get faster is to take a great many of them apart. So this set is longer on splitting than on anything else, and the reading passage at the end is chosen for density, not difficulty: it is a formula you will meet dozens of times, and it is worth learning thoroughly once.

Stage 1 is four passages in which the compounds are already resolved for you, because hearing where a seam falls is a different skill from finding it, and it comes first. Every Pāli line is canonical and cited; none has been composed. Five of the later items contain a trap, and they are not marked. Where you find one, say what the trap is as well as what the answer is; naming it is a different act from spotting it. Worked answers are at the back.

### Sotānugata — compounds

Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear the joins.

1. jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)

    *jātipaccayā* with birth as condition · *jarāmaraṇaṁ* old age and death · *sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā* < *soka* sorrow + *parideva* lamentation + *dukkha* pain + *domanassa* dejection + *upāyāsa* distress · *sambhavanti* come to be

    "With birth as condition, old age and death come to be, and sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and distress."

    Say it aloud slowly and hear where the seams fall. Four Pāli words carry thirteen English ones, and the last of them has five members written solid, yet only one ending appears in it. Say which member that ending sits on and what it settles about the whole. Then say why four of the five members, looked up in a digital text as the compound spells them, would not be found: the failure is the compound behaving correctly.

2. 'sambādho gharāvāso rajopatho, abbhokāso pabbajjā. (MN 27 — M i 179)

    *sambādho* crowded, confined · *gharāvāso* < *ghara* house + *āvāsa* dwelling · *rajopatho* < *raja* dust + *patha* path · *abbhokāso* open space, the open air · *pabbajjā* going forth

    "Life at home is confined, a road of dust; the going forth is the open air."

    Say it aloud. Five words and no verb anywhere. Two of them are compounds; a third looks like one and is not. Name the two and their classes, and say which of them stands in apposition to the other. Then take the third: say what it would divide into if the appearance were true, what makes that division so attractive, and how the question is actually settled.

3. Tirokuṭṭesu tiṭṭhanti, sandhisiṅghāṭakesu ca; Dvārabāhāsu tiṭṭhanti, āgantvāna sakaṁ gharaṁ. (Kp 7)

    *tirokuṭṭesu* < *tiro* beyond + *kuṭṭa* wall · *tiṭṭhanti* they stand · *sandhisiṅghāṭakesu* < *sandhi* junction + *siṅghāṭaka* crossroads · *ca* and · *dvārabāhāsu* < *dvāra* door + *bāhā* post · *āgantvāna* having come · *sakaṁ* their own · *gharaṁ* house

    "Outside the walls they stand, and at the junctions and crossroads; they stand beside the door posts, having come to their own house."

    Say it aloud. Three compounds, three classes, and no two of them are alike. An *avyayībhāva* is normally a frozen neuter accusative in *-aṁ* and does not decline; the first word here is one, and it is a locative plural. Say what that shows about the class. Then name the classes of the other two, which are not the same as each other.

4. Two cases from the Vinaya's case law on theft, the first and the second, in a fixed frame and one word apart:

    Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacittaṁ uppādesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)

    Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacitto āmasi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)

    *tena kho pana samayena* now at that time · *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *rajakattharaṇaṁ* < *rajaka* laundry worker + *attharaṇa* spreading — the place where laundry workers spread their cloth (n. acc. sg.) · *gantvā* having gone · *mahagghaṁ* costly (n. acc. sg.) · *dussaṁ* cloth (n. acc. sg.) · *passitvā* having seen · *theyyacittaṁ* a thought of theft (n. acc. sg.) · *theyyacitto* with theft in mind (m. nom. sg.) · *uppādesi* he produced (3 sg. aor.) · *āmasi* he touched (3 sg. aor.)

    "Now at that time a certain monk went to a laundry workers' spreading-ground, saw a costly cloth, and produced a thought of theft."

    "Now at that time a certain monk went to a laundry workers' spreading-ground, saw a costly cloth, and, with theft in mind, touched it."

    Read the two aloud one after the other. Nothing inside the compound changes, and nor does anything else in the frame, except one ending. Say what *theyya* + *citta* is in each sentence (what class, what gender, what it names), and say why the second sentence contains no word for a thought at all. Set 08 read four passages from this same series of cases for their aorists; here are the compounds those sentences still hold once the verbs have been accounted for.

### Vacasā paricitā — compounds

Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

5. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna sāsanaṁ. (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

    *sabbapāpassa* < *sabba* all + *pāpa* evil, in the genitive · *akaraṇaṁ* < *a-* not + *karaṇa* doing · *kusalassa* of the wholesome · *upasampadā* undertaking · *sacittapariyodapanaṁ* < *sa* one's own + *citta* mind + *pariyodapana* cleansing · *etaṁ* this · *buddhāna* of the awakened ones · *sāsanaṁ* teaching

    "Not doing any evil, undertaking the wholesome, cleansing one's own mind — this is the teaching of the awakened ones."

    Rebuild it from the gloss, and name each compound as you rebuild it. Four compounds and three classes. One relation in the verse is written as two separate words where it could as easily have been compounded; find it, and say what having both options available suggests about the ones that were taken.

6. svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko paccattaṁ veditabbo viññūhī'ti. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

    *svākkhāto* well proclaimed · *bhagavatā* by the Blessed One (m. inst. sg.) · *dhammo* the teaching (m. nom. sg.) · *sandiṭṭhiko* visible here and now · *akāliko* timeless · *ehipassiko* < *ehi* come! + *passa* see! + *-ika* · *opaneyyiko* leading onwards · *paccattaṁ* individually · *veditabbo* to be known · *viññūhi* by the wise · *ti* (quotation)

    "The teaching is well proclaimed by the Blessed One — visible here and now, timeless, inviting one to come and see, leading onwards, to be known individually by the wise."

    Recite it until it is fluent; many readers will have it by heart already. Then rebuild it from the gloss. Four words in a row end in *-iko*. The chapter's last section says that suffix makes an adjective out of a finished compound: turn that claim on all four and look each one up in the dictionary. Only two of them have a compound underneath, so say what the other two are built on instead, and what that does to the chapter's statement: whether it makes it wrong, or narrower than it sounds. One of the two also shows the vowel change the chapter mentions only in a footnote. Then say why *ehipassiko* fits no class in any grammar's list.

### Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — compounds

**Build the form.** Produce the answer before checking it.

26. Resolve *dukkhasamudayo* into two separate words, supplying the case ending the first member would have had.

27. Resolve *sīladhanaṁ* on the *eva* pattern the chapter gives, and then say why 'the wealth of virtue' is not quite what the compound says.

28. *Cittaṁ* is neuter and *bhikkhu* is masculine. Build a *bahubbīhi* from *su-* + *bhāvita* + *citta* meaning 'whose mind is well developed', in the form it would take describing a monk. Then find it in the canon.

29. The grammars' standing example of a possessive compound for 'a man whose hands have been cut off' is *chinnahattho puriso*. Ask of both *chinnahattho* and *hatthacchinno* whether the canon uses them, and say which of the two Pāli actually has, and what the answer implies about a grammar's illustrations as against its rules.

**Read the sentence.** Give the three layers: the line as printed, a word-by-word gloss with every compound resolved, and your own rendering.

30. Saḷāyatanañcidaṁ, bhikkhave, kiṁnidānaṁ kiṁsamudayaṁ kiṁjātikaṁ kiṁpabhavaṁ? (MN 11 — M i 67)

31. Santi sattā apparajakkhajātikā, assavanatā dhammassa parihāyanti. (SN 6.1 — S i 137)

**Put it back into Pāli.** The answer is a particular line of canon, and getting close to it is the exercise. A well-formed sentence that is not the one in the book is a good result, but it is a different exercise.

32. 'Now this, monks, is the noble truth of the origin of suffering.' Two compounds, one nested inside the other as its attribute.

33. 'Outside the walls they stand.' Two words.

**Read at length.**

34. Read **MN 27 — M i 178** twice through without stopping to parse anything. This is not a drill and should not be treated as one; read it as you would read a paragraph in a language you know. Only then answer:

   - The passage contains at least twenty compounds. Find the one whose first member keeps a case ending, and name the case.
   - *Sadevakaṁ*, *samārakaṁ*, *sabrahmakaṁ*, *sassamaṇabrāhmaṇiṁ*, *sadevamanussaṁ* are five of a kind. What kind, and what is the *sa-* doing?
   - *Gahapati vā gahapatiputto vā* has *vā* written out. Why did the passage not compound them?
   - *Kesamassuṁ* is a collective singular. What would the other kind of *dvanda* have looked like, and would the sense have changed?

**A compound list from the canon.** The Vinaya defines a word by listing the kinds of thing it covers, and the list is almost entirely compounds.

35. Rājāno nāma pathabyārājā padesarājā maṇḍalikā antarabhogikā akkhadassā mahāmattā, ye vā pana chejjabhejjaṁ karontā anusāsanti. Ete rājāno nāma. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 47) — Take the first two apart and name their class. Then say what *chejjabhejjaṁ* is, and why that one is not the same kind of compound as the two before it.

**Reaching back.**

36. *Chapter 2.* *Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā* (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1). Chapter 2 uses this line to show that very often no sandhi happens at all. Take the three compounds in it: which are *kammadhārayas* and which are *bahubbīhis*, and how do you know?

37. *Chapter 10.* *Sammāsambuddho* ends in a past participle. Is that participle doing a participle's work here, or a noun's? What in the sentence at MN 27 — M i 178 decides it?

38. *Chapter 12.* Chapter 12 treats *yathābhūtaṁ* among the indeclinables and this chapter treats it as an *avyayībhāva*. Both are correct. Say in one sentence why there is no contradiction.

**Say it aloud.**

39. Read this verse aloud five times, slowly, holding the long vowels and sounding the double consonants. Then say which compound in it no ordinary class will account for, and what it means.

    Gambhīrapaññaṁ medhāviṁ,\
    maggāmaggassa kovidaṁ;\
    Uttamatthamanuppattaṁ,\
    tamahaṁ brūmi brāhmaṇaṁ. (Dhp 403 — Dhp p. 57)

### Coming back — compounds

The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

**Day 3.** Items 12–18 and 19–25. Splitting and classing are the whole of the skill, and they are the two things that decay fastest when a reader stops reading canon daily.

**Day 10.** Items 26–29 and 34. If the twenty compounds in 34 resolve without the chapter open, the formula has stopped being an obstacle and become a foothold.

**Day 30.** Items 1–4 aloud, and 32–33. If item 4 still shows you the whole *bahubbīhi* rule in one changed ending, the class has been understood as a use, not memorised as a list.
