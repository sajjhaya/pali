## Review: The Sentence and the Verse

A review is more than a recap. The exercises after each chapter test that chapter, and they are the right instrument for that. This set tests what only becomes visible once several chapters are in place: where they overlap, where a form belongs to both, and where the habit one chapter builds is the habit the next one breaks. Nothing here can be answered from a single chapter.

It covers the whole of Parts IV, V, VI and VII: **Participles**, **Absolutives and Infinitives**, **Indeclinables and Particles**, **Compounds**, **Word Formation**, **Case Syntax**, **Clause and Sentence Syntax**, **Relative Clauses**, and **Metre**. That is nine chapters, all of them now through the verification passes, and the largest stretch any review in this book has had to cover. R1–R3 each answered to one Part; this one answers to four, because Part VII is a single chapter and does not support a review of its own, and because the nine chapters share one idea worth holding together, not four smaller ones held apart.

The idea is this: each of these chapters is a way of building something larger than a word without adding many words to do it. A participle stands in for a clause. A compound stands in for a phrase, sometimes a whole sentence's worth of one. A case ending stands in for what a preposition would carry in English. A pair of pronouns, one *y-* and one *t-*, stands in for a conjunction English cannot do without. A count of syllables stands in for the punctuation a manuscript never had. Nine chapters, one method: say less, on the understanding that the reader will do the arithmetic.

The four stages run as they do in the chapter sets, with one constraint on the first two that belongs to the review: every line in stages 1 and 2 has already been worked in an earlier set, and is here to be asked a question that set could not ask. Two of them are halves of passages the workbook has so far only ever printed in halves.

### Sotānugata — the sentence and the verse

Nothing is withheld. Each line is given with its gloss. Say it aloud, then answer.

1. Tiṭṭhaṁ caraṁ nisinno va, Sayāno yāvatāssa vitamiddho; (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

    *tiṭṭhaṁ* standing (m. nom. sg., pres. part.) · *caraṁ* walking (m. nom. sg., pres. part.) · *nisinno* seated (m. nom. sg., p.p.) · *va* or · *sayāno* lying down (m. nom. sg., pres. part.) · *yāvatā·assa* < *yāvatā* as long as + *assa* he is · *vitamiddho* free of drowsiness (m. nom. sg.)

    "Standing, walking, seated or lying down, for as long as he is free of drowsiness —"

    Say it aloud. You met this half of the verse in the participles set, and the other half in the tenses set: *Etaṁ satiṁ adhiṭṭheyya*, "he should undertake this mindfulness". Put the two halves back together and answer three things.

    Four participles stand here and one of them is not the same kind as the other three. Say which, and say what that difference of kind does to the sense. Then say what all four have in common syntactically: they are not the subject of anything, and there is no verb in this half of the verse at all. Then, with the other half in front of you: say what these four are attached to, and say why a language with participles can put four alternatives into a single clause where English needs a subordinate one.

2. Tirokuṭṭesu tiṭṭhanti, sandhisiṅghāṭakesu ca; Dvārabāhāsu tiṭṭhanti, āgantvāna sakaṁ gharaṁ. (Kp 7)

    *tirokuṭṭesu* < *tiro* beyond + *kuṭṭa* wall · *tiṭṭhanti* they stand · *sandhisiṅghāṭakesu* < *sandhi* junction + *siṅghāṭaka* crossroads · *ca* and · *dvārabāhāsu* < *dvāra* door + *bāhā* post · *āgantvāna* having come · *sakaṁ* their own · *gharaṁ* house

    "They stand beyond the walls, and at the junctions and crossroads; they stand at the doorposts, having come to their own house."

    Say it aloud. Four lines, and three chapters of this stretch are in them. Name the class of each of the three compounds. Then name the case they all stand in and its job here; it is the same job three times, and the plainest one the case has. Then *āgantvāna*, which is an absolutive with a longer ending than the one you were taught first: say what the ordinary form would be, and say why the longer one stands in a line of verse. Two chapters have to be held together to answer that last one.

3. Ayaṁ, deva, pūraṇo kassapo saṅghī ceva gaṇī ca gaṇācariyo ca ñāto yasassī titthakaro sādhusammato bahujanassa rattaññū cirapabbajito addhagato vayoanuppatto. (DN 2 — D i 47)

    *ayaṁ* this (m. nom. sg.) · *deva* your majesty (m. voc. sg.) · *pūraṇo kassapo* Pūraṇa Kassapa (m. nom. sg.) · *saṅghī* having a community (m. nom. sg.) · *ceva … ca* both … and · *gaṇī* having a following · *gaṇācariyo* a teacher of a following · *ñāto* well known · *yasassī* renowned · *titthakaro* a founder of a sect · *sādhusammato* held in high regard · *bahujanassa* by many people (m. gen. sg., agent with a past participle) · *rattaññū* of long standing · *cirapabbajito* long gone forth · *addhagato* advanced in years · *vayoanuppatto* come to the last stage of life

    "Your majesty, this Pūraṇa Kassapa has a community and a following, teaches a following, is well known and renowned, a founder of a sect, held in high regard by many people, of long standing, long gone forth, advanced in years, come to the last stage of life."

    Say it aloud. It is a courtier flattering a king, and it is meant to sound like a list that will not stop. This one sentence needs four chapters of this stretch, and it has no verb in it.

    Say first what is holding it together: every word after *kassapo* is in one case, and you should say which and what the relation is called. Then sort the epithets into their kinds (the ones that are past participles, the ones built with a possessive suffix, and the ones that are compounds), and name the chapter that accounts for each kind. Then *bahujanassa*, which a reader is likely to get wrong: say what case it is, what it is attached to, and why it is not what it looks like.

### Vacasā paricitā — the sentence and the verse

Recite each until it is fluent, then rebuild it with the Pāli covered.

4. jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)

    *jātipaccayā* with birth as condition · *jarāmaraṇaṁ* old age and death · *sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā* < *soka* sorrow + *parideva* lamentation + *dukkha* pain + *domanassa* dejection + *upāyāsa* distress · *sambhavanti* come to be

    "With birth as condition, old age and death come to be, and sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and distress."

    Rebuild it from the gloss, aloud. It is the last link of dependent origination and you will meet it more often than almost any other sentence in the canon, so it is worth having whole.

    Then: four words, and two of them are compounds of different classes. Name both classes and say what each does that a string of separate words would not. Then *jātipaccayā*: say what case it is and what job that case has here, and say what the same two words would look like if the relation were spelt out instead of compounded.

5. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna' sāsanaṁ. (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27) — Read it aloud, holding every long vowel to its full two beats. Then answer: name the suffix and the negative prefix in *akaraṇaṁ*, the class of the compound and the action-noun suffix on its last member in *sacittapariyodapanaṁ*, and say what the last word's genitive plural would have had to keep, and what it had to lose, for the line to be a line of verse at all.

### Manasānupekkhitā — the sentence and the verse

**Recall.** Answer from memory, in a phrase. If a question needs the book open, that is the answer to it.

6. A transitive verb's past participle and an intransitive verb's point in opposite directions. State the rule, and say what English does with one shape that hides the difference.
7. An absolutive's unstated agent is ordinarily the main verb's subject. Say what has to be true of the main clause before that stops holding, and give the tradition's name for the construction that results.
8. Name the tradition's four jobs for a *nipāta*, and say which one changes how a page is actually read.
9. Give *ekapadatta* and *ekavibhattitā* in a sentence each, and say which of the two catches a beginner out.
10. A *taddhita* suffix strengthens a vowel that is not next to it. Say where, and say why that single fact decides where a word is filed in the dictionary.
11. The tradition counts eight *vibhatti* and six *kāraka*. Name the two endings left over, and say why the tradition puts them outside the system, as opposed to merely finding them hard to classify.
12. State the rule governing the whole absolutive chain in one sentence, and say what has to change about the sentence before the chain stops working.
13. Give the rule that decides every relative pronoun, and say which half of it is agreement and which is function.
14. Give the rule for *e* and *o*, and say what makes them different from every other vowel in the language for this purpose.
15. Participles, absolutives and infinitives are three chapters built from one class of suffix. Name the tradition's word for that class, and say what single job unites all three formations under it.
16. *Bahubbīhi* is not a seventh class of compound. Say what it actually is, and name the suffix that does structurally the same thing (turns a finished compound into an adjective), and say which chapter treats it, which is not the one you would guess.
17. The genitive and the vocative sit outside the *kāraka* system, each for a stated reason. An absolute construction is named after its case for a related reason. Give both.
18. Two light syllables equal one heavy. Name the two devices this equivalence licenses in either direction, and say which is common and which is rare.

**Say what the form is, and what decides it.** Each of these is either ambiguous or belongs to more than one chapter at once. Give the analysis, and name what in the sentence decides it; that is the point of the group.

### Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — the sentence and the verse

**Read the sentence.** Give a word-by-word gloss and an idiomatic rendering, and account for every non-finite form, every case, and every relative pair in it. Each of these needs at least two chapters from this stretch.

33. So kho ahaṁ … akāmakānaṁ mātāpitūnaṁ assumukhānaṁ rudantānaṁ kesamassuṁ ohāretvā kāsāyāni vatthāni acchādetvā agārasmā anagāriyaṁ pabbajiṁ. (MN 26 — M i 163)

    - Two absolutives share a subject with the main verb; one whole phrase does not. Say whose action each construction reports, and say why the second could not have been an absolutive.

34. Evaṁ passaṁ, bhikkhave, sutavā ariyasāvako rūpasmimpi nibbindati, vedanāyapi nibbindati, saññāyapi nibbindati, saṅkhāresupi nibbindati, viññāṇasmimpi nibbindati. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)

    - Three of the five case-marked words are unambiguous. Two are not. Say what decides the two, and name the two different things that decide them.

35. Seyyathāpi, bhante, purisassa agāraṁ ekādasadvāraṁ, so tasmiṁ agāre āditte ekamekenapi dvārena sakkuṇeyya attānaṁ sotthiṁ kātuṁ. (MN 52 — M i 353)

    - Three constructions from this stretch are in this one sentence. Name all three and say what each contributes.

36. No cetaṁ, bhikkhave, abhavissa ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, nayidha jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyetha. Yasmā ca kho, bhikkhave, atthi ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, tasmā jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyati. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 80)

    - Two sentences, two moods in the first and one mood repeated in the second. Name the moods and say what the shift between the two sentences does to the meaning of *yasmā*.

37. Ito so, bhikkhave, ekanavutikappe yaṁ vipassī bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi. (DN 14 — D ii 2)

    - Buddhaghosa resolves this *yaṁ* instead of leaving it unanalysed. Give his resolution, name the case he assigns it, and say what would have to be true of an empty relative for a reader to accept his method generally, not only here.

38. Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    - No finite verb, and no *yo … so*. Name the two devices holding the sentence together, and say what kind of clause this is on the reckoning of the chapter on clause syntax.

**Scan the line.** Each of these has already been scanned in the metre chapter. Do not merely repeat the grid; say what the departure is, and connect it to something taught earlier in the book.

39. **Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna' sāsanaṁ.** (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27) — in the last line, what case and number does the full, un-elided form of the last word carry, and where in the book was that ending first taught?
40. **Bahū devā manussā ca, maṅgalāni acintayuṁ; Ākaṅkhamānā sotthānaṁ, brūhi maṅgalamuttamaṁ.** (Kp 5) — name the variation the third *pāda* is in, and say what its cadence is in place of the *pathyā*'s.
41. **Tapo ca brahmacariyañca, ariyasaccāna' dassanaṁ; Nibbānasacchikiriyā ca, etaṁ maṅgalamuttamaṁ.** (Kp 5) — the first *pāda* needs two separate accommodations and the second needs two more. Name all four, and say why none of them can do another's work.
42. **Catūhapāyehi ca vippamutto, Chaccābhiṭhānāni abhabba kātuṁ;** (Kp 6) — this line and its counterpart *abhabbŏ* raise a question this book has raised twice before, in two different chapters. Name both.
43. **Satto guhāyaṁ bahunābhichanno, Tiṭṭhaṁ naro mohanasmiṁ pagāḷho;** (Snp 4.2 — Sn p. 151) — the second line departs from the first inside the metre's break. Say how.
44. **Appaṁ vata jīvitaṁ idaṁ, Oraṁ vassasatāpi miyyati;** (Snp 4.6 — Sn p. 158) — count the measures in both lines. They do not come out the same. Say what the metre is fixing, then, if it is not the length of one line against the next.

**Read at length.** Three consecutive passages, all of them met before, none of them together. Read all three through before answering.

45. Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    - The first sentence names its construction by its tense. Which chapter's construction is it, and what does the tense say about when Koṇḍañña's insight arrives relative to the discourse still being spoken?
    - The second sentence has no finite verb and no correlative pronoun of the ordinary shape. Name what generalises it, and name the chapter that treats it.
    - The third sentence's case would be an ordinary locative of place if the sentence stopped one word earlier. What turns it into something else, and whose action does the instrumental name?

**Reaching back.** Five items reaching into Parts I–III. Each is a place where what you learnt there and what you learnt in this stretch have to be held together.

46. Chapter 2. *Duccaritaṁ* keeps its *c*; *duttaro* keeps its *t*; *uggacchati* keeps its *g*. State the rule from the compounds chapter that explains why an ordinary rule of sandhi does not flatten any of them, and say whose problem the rule is solving.
47. Chapter 3. *Jātitopi anussarati, nāmatopi anussarati, gottatopi anussarati.* (DN 14) Name the suffix, say which earlier chapter introduces it, and say what the *pi* on each word has done to a reader's chances of finding the bare form by search.
48. Chapter 5. The relative stem *ya(d)-* inflects as one demonstrative stem does, on one source's authority. Name the stem and the source, then follow the chain back to the earlier chapter that actually prints a paradigm in full. It is one step further back than you will expect.
49. Chapter 4. The chapter on gender and adjectives promised the adverbial accusative would return, and it did. Give one example from each of the two chapters, and name the larger table the second chapter built on top of the promise.
50. Chapter 8. The conditional tense was called the rarest finite form in the language, in the review that covered it. Where does it finally get the one job it does regularly, and what is the standing form of that job?

**Put it back into Pāli.** The nine chapters of this stretch have one method between them, and every item so far has run it in the direction of taking things apart. This one runs it the other way.

51. Here are the members of three compounds this review has already printed, given uncompounded. Build each, put the first into the locative plural as its line has it, and name the class of each.

    *tiro* + *kuṭṭa* · *ghara* + *āvāsa* · *raja* + *patha*

    Then say which of the three you could not have built from its members alone. The answer is a fact about one word, not a rule about compounding, and the form you would otherwise have written does not occur anywhere in the canon. Check the first against item 2, where it stands in its line, and the other two against the compounds set, where they stand in theirs.

    Finally, the general question this stretch has been building towards. Say in two sentences what a compound buys a reciter that a string of separate words does not, and what it costs a reader.

### Coming back — the sentence and the verse

The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

**Day 3.** Items 1 to 5 aloud. Five passages, and between them the whole method of this stretch: four participles standing in for a clause, three compounds standing in for three phrases, a courtier's list with no verb in it, the last link of dependent origination in four words, and a verse whose final genitive plural had to lose a syllable to be a verse at all. Say them, do not read them.

**Day 10.** Items 39 to 44. The six scanned lines, together, and from the marks up, not from the answer down. Scansion alone in the book can be done wholly by rule, so a second attempt either comes out identical to the first or shows you which rule you have not got.

**Day 30.** Items 46 to 51. The five reaching-back items and the production item. These are the only places in the book where a claim about the book itself is being asked for, and they are the last things to be sure of: if you can say where each of these nine chapters reaches back to, then the book has been read as one argument, not as nineteen.
