## Clause and Sentence Syntax

### One finite verb, four sentences

The last chapter was about what a single ending does. This one is about what happens when a sentence gets long, and it is best begun by looking at one.

- Sīho, bhikkhave, migarājā sāyanhasamayaṁ āsayā **nikkhamati**. Āsayā *nikkhamitvā* **vijambhati**. *Vijambhitvā* samantā catuddisā **anuviloketi**. Samantā catuddisā *anuviloketvā* tikkhattuṁ sīhanādaṁ **nadati**. (AN 4.33 — A ii 33)
- *sīho* the lion · *bhikkhave* monks · *migarājā* king of beasts · *sāyanhasamayaṁ* in the evening (m. acc. sg., adverbial) · *āsayā* from his lair (m. abl. sg.) · *nikkhamati* comes out · *nikkhamitvā* having come out · *vijambhati* he stretches · *vijambhitvā* having stretched · *samantā catuddisā* in all four directions · *anuviloketi* he surveys · *anuviloketvā* having surveyed · *tikkhattuṁ* three times · *sīhanādaṁ* a lion's roar (m. acc. sg.) · *nadati* he roars
- The lion, monks, the king of beasts, **comes out** from his lair in the evening. Having come out from his lair, **he stretches**. Having stretched, **he surveys** the four directions all around. Having surveyed the four directions all around, **he roars** a lion's roar three times.

Four sentences, four finite verbs, and every one of them is anticipated by a non-finite form that repeats the sentence before. The pattern is how the passage is built, and missing it, you will read four disconnected statements where the Pāli gives one continuous action.

That is this chapter's subject. Pāli subordinates by case and by participle, not by conjunction, and the finite verb is the scarce resource that a long sentence is organised around. English marks its subordinate clauses at the front, with 'when', 'although', 'because', 'if'; Pāli marks them, when it marks them at all, on the ending of a participle in the middle. The whole of what follows is an inventory of how it does that, and of what to do when it has not done it at all.

### What holds a sentence together

Start from Perniola's definition, because it is the narrowest: a sentence or clause is a group of words with a finite verb and a complete meaning, and "the finite verb is sometimes understood."[^cls-perniola-def] Both halves matter. A finite verb makes a clause a clause, and Pāli will often decline to supply one.

[^cls-perniola-def]: Perniola, *Pali Grammar*, the unnumbered paragraph that opens his chapter XXI, p. 383, immediately above §296.

Duroiselle states the ordinary order in four rules, and they hold well enough to be useful even though every one of them has exceptions.[^cls-duroiselle-order] The predicate comes last. In a simple sentence with an object the order is subject, object, predicate: *dāso kammaṁ karoti*, 'the slave does the work'. Words qualifying the subject or the object come before it, and adverbs come before the verb. Adverbs of time come first of all, and so many suttas open *ekaṁ samayaṁ* or *tena kho pana samayena* before anything else has happened.

[^cls-duroiselle-order]: Duroiselle §588, p. 299. His §589 adds a small thing early: Pāli has no article, and where English needs one, *eko* or *ekacce* serves for 'a' and *so* or *eso* for 'the': *so puriso*, 'the man'.

His first rule is too strong, and his own grammar contains the evidence against it. Perniola records that a question can be marked by putting the verb at the front: *atthi nu kho, bhante, añño koci mayā upaṭṭhākataro*, 'is there, sir, anyone who is a better attendant to you?' (MN 81 — M ii 51)[^cls-verb-first] Word order in Pāli is not free, but it is a rhetorical instrument, not a grammatical one: the endings carry the grammar, and that leaves the order available for emphasis. Fronting is how a Pāli sentence shouts.

[^cls-verb-first]: Perniola §302.d, p. 389, who cites it as MN II 51:4 and prints it short (*atthi nu te añño koci mayā upaṭṭhākataro*, where the sentence has *nu kho, bhante*), construing the *te* as the dative his English renders, "a better attendant to you". It should be said that the canonical sentence carries *nu kho* as well, so the word order does not mark the question alone. His second example is weaker still: *anuññātosi mātāpitūhi agārasmā anagāriyaṁ pabbajjāya*, which he prints with *pana tvaṁ* after the first word, is at MN 82 — M ii 60 and is a statement, not a question: Raṭṭhapāla's friends telling him he has his parents' permission. The fronting is real; the reader should look for a particle before concluding that fronting alone marks a question.

#### The sentence with no verb

Where English requires *is*, Pāli commonly requires nothing. The forms of *hoti* and *atthi* are understood, not written, and this is at its most frequent where the predicate is a past participle or an agent noun.[^cls-nominal] Wait for the verb in such a sentence and you will wait for ever.

[^cls-nominal]: Perniola §296, pp. 383–384; Duroiselle §590(1), where the four kinds of predicate are set out: a finite verb, a substantive with *hoti* understood, an adjective with *hoti* understood, and a past participle standing as a finite verb.

- **Appamādo amatapadaṁ**, pamādo maccuno padaṁ. (Dhp 21 — Dhp p. 4)
- *appamādo* diligence (m. nom. sg.) · *amatapadaṁ* the state of the deathless (nt. nom. sg.) · *pamādo* negligence (m. nom. sg.) · *maccuno* of death (m. gen. sg.) · *padaṁ* the state, the footing (nt. nom. sg.)
- **Diligence is the state of the deathless**; negligence is the state of death.

There are four words in the first half and no verb among them, and the sentence is complete. Notice also what does *not* happen: *appamādo* is masculine and *amatapadaṁ* is neuter, and they sit in apposition without either changing to match the other. When a substantive stands where the verb would be, the concord of gender and number lapses: the two nouns are identified with each other, not made to agree.[^cls-no-concord] Where an adjective or a past participle is the predicate, concord holds, and it is gender and number that are at stake: *so gato*, *sā gatā*, *taṁ gataṁ*.

[^cls-no-concord]: Duroiselle §590(4), which is the only statement of this in the collection, and §590(3) for the adjective and past participle.

#### Agreement, and what it will not tell you

Three agreements operate, and they are best stated flat before the exceptions crowd in.[^cls-concord] A finite verb agrees with its subject in **number and person**. An adjective or participle qualifying a noun agrees with it in **gender, number and case**. A relative agrees with its antecedent in **gender, number and person**, the next chapter's subject. When several subjects of different persons share one verb, the verb goes into the first person plural if a first person is among them, and otherwise into the second person plural.

[^cls-concord]: Duroiselle §§590–592, pp. 299–300. His illustrations of the mixed-person rule are constructed and occur nowhere in the canon; the rule itself is sound and matches the canon.

The commonest exception is not marginal at all. *Sati*, the locative singular of the present participle of *atthi*, is the workhorse of the whole absolute construction, and it stands beside the feminine *jāti* at *jātiyā sati* and beside a plural at *saṅkhāresu … sati*, masculine singular in both.[^cls-sati-agreement] The Digital Pali Dictionary files two entries for those three letters, one locative singular and one locative plural. Wijesekera reports that the habit is shared with other Indo-European languages, and English does the same thing in 'there's two of them'. The form does not agree, and a reader should stop waiting for it to.

[^cls-sati-agreement]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses*, "The locative absolute", p. 51, on the formula of conditionality: "the singulars *asati* and *sati* are used even when the corresponding locative noun is the plural *saṅkhāresu*." Wijesekera §184.b.iii reaches the plural half of it independently, from *pādesu sati abhikkamapaṭikkamo paññāyati* (SN 35.236 — S iv 171), 'when there are feet, going and coming appears'.

### The absolutive chain

Return to the lion. The construction there is the commonest way Pāli builds a long sentence, and it has one governing rule: when a run of actions shares a subject, every verb but the last goes into the absolutive.[^cls-absolutive-chain] The absolutive is indeclinable and carries no person, no number and no tense; what it carries is priority. The one finite verb at the end supplies everything the others have gone without.

[^cls-absolutive-chain]: Perniola §303, closing note (p. 391), and §310.e (p. 402). The morphology of the absolutive (the *-tvā*, *-tvāna*, *-ya* and *-tuna* formations and which stems take which) is in the chapter on absolutives and infinitives.

- Atha kho bhagavā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ *nivāsetvā* pattacīvaram*ādāya* sāvatthiṁ piṇḍāya **pāvisi**. (MN 26 — M i 160)
- *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *pubbaṇhasamayaṁ* in the morning (m. acc. sg., adverbial) · *nivāsetvā* having dressed · *pattacīvaraṁ* bowl and robe (nt. acc. sg.) · *ādāya* having taken · *sāvatthiṁ* Sāvatthī (f. acc. sg.) · *piṇḍāya* for alms (m. dat. sg.) · *pāvisi* he entered (3 sg. aor.)
- Then the Blessed One dressed in the morning, took his bowl and robe, and **entered** Sāvatthī for alms.

The English needs three finite verbs and a conjunction. The Pāli has one finite verb and two absolutives, and it is a single grammatical unit, not three joined ones.

The chain has three shapes, and Bodhi distinguishes them.[^cls-bodhi-three] The plain sequence is above. The second is rhetorical repetition, where the same absolutive is chained across a list (*jātisaṁvattanikepi saṅkhāre abhisaṅkharitvā jarāsaṁvattanikepi saṅkhāre abhisaṅkharitvā*, and so on for death and for sorrow, SN 56.42 — S v 449), and the effect is cumulative, not narrative. The third is the lion's, and nothing in Pāli prose is more characteristic: the finite verb of one sentence becomes the absolutive that opens the next. Each sentence is joined on at the last action of the sentence before. Whether one prints such a passage as four sentences or as one is only a decision about punctuation, since the Pāli has no punctuation to consult.

[^cls-bodhi-three]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses*, "The absolutive", pp. 49–50, where all three are illustrated. He treats the third as a way of constructing paragraphs, not sentences, the right level at which to describe it: it is a feature of oral composition, and it makes a long passage recitable.

And the chain has a limit, on which this chapter turns. It works because the subject does not change. The moment the prior action belongs to somebody else, the absolutive cannot be used, because it cannot say whose action it is. Pāli then reaches for a different instrument.

### What an absolute construction is

Take a noun or a pronoun, put it in an oblique case, and agree a participle with it. The result has a subject and a predicate of its own, and it is therefore a clause; it stands outside the grammar of the main sentence, and it is therefore called **absolute**, from Latin *absolutus*, 'freed, detached'. Wijesekera's formulation is exact, and every word of it counts: the construction "differs from the simple temporal or modal use of a case only in the predicative character of the participle. Since the latter's presence makes it virtually a clause separate from the main sentence having a predicate of its own, it is regarded as freed or detached (*absolutus*) from the construction of the rest of the sentence. But there is always a logical connection between the two."[^cls-wij-absolute]

[^cls-wij-absolute]: Wijesekera §182. The term 'absolute' is not a classical Latin usage in this sense; Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students*, p. 37, notes that it "seems to have been a development of later grammar in Europe."

Two practical consequences follow from that definition. Because the absolute has its own subject, its subject is normally different from the main clause's; that is its purpose. And because the case is what detaches the phrase, the construction is named after the case it uses: locative absolute, genitive absolute, and the two rarer ones.

Which gives the rule a reader most needs, and Bodhi is the only source in the collection to state it as a contrast:

> If the prior action is done by the subject of the main verb, the **absolutive** of that action is used; if the prior action is done by someone or something else, the **locative absolute** with the past participle is used.[^cls-bodhi-rule]

[^cls-bodhi-rule]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses*, "The locative absolute", p. 50. The distinction makes the two constructions non-competing, not merely different.

#### What the tradition calls it

The indigenous grammarians have no category 'absolute construction' at all. What they have is a **case-substitution rule**, and it sits in the *kāraka* section beside the substitutions the last chapter taught. Kaccāyana gives two suttas, neither of which mentions a participle:

| Sutta | Text | The *vutti*'s gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Kacc 305 | *Anādare ca* | *anādare chaṭṭhīvibhatti hoti, sattamī ca* |
| Kacc 313 | *Kālabhāvesu ca* | *kālabhāvesu ca kattari payujjamāne sattamīvibhatti hoti* |

The first says that in the sense of ***anādara*** (conventionally 'disregard') the sixth case-ending occurs, and the seventh. The second says that in expressions of *kāla*, 'time', and ***bhāva***, 'state', the seventh occurs when the agent is being employed.[^cls-kacc] Between them they cover the locative and genitive absolutes exactly, without ever naming a construction. That is no oversight: the tradition is describing the uses of a *vibhatti*, and an absolute is one use of the seventh *vibhatti*.

[^cls-kacc]: Read in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition of the Kaccāyanabyākaraṇa, Kārakakappa, sixth *kaṇḍa*. Wijesekera §158.a gives the *anādara* rule as Kaccāyana 307; 307 is *Tatiyāsattamīnañca*. Thānuttamo's numbers, 305 and 313 (p. 71), are the right ones.

Aggavaṁsa's *Saddanīti* sharpens both. His rule for the locative is *Yambhāvo bhāvalakkhaṇaṁ*, and he unpacks the compound in the sentence after it: *bhāvantarassa lakkhaṇaṁ bhāvalakkhaṇaṁ*, "***bhāvalakkhaṇa*** is the marking of one state by another"; and then, "when that state is understood, the seventh occurs, and the sixth is also seen."[^cls-saddaniti-325] Moggallāna and the *Payogasiddhi* put the same rule in nearly the same words: *yassa bhāvo bhāvantarassa lakkhaṇaṁ bhavati, tato sattamī*, 'the state by which another state is marked goes into the seventh'.

[^cls-saddaniti-325]: *Saddanīti* 325; Moggallāna 34 and *Payogasiddhi* 34. Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students*, p. 37, gives this rule as *yassa bhāvena bhāvalakkhaṇaṁ bhavati, tasmiṁ sattamī vibhatti bhavati*, citing *Saddanīti* 728, Moggallāna 37 and *Payogasiddhi* 35. That sentence occurs in none of the three, nor anywhere else in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; it is Pāṇini's Sanskrit rule II.3.37 translated back into Pāli. His analysis of the construction is excellent and this book leans on it; his quotation of the rule is not the rule.

And his gloss on *anādara* is better than any Western rendering of it. *Anādaro'ti dvinnaṁ lakkhaṇa-lakkhitabbakriyānaṁ ekappahārena pavattiyā adhivacanaṁ*: "***anādara*** is a term for the simultaneous occurrence of the two actions, the marking one and the one to be marked."[^cls-anadara] That means 'at the same time as', not 'disregard', and it explains at a stroke why one construction covers both 'while' and 'in spite of', which every grammar in the collection reports as two senses and none of them accounts for. Something done in spite of something else is something done while it is going on.

[^cls-anadara]: *Saddanīti* 326, *Chaṭṭhī cānādare*. The word *anādara* is inherited from Pāṇini II.3.38, *ṣaṣṭhī cānādare*, where it does mean disregard; Aggavaṁsa keeps the term and redefines it around what the Pāli examples actually show.

#### Which participles may stand in one

Wijesekera puts a restriction on it, and the restriction is specific enough to be tested. The participle in an absolute construction is either a present participle or a past passive one. The future passive participle is never so employed, nor is the past active participle, "since these cannot agree with the substantive put in the oblique case."[^cls-wij-participles]

[^cls-wij-participles]: Wijesekera §182.

Half of that holds and half does not. No future passive participle stands in an absolute construction anywhere I have looked, and the reason he gives is sound. But the past active participle in *-āvin* does, and the case is canonical:

- Seyyathāpi, ānanda, gahapatissa vā gahapatiputtassa vā manuññaṁ bhojanaṁ **bhuttāvissa** bhattasammado hoti. (DN 17 — D ii 195)
- *seyyathāpi* just as · *ānanda* Ānanda · *gahapatissa* of a householder (m. gen. sg.) · *gahapatiputtassa* of a householder's son (m. gen. sg.) · *manuññaṁ* delicious (nt. acc. sg.) · *bhojanaṁ* food (nt. acc. sg.) · *bhuttāvissa* of one who has eaten (m. gen. sg., past active participle of *bhuñjati*) · *bhattasammado* drowsiness after a meal (m. nom. sg.) · *hoti* there is
- Just as, Ānanda, **when a householder or a householder's son has eaten** delicious food there is drowsiness after the meal.

*Bhuttāvī* is a past **active** participle (the Digital Pali Dictionary resolves it √*bhuj* + *tāvī* and files it as one), and it stands here in the genitive with its own object *bhojanaṁ*, marking a state that belongs to somebody other than the subject of *hoti*.[^cls-bhuttavissa] So the rule is right about the future passive participle and too strong about the past active one. Weakened, it still holds: a reader will meet the present participle and the past passive one in the great majority of cases, and *bhuttāvissa* is rare enough that Thānuttamo had to go looking for it.

[^cls-bhuttavissa]: The example is Thānuttamo's, p. 70 n. 102, who takes the point from Hendriksen and from correspondence with Oberlies. *Bhuttāvissa* has thirteen root-text occurrences. He also notes that the genitive absolute takes a past participle only as the final member of a compound, and that its agent is invariably a living being, both of which are borne out below.

### The locative absolute

This is the ordinary one. It occurs everywhere, it is the construction the tradition's *bhāvalakkhaṇa* rule is written for, and the tense of the participle tells the reader almost everything: a past participle where the subordinate action precedes the main one, a present participle where the two run together.[^cls-tense-rule]

[^cls-tense-rule]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses*, "The locative absolute", p. 50.

- **Parinibbute bhagavati** saha parinibbānā mahābhūmicālo ahosi bhiṁsanako salomahaṁso. (DN 16 — D ii 156)
- *parinibbute*<sub>a</sub> fully extinguished (m. loc. sg., past participle) · *bhagavati*<sub>a</sub> the Blessed One (m. loc. sg.) · *saha parinibbānā* together with the full extinguishment · *mahābhūmicālo* a great earthquake (m. nom. sg.) · *ahosi* there was (3 sg. aor.) · *bhiṁsanako* terrifying · *salomahaṁso* hair-raising
- **When the Blessed One had attained final Nibbāna**, there was, together with the final Nibbāna, a great earthquake, terrifying and hair-raising.

- Imasmiñca pana **veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne** āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)
- *imasmiṁ*<sub>a</sub> this (nt. loc. sg.) · *veyyākaraṇasmiṁ*<sub>a</sub> exposition (nt. loc. sg.) · *bhaññamāne*<sub>a</sub> being spoken (nt. loc. sg., present participle of the passive *bhaññati*, from *bhaṇati*) · *āyasmato koṇḍaññassa* of the venerable Koṇḍañña (m. gen. sg.) · *virajaṁ* dust-free · *vītamalaṁ* stainless · *dhammacakkhuṁ* the eye of the Dhamma (nt. nom. sg.) · *udapādi* arose (3 sg. aor.)
- And **while this exposition was being spoken**, the dust-free, stainless eye of the Dhamma arose in the venerable Koṇḍañña.

In the first the Buddha's *parinibbāna* is over before the earth moves, and the participle is past. In the second the discourse is still being delivered when Koṇḍañña's insight breaks, and the participle is present. Neither sentence contains a word meaning 'when'.

#### The six senses

The temporal sense is the base, but it is not the only one. Wijesekera lists six, and four of them are invisible to anyone taught only 'when'.[^cls-six-senses]

[^cls-six-senses]: Wijesekera §182 for the enumeration and §§183–184 for the examples; the senses of cause, condition and hypothesis are his §184.a–b, and the concessive is §183.d.

| Sense | Marked by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time | nothing; the default | *parinibbute bhagavati* (DN 16 — D ii 156) |
| Attendant circumstance | a present participle | *ukkāsu dhāriyamānāsu … niyyāsi* (DN 3 — D i 108) |
| Cause | context; often a verb of feeling | *dibbe cakkaratane antarahite anattamano* (DN 26) |
| Condition | *sati* or *asati*, usually | *jātiyā sati jarāmaraṇaṁ hoti* (SN 12.49 — S ii 78) |
| Hypothesis | an interrogative main clause | *dassane, bhagavā, sati kathaṁ paṭipajjitabbaṁ* (DN 16 — D ii 141) |
| Concession | *eva*, *evaṁ pi*, or a negative main clause | *deve vassante … neva passeyya* (DN 16) |

A reader of the suttas meets the conditional use first and most often, because the entire formula of dependent origination is built on it:

- **Jātiyā sati** jarāmaraṇaṁ hotī ti. (SN 12.49 — S ii 78)
- *jātiyā* birth (f. loc. sg.) · *sati* there being (m. loc. sg., present participle of *atthi*) · *jarāmaraṇaṁ* old-age-and-death (nt. nom. sg.) · *hoti* there is · *ti* end of quotation
- '**When there is birth**, there is old-age-and-death.'

No word in that sentence says 'if' or 'because'. The locative absolute says only that one state is the mark of another, as the doctrine says too, and so Aggavaṁsa, illustrating his rule for the locative absolute, reaches past all the examples a grammarian might have invented and quotes *imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imasmiṁ asati idaṁ na hoti*.[^cls-aggavamsa-idappaccayata] The grammar and the doctrine are the same sentence.

[^cls-aggavamsa-idappaccayata]: *Saddanīti* 325, whose run of illustrations closes with the formula of conditionality. The formula itself is at MN 79 — M ii 32 and in many places; the SN 12.49 line above is its expansion into the twelve links.

The concessive sense reverses the reader's expectation, and 'when' produces nonsense in it:

- **Deve vassante deve gaḷagaḷāyante vijjullatāsu niccharantīsu asaniyā phalantiyā** neva passeyya, na pana saddaṁ suṇeyya. (DN 16 — D ii 131)
- *deve*<sub>a</sub> the sky (m. loc. sg.) · *vassante*<sub>a</sub> raining (m. loc. sg., pres. part.) · *gaḷagaḷāyante*<sub>a</sub> thundering (m. loc. sg., pres. part. of the onomatopoeic *gaḷagaḷāyati*) · *vijjullatāsu*<sub>b</sub> the lightning-streaks (f. loc. pl.) · *niccharantīsu*<sub>b</sub> flashing out (f. loc. pl., pres. part.) · *asaniyā*<sub>c</sub> the thunderbolt (f. loc. sg.) · *phalantiyā*<sub>c</sub> splitting (f. loc. sg., pres. part.) · *neva passeyya* he would not see · *na pana saddaṁ suṇeyya* nor would he hear a sound
- **Even with the rain falling, the sky thundering, lightning flashing and thunderbolts splitting**, he would neither see nor hear a sound.

Five nouns and four participles, all in the locative, all in a single absolute clause, and the concessive force comes from the negative in the main clause, not from any of them. Wijesekera's observation is that the concessive reading is available to the locative absolute where the genitive absolute would carry *anādara*, which, on Aggavaṁsa's definition of *anādara*, is the same fact stated twice, not a coincidence.

#### The agent, and the phrases with no subject at all

When the participle is passive, the *logical* subject can be added in the instrumental while the grammatical subject stays in the locative. That is how the Dhammacakkappavattana's closing sentences are built, and the instrumental is often the first sign that the locative is absolute, not local.

- **Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke** bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)
- *pavattite*<sub>a</sub> set rolling (nt. loc. sg., past part.) · *bhagavatā* by the Blessed One (m. ins. sg.) · *dhammacakke*<sub>a</sub> the wheel of Dhamma (nt. loc. sg.) · *bhummā devā* the earth-dwelling gods (m. nom. pl.) · *saddaṁ* a cry (m. acc. sg.) · *anussāvesuṁ* raised (3 pl. aor.)
- And **when the wheel of Dhamma had been set rolling by the Blessed One**, the earth-dwelling gods raised a cry.

At the other extreme the subject may be missing entirely, and the adverb *evaṁ* stands in its place.[^cls-stock] Two such phrases are so common that they are effectively single words: ***evaṁ vutte***, 'when this had been said', which introduces most of the replies in the canon; and ***evaṁ sante***, 'this being so', which introduces most of the traps.

[^cls-stock]: Wijesekera §186; Bodhi, "The locative absolute", p. 51, makes the same observation and adds the longest instance, seven chained passive present participles (*tathāgatena evaṁ ācikkhiyamāne desiyamāne paññapiyamāne paṭṭhapiyamāne vivariyamāne vibhajiyamāne uttānīkariyamāne*, SN 22.94 — S iii 139), where *evaṁ* governs all seven and the agent stands in the instrumental. The vowel of the third participle varies between editions: the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana reads *paññapiyamāne* here and three times elsewhere, while the Buddha Jayanti and the PTS print the long *paññāpiyamāne* throughout, as does Bodhi. The quotation follows the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, which is this book's text.

- **Evaṁ sante**, bho, na mayaṁ jānāma. (MN 93 — M ii 157)
- *evaṁ* thus, so · *sante* there being (m. loc. sg., pres. part. of *atthi*) · *bho* sir · *na mayaṁ jānāma* we do not know
- '**That being so**, sir, we do not know.'

### The genitive absolute

The same construction in the sixth case, and it is much rarer. Wijesekera calls it restricted "to a few standing phrases", and reports two conditions inherited from Sanskrit that Pāli mostly keeps: the noun is almost always a person or a personal pronoun, and the predicate has a durative sense: a present participle, or an adjective serving as one.[^cls-gen-conditions]

[^cls-gen-conditions]: Wijesekera §158, following Speyer's *Sanskrit Syntax* §369. Thānuttamo p. 71 confirms both conditions from Hendriksen and from Oberlies, and states the first more strongly: the agent of a genitive absolute is invariably a living being.

It is the construction that carries the Bodhisatta's going-forth, which is the passage the grammarians themselves reach for:

- So kho ahaṁ … **akāmakānaṁ mātāpitūnaṁ assumukhānaṁ rudantānaṁ** kesamassuṁ *ohāretvā* kāsāyāni vatthāni *acchādetvā* agārasmā anagāriyaṁ **pabbajiṁ**. (MN 26 — M i 163)
- *so kho ahaṁ* I, that one (m. nom. sg.) · *akāmakānaṁ*<sub>a</sub> unwilling (m. gen. pl.) · *mātāpitūnaṁ*<sub>a</sub> mother and father (m. gen. pl.) · *assumukhānaṁ*<sub>a</sub> with tear-stained faces (m. gen. pl.) · *rudantānaṁ*<sub>a</sub> weeping (m. gen. pl., pres. part.) · *kesamassuṁ* hair and beard (nt. acc. sg.) · *ohāretvā* having shaved off · *kāsāyāni vatthāni* ochre robes (nt. acc. pl.) · *acchādetvā* having put on · *agārasmā* from home (nt. abl. sg.) · *anagāriyaṁ* to homelessness (nt. acc. sg.) · *pabbajiṁ* I went forth (1 sg. aor.)
- And I, **though my mother and father were unwilling and were weeping with tear-stained faces**, shaved off my hair and beard, put on ochre robes, and **went forth** from home into homelessness.

That is *anādara* on the traditional analysis and 'although' on ours, and the two amount to the same thing: the parents' weeping and the going-forth are simultaneous, and the sentence declines to subordinate one to the other. Notice what else the sentence does. Four words in the genitive make a clause with its own subject; two absolutives carry the actions that share the main subject; and one aorist at the end carries the whole. Every device in this chapter is in that one sentence, which is one reason the passage is quoted so often by people who are not thinking about grammar at all.

The genitive absolute is not always concessive. In its plain temporal use it is interchangeable with the locative, and the clearest demonstration of that is a phrase which occurs in both cases:

- Atha kho tesaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ **acirapakkantassa bhagavato** ayamantarākathā udapādi. (DN 14 — D ii 8)
- *tesaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ* of those monks (m. gen. pl.) · *acirapakkantassa*<sub>a</sub> not long departed (m. gen. sg., past part. compounded with *acira* 'not long') · *bhagavato*<sub>a</sub> the Blessed One (m. gen. sg.) · *ayaṁ antarākathā* this conversation in the meantime (f. nom. sg.) · *udapādi* arose (3 sg. aor.)
- Then, **not long after the Blessed One had left**, this conversation arose among those monks.

- Atha kho māro pāpimā **acirapakkante āyasmante ānande** yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami. (DN 16 — D ii 104)
- *māro pāpimā* Māra the Evil One (m. nom. sg.) · *acirapakkante*<sub>a</sub> not long departed (m. loc. sg.) · *āyasmante ānande*<sub>a</sub> the venerable Ānanda (m. loc. sg.) · *yena … tena* to where · *upasaṅkami* he approached (3 sg. aor.)
- Then Māra the Evil One, **not long after the venerable Ānanda had left**, approached the Blessed One.

Same idiom, same participle, same sense, two cases. And the pair resolves something Wijesekera flagged and could not explain. He noticed that *pakkanta-* was the only past participle he could find in a genitive absolute, contrary to the durative rule, and left it as an exception.[^cls-acirapakkanta] Thānuttamo's refinement accounts for it: the genitive absolute admits a past participle as the final member of a compound, and *acira-pakkanta* is one. The exception turns out to be a second clause of the rule.

[^cls-acirapakkanta]: Wijesekera §158.c, who calls its use "a later development in Pāli … contrary to the general laws prevailing in Skr." He records the parallel locative at §183.c without drawing the conclusion.

### The accusative absolute

Rarer again, and confined in practice to one participle. *Santaṁ*, the accusative singular of the present participle of *atthi*, appears with a noun in the accusative, and the sense is the ordinary absolute sense of a state obtaining while something else is the case.

- **Santaṁ yeva kho pana paraṁ lokaṁ** 'natthi paro loko' ti saṅkappeti. (MN 60 — M i 402)
- *santaṁ*<sub>a</sub> existing (m. acc. sg., pres. part. of *atthi*) · *yeva* indeed · *paraṁ lokaṁ*<sub>a</sub> the other world (m. acc. sg.) · *natthi paro loko* there is no other world · *ti* end of quotation · *saṅkappeti* he forms the thought
- **Although the other world does exist**, he forms the thought 'there is no other world'.

Two facts support it that no argument from frequency could. The first is that the commentary names it. Buddhaghosa, glossing the Poṭṭhapāda Sutta's *evaṁ santaṁ*, writes: *evaṁ santan ti evaṁ sante. Bhummatthe hi etaṁ upayogavacanaṁ*: "'*evaṁ santaṁ*' means '*evaṁ sante*'; for this is an accusative in the sense of the locative."[^cls-buddhaghosa-acc] He does what a grammarian does: he identifies an unexpected ending by naming the ending it stands for. And the second is that the accusative phrase has a locative twin in the same stock formula, *evaṁ sante* beside *evaṁ santaṁ*, which is the same demonstration the genitive gave above.

[^cls-buddhaghosa-acc]: Sumaṅgalavilāsinī on DN 9 — D i 186, at Sv II 376; the sutta text is *Evaṁ santaṁ kho te, poṭṭhapāda, aññāva saññā bhavissati añño attā*. The formula *bhummatthe upayogavacanaṁ* is a standing one in the commentaries and is used of ordinary accusatives elsewhere. Wijesekera §56 reports this gloss and it is the strongest evidence for the construction.

> **Grammars differ on the accusative absolute.** Wijesekera §56 reads *santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ byāpādaṁ 'atthi me ajjhattaṁ byāpādo' ti pajānāti* (MN 10 — M i 60) as a probable accusative absolute: 'there being inward ill-will, he knows that there is inward ill-will'. Bodhi reads the parallel line as an ordinary direct object with a participle agreeing with it: 'he understands the lust for forms existing internally'. Both readings fit the Pāli, and the commentary supports each in a different place: *bhummatthe* at DN 9, but at DN 4 it glosses *tathā santaṁ yeva* as *itthambhūtākhyānatthe upayogavacanaṁ*, an accusative describing a state of being, and resolves it with a genitive. The safe position is that *santaṁ* + accusative is sometimes absolute and sometimes not, and that the main verb decides: where the accusative could be its object, it probably is.

It should also be said that no premodern Pāli grammarian classified the accusative with the locative and the genitive here. Collins is careful about this, and he is right to be: the accusative absolute and the instrumental "semi-absolute" are modern Western categories laid over Pāli, not divisions the tradition made.[^cls-collins-modern] That does not make them wrong. It does mean that if you do not find them in Kaccāyana, you have missed nothing.

[^cls-collins-modern]: Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students*, p. 39: "No premodern Pāli grammarian classified any usages of the instrumental and accusative with locative and genitive as *bhāvalakkhaṇa* or *anādara*." His instrumental examples (*Somhi etarahi rūḷhena vaṇena sañchavinā arogo sukhī*, MN 101 — M ii 216, 'now that the wound has healed and is covered with skin I am well and happy') read equally well as instrumentals of attendant circumstance, which he says.

### The nominative absolute, which is probably not one

Three sources in this collection report a nominative absolute in Pāli. Duroiselle asserts one and gives no examples.[^cls-duroiselle-nom] The *Niruttidīpanī* claims one, and the tradition's other grammars make the same claim in the same words (*Kvaci paṭhamāpi bahulaṁ dissati*, 'sometimes the nominative too is frequently seen'), with two verses from the Jātaka to prove it. Wijesekera and Collins both report a handful of Nikāya instances while hedging about them. It needs settling carefully, because nowhere else in this chapter do the sources and the evidence come apart.

[^cls-duroiselle-nom]: Duroiselle §603.ii. Wijesekera §26 notes the assertion and the absence of examples.

The tradition's two verses do not show what they are offered for. They are canonical (*Gacchanto so bhāradvājo, addassa accutaṁ isiṁ* and *Yāyamāno mahārājā, addā sīdantare nage*, both in the Jātaka), and the grammarians quote them with small slips of their own.[^cls-jataka-verses] But in each of them the nominative participle agrees with the subject of the main verb: it is Bhāradvāja who is going and Bhāradvāja who sees the sage. That is an ordinary participle qualifying a subject, and it is not detached from anything. Wijesekera makes this objection and Collins makes it independently; modern grammar, he says, "would see the participles simply as adjectives qualifying the subject of the main clause."

[^cls-jataka-verses]: The verses are in the Jātaka; the grammarians print *addasā* where the canon reads *addassa*, and *so* where the canon reads *te* in the third of their stock verses. The claim itself, with both verses, is in the *padamālā* of the *Saddanīti*.

The Nikāya evidence comes down to one word, and the editions disagree about it. The sentence is DN 1's account of the world contracting, and all three sources rest their case on it. Read the editions side by side:

| Edition | DN 1 |
|---|---|
| Pali Text Society, D I 17 | ***Saṁvaṭṭamāno loko*** yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvaṭṭanikā honti |
| Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana | *Saṁvaṭṭamāne loke* yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvattanikā honti |
| Buddha Jayanti | *Saṁvaṭṭamāne loke* yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvattanikā honti |

The nominative is one edition's reading against two, and *saṁvaṭṭamāno* occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana in any of its four layers. The Burmese and Sinhalese editions read a perfectly ordinary locative absolute ('when the world is contracting, beings are for the most part reborn in the world of streaming radiance'), and they read it at DN 24 and DN 27 as well, in the same words.

And the Pali Text Society reads the locative everywhere else itself. Across its volumes *saṁvaṭṭamāne* stands eight times (three in Aṅguttara IV, two in Aṅguttara V, two in Dīgha III at the parallels to this passage, one in the Itivuttaka) against the single nominative at Dīgha I 17.[^cls-samvattamano] So this is one word in one volume, against the same edition's practice everywhere the sentence recurs, not an editorial policy about the construction.

[^cls-samvattamano]: Counted over all three romanised editions. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's own apparatus does not record the PTS reading at this line. One practical point, for anyone counting in a transcription of a printed volume: such transcriptions keep the line-end hyphenation of the page, so Dīgha I 17 stands as 'Saṁ-' and 'vaṭṭamāno' on two lines, and a search for the whole word fails on a page that carries it. The hyphenated halves have to be rejoined before anything is counted.

The second Nikāya instance is not a variant reading at all. Collins offers DN 19, "when Govinda the brahmin died king Disampati grieved", as a place where some manuscripts have a nominative where others have a locative. Every edition prints the locative (*Govinde brāhmaṇe kālaṅkate rājā disampati paridevesi*, DN 19 — D ii 231, with *kālakate* in the PTS and the Buddha Jayanti), and the nominative *Govindo brāhmaṇo kālakato* is twenty lines away, inside the king's quoted lament, where it is an ordinary verbless sentence with no absolute about it. Two forms, two functions, and no variation.

So the summary is short. The nominative absolute is not established for Pāli. Every case offered for it either has a competing reading in another edition or admits an ordinary analysis, and Wijesekera, who wanted it to exist and reached for a plausible explanation in the anacoluthon of long spoken sentences, conceded that "it is not a regular construction in either Pāli or Sanskrit."[^cls-wij-nom] What a reader meets often, and should not confuse with it, is the semi-absolute nominative with *samāna-* or *santa-*: *daharo va samāno*, 'while still young', where the participle does agree with the subject and the temporal sense is real. That is in the Bodhisatta's going-forth quoted above, four words before the genitive absolute, and telling the two apart in one sentence is as good a test of this chapter as any.

[^cls-wij-nom]: Wijesekera §26, and §26.b for the *samāna-* construction, which he calls semi-absolute for the reason given here.

### Conditional sentences

A conditional has two parts: the clause carrying the condition, called the **protasis**, and the clause carrying its consequence, the **apodosis**. Pāli marks the protasis with *sace*, with the enclitic *ce*, or with *yadi*; *ce* never begins a sentence, because it has no accent of its own.[^cls-conditional-particles] The mood of the verbs decides the *kind* of conditional, not the particle, and there are three kinds.

[^cls-conditional-particles]: Perniola §308, pp. 396–399. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana writes *yaṁ nūna* where the Buddha Jayanti writes the assimilated *yannūna*, an editorial difference, not a grammatical one; the enclitic behaviour of *ce* and its neighbours is treated in the chapter on indeclinables.

**A real condition** ('if you listen, you will understand') puts both verbs in the indicative, present or future, and the apodosis may be an imperative instead.

- **Sace** te **agaru**, **bhāsassū** ti. (DN 21 — D ii 284)
- *sace* if · *te* to you (dat. sg.) · *agaru* not burdensome (nom. sg., *na* + *garu*) · *bhāsassu* speak! (2 sg. imp. mid. of *bhāsati*) · *ti* end of quotation
- '**If** it is **no trouble** to you, **speak**.'

The protasis there has no verb at all (*hoti* is understood, as it so often is), and the apodosis is an imperative. That is the ordinary form of a request in the canon.

**A possible condition** puts the optative in both clauses. This is much the commonest type in the suttas, and once a reader has met it a few times the pair of optatives becomes the surest signal in the sentence.

- Sace maṁ bhagavā **anujāneyya**, **āgaccheyyāhaṁ** imaṁ ambavanaṁ padhānāya. (AN 9.3 — A iv 355)
- *sace* if · *maṁ* me (acc. sg.) · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *anujāneyya* were to permit (3 sg. opt.) · *āgaccheyyaṁ* I would come (1 sg. opt.) · *ahaṁ* I · *imaṁ ambavanaṁ* to this mango grove (nt. acc. sg.) · *padhānāya* for striving (nt. dat. sg.)
- **If the Blessed One were to allow me**, **I would come** to this mango grove to strive.

Meghiya is floating a request, not reporting a fact, and the two optatives say so. *Sace* alone would not have: it introduces real conditions too.

**An unreal condition** ('if you had listened, you would have understood') is the third, and only here does the conditional tense, whose forms the chapter on tenses and moods sets out, have a settled use in the language. The false premise goes into the conditional, usually *abhavissa*; the consequence goes into the optative. And then, characteristically, the Pāli does not leave the false premise standing: it answers it with *yasmā … tasmā*, 'because … therefore', restoring the true state of affairs.[^cls-unreal]

[^cls-unreal]: Perniola §308.III, pp. 398–399; Bodhi, "Conditional sentences", p. 49, who identifies the *yasmā*/*tasmā* answer as the regular sequel. The formation of the conditional is in the chapter on tenses and moods, which has few occasions to exemplify it; this is the occasion.

- **No cetaṁ**, bhikkhave, **abhavissa** ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, nayidha jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ **paññāyetha**. **Yasmā ca kho**, bhikkhave, **atthi** ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, **tasmā** jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ **paññāyati**. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 80)
- *no ce* if not · *etaṁ* this (nt. nom. sg.) · *abhavissa* were to be (3 sg. cond.) · *ajātaṁ* unborn · *abhūtaṁ* unbecome · *akataṁ* unmade · *asaṅkhataṁ* unconditioned (all nt. nom. sg.) · *na idha* not here · *jātassa* of the born (nt. gen. sg.) · *nissaraṇaṁ* escape (nt. nom. sg.) · *paññāyetha* would be discerned (3 sg. opt. mid. of the passive *paññāyati*) · *yasmā ca kho* but because · *atthi* there is · *tasmā* therefore · *paññāyati* is discerned
- **If there were no** unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, **no escape** from the born, become, made and conditioned **would be discerned** here. **But because there is** an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, **therefore** an escape from the born, become, made and conditioned **is discerned**.

Two conditionals, two optatives and two indicatives, and the doctrine rests on the difference between them. The grammar is central to that passage: its whole force is that the first sentence is contrary to fact and the second is not, and only the mood of the verbs says so.

A relative clause carries a condition too, and most of the Pātimokkha is built that way. A rule states its case as a generic relative with an optative, and its consequence as briefly as the class of offence allows, often in a clause with no verb in it at all. The nuns' rule about garlic runs to six words:

- **Yā pana bhikkhunī** lasuṇaṁ **khādeyya**, **pācittiyaṁ**. (Bi Pc 1 — Vin iv 259)
- *yā* whatever (f. nom. sg.) · *pana* (filler) · *bhikkhunī* nun (f. nom. sg.) · *lasuṇaṁ* garlic (nt. acc. sg.) · *khādeyya* should eat (3 sg. opt.) · *pācittiyaṁ* an offence requiring confession (nt. nom. sg.)
- "**Whatever nun should eat** garlic: **an offence requiring confession**."

There is no *sace*, no *ce* and no *yadi*. The optative carries the condition by itself, and the relative carries the generality: *yā pana bhikkhunī* is any nun there may be, not some particular nun. The consequence then arrives as a single noun, with *hoti* understood as it was in *appamādo amatapadaṁ* at the head of this chapter.[^cls-pacittiya] A rule is a conditional with the 'if' left to a mood and the 'then' left to a case ending.

[^cls-pacittiya]: DPD, s.v. *pācittiya*, neuter, '(vinaya) offence requiring confession'. The junction this rule depends on (an optative in *-eyya* followed directly by *pācittiya-*) stands 115 times in the Vinaya's root texts, counted as a phrase.

A locative absolute can also carry a condition without any particle at all, and Bodhi notes a case where the situation is so unlikely that 'if' translates better than 'when':

- **Āditte**, bhikkhave, **cele vā sīse vā** kimassa karaṇīyan ti? (SN 56.34 — S v 440)
- *āditte*<sub>a</sub> ablaze (nt. loc. sg., past part.) · *cele*<sub>a</sub> cloth, garment (nt. loc. sg.) · *vā … vā* either … or · *sīse*<sub>a</sub> head (nt. loc. sg.) · *kiṁ* what · *assa* would be (3 sg. opt. of *atthi*) · *karaṇīyaṁ* to be done (nt. nom. sg., fpp) · *ti* end of question
- '**If one's turban or one's head were ablaze**, monks, what should be done?'

### The simile, and the sentence that answers it

The discourses make constant use of similes, and the simile has a fixed shape: ***seyyathāpi***, 'just as', opens the first term, and ***evam eva*** or ***evam evaṁ***, 'just so', opens the second. The verbs in the first term are usually optative, because the case is imagined, not reported.[^cls-simile]

[^cls-simile]: Perniola §306.a, p. 392; Bodhi, "Similes", p. 52, who notes the optative convention. Perniola adds three further ways of building the second term: with the correlative adverbs *yathā … tathā*, with a *bahubbīhi* in *-upama*, and with *seyyo* … *yañ ce* for a comparison of superiority.

- **Seyyathāpi**, bhante, purisassa agāraṁ ekādasadvāraṁ, so tasmiṁ **agāre āditte** ekamekenapi dvārena **sakkuṇeyya** attānaṁ sotthiṁ kātuṁ. (MN 52 — M i 353)
- *seyyathāpi* just as · *purisassa* of a man (m. gen. sg.) · *agāraṁ* a house (nt. nom. sg.) · *ekādasadvāraṁ* with eleven doors (nt. nom. sg., *bahubbīhi*) · *so* he (m. nom. sg.) · *tasmiṁ agāre* that house (nt. loc. sg.) · *āditte* ablaze (nt. loc. sg., past part.) · *ekamekena dvārena* by any one door (nt. ins. sg.) · *sakkuṇeyya* would be able (3 sg. opt.) · *attānaṁ* himself (m. acc. sg.) · *sotthiṁ kātuṁ* to make safe
- **Just as**, sir, if a man had a house with eleven doors, and **that house were ablaze**, he **would be able** to get himself to safety by any one of the doors.

Three of this chapter's constructions are in that one sentence: the simile frame, a locative absolute carrying a condition, and an infinitive of purpose. It is also a good measure of how much of the grammar a reader has to have available to follow one ordinary sentence of the canon at reading speed.

### Quotation, and the *ti* that is not one

Where English says 'he knew that the cat was in the house', Pāli almost always quotes: the thought is given as direct speech and closed with ***ti***.[^cls-ti] The construction belongs with the indeclinables and is treated there; what belongs here are the two things it does at the level of the clause.

[^cls-ti]: Perniola §307, p. 395, who notes that it is nearly always *ti*, not *iti*; Bodhi, "Embedded direct speech", pp. 51–52. The sandhi that *ti* produces on the preceding word (the lengthened vowel, the assimilated final niggahīta) is in the chapter on sandhi, and the particle itself in the chapter on indeclinables.

The first is that Pāli does have a real indirect statement, and it is easy to miss because it looks like an ordinary object: a noun or pronoun in the accusative with a participle agreeing with it, the two together forming the object of the main verb. *Addasā kho nigrodho paribbājako sandhānaṁ gahapatiṁ dūratova āgacchantaṁ* (DN 25 — D iii 37) is not 'the wanderer Nigrodha saw the householder Sandhāna from afar, coming', but 'the wanderer Nigrodha saw **that** the householder Sandhāna **was coming** from afar'.[^cls-indirect] The participle carries what English would put in a subordinate clause.

[^cls-indirect]: Perniola's own example is *addasā bhagavantaṁ dūrato va āgacchantaṁ*, which he prints twice: at §307, p. 395, uncited and without the *va*, and again at §309.a, p. 399, with it and cited to MN II 2:2. The wording quoted here is the second. The construction is why a reader should be slow to detach an accusative participle from its accusative noun, and the same ambiguity makes the accusative absolute hard to identify.

The second is that a *ti* clause need not be a quotation at all. It can carry **purpose**: an imperative or optative closed with *ti* states the aim for the sake of which the main action is done.

- '**Pasaṁsā me āgacchatū**' ti sīlaṁ rakkheyya paṇḍito. (Iti 76 — It p. 67)
- *pasaṁsā* praise (f. nom. sg.) · *me* to me (dat./gen. sg.) · *āgacchatu* let it come (3 sg. imp.) · *ti* end of quotation · *sīlaṁ* virtue (nt. acc. sg.) · *rakkheyya* would guard (3 sg. opt.) · *paṇḍito* the wise one (m. nom. sg.)
- The wise one would guard his virtue **so that praise might come to him**.

Read as a quotation that sentence is nonsense, since nobody is speaking. Read as a purpose clause it is exact, and it is the ordinary Pāli way of saying 'in order that'.[^cls-ti-purpose]

[^cls-ti-purpose]: Perniola §311.a, p. 404, who cites it as Itv 67:6; the sutta is Itivuttaka 76. His §311.b gives two more (the infinitive and the dative of an action noun in *-na*, *nadiṁ gamissāma sināyituṁ* and *bhagavantaṁ dassanāya upasaṅkami*), which are treated in the chapters on infinitives and on case syntax respectively; and his §311.c a fourth, a *tappurisa* built on an action noun with *atthaṁ* in the accusative, *nayidaṁ brahmacariyaṁ vussati janakuhanatthaṁ*, which belongs with the compounds.

### What is left, and where it is

Four kinds of subordinate clause remain, and all four are built the same way: a relative word in the subordinate clause answered by a demonstrative in the main one. That correlative construction is the next chapter's subject, and this section is a map, not a treatment.[^cls-correlatives]

[^cls-correlatives]: Perniola §§310–314, pp. 401–407, treats all four; the *ya-*/*ta-* forms themselves, with their paradigms, are in the chapters on pronouns and on relative clauses.

| Kind | Introduced by | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| Time | *yadā*, *yāva*, *yasmiṁ samaye* | *tadā*, *tāva*, *atha*, *tasmiṁ samaye* |
| Cause | *yasmā*, *yato*, *yatvādhikaraṇaṁ* | *tasmā*, *tato* |
| Result | *yathā*, *yena*, *yatra hi nāma* | *tathā*, *yāva*, *sādhu* |
| Concession | *api* or *pi*, alone or with *ce* | — |

Three notes on that table, because each hides something a reader will meet.

*Yatvādhikaraṇaṁ* looks like a rarity and is not. It occurs eighty-five times in the root texts, and never as a separate word: the canon has ***yatvādhikaraṇamenaṁ***, joined to the pronoun that follows, in the formula of sense restraint. A search for the bare form will suggest it belongs to the grammarians.

*Yatra hi nāma* takes a future and carries astonishment, not result. The monks at DN 14, in the conversation this chapter quoted for the genitive absolute, exclaim at the Buddha's power of recollection: *yatra hi nāma tathāgato atīte buddhe parinibbute … jātitopi anussarissati* (DN 14 — D ii 8), '**that** the Realised One should recollect, of past Buddhas who have attained final Nibbāna, even their birth'. The future there is not future time, and there is a locative absolute inside it.[^cls-yatra]

And the concessive clause has no conjunction at all. It is marked by *pi* or *api* attached to a word in a clause whose verb is usually a participle: *Chinnopi rukkho punareva rūhati* (Dhp 338 — Dhp p. 49), 'though the tree is cut, it grows again'. The relation is left for the reader to supply, which is the habit this whole chapter has been describing.

[^cls-yatra]: Perniola §312, pp. 404–405, for the construction. His own illustration of it, *yāva pāpo ayaṁ devadatto alakkhiko, yatra hi nāma samaṇassa gotamassa vadhāya parakkamissati*, does not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana in that wording; the DN 14 sentence is given here in its place.

### Where clause syntax is treated elsewhere

**The particles** (*na* and *mā* and the moods they take, *ca* and *vā* and *pi*, the interrogatives *nu kho*, *kacci*, *nanu* and *api nu*, and *iti* with its sandhi) are each treated in the chapter on indeclinables, where their forms and their enclitic behaviour belong. This chapter has added only their effect on a whole clause.

**The relative correlatives** (the whole *ya-*/*ta-* apparatus, and the adjectival clause built with *yo … so*) are the next chapter's, and it treats them with paradigms.

The participles and the absolutive, whose forms every construction here depends on, are in the chapters on participles and on absolutives and infinitives. This chapter uses them and derives none of them; where it names a form as a present participle or a past passive one, that is the claim those chapters establish.

**The cases themselves** are in the chapter on case syntax, which left the absolutes here on purpose, on the ground that an absolute construction is a dependent clause and a reader meets it as one. It kept the observation this chapter has spent its middle third unfolding: these constructions are named after their case because only the case marks them off, and when the case will not decide, no other word in the sentence will.

### Where to start in a long sentence

Begin with the finite verbs. They are few, and each is a place the sentence could stop. Then take every absolutive as an action finished before the next, by the same subject, and every participle standing in an oblique case with its own noun as a clause of its own, joined to the rest by nothing but that case. Where no finite verb is printed, supply *hoti* and see whether the sentence closes. Every remaining word is read against one of these. The next chapter ends by doing that to a single sentence, word by word.
