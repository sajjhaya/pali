## Verbs: The Present System

A **finite verb** is one that inflects for person: it has separate forms for 'I', 'you', 'he'. Everything else in the verbal system is **infinite**: the participles, which inflect for gender, case and number but not for person, and the absolutives and infinitives, which do not inflect at all. Those are Part IV's. This part is about the forms that carry a person, and this chapter is about the three of them that are built on one stem.

How much of the Old Indic verb Pāli has let go is the first thing to establish, because a reader arriving from Sanskrit will otherwise spend some time looking for things that are not there. The dual is gone completely. The subjunctive and the precative are gone from the moods.[^no-subjunctive] The middle voice survives only in scattered forms, and its old work has largely passed to the causative. The perfect has been reduced to two or three stray forms in the whole canon.[^stray-perfect] What is left is a system a reader can hold in the head: three tenses (present, past and future) and four moods, of which the indicative, the imperative and the optative are formed on the present stem and belong here.

[^no-subjunctive]: Oberlies §44 (p. 199): "The subjunctive and the precative are missing from the moods", with a remark at §46.2 on what survives of the Old Indic subjunctive in a handful of imperative forms.

[^stray-perfect]: Perniola §61 (p. 74): "Further there are in the Pali Canon two or three stray forms of the perfect tense. In the postcanonical literature these forms are more numerous." Oberlies §44, p. 199, reaches the same place from the other direction, treating imperfect, aorist and perfect as having collapsed into a single **preterite**, and so they are handled together in the next chapter, not as three tenses.

### The present stem

Almost everything in this part follows from one fact, and it is a fact about Pāli, not about how Pāli is taught. In Old Indic the root was the centre of the verb and every form was derived from it. In Pāli the root has lost that place. Oberlies states it clearly: "The focus of the verbal conjugation is no longer the root but the present stem, i.e. the 3sg. of the indicative without the ending *-ti*. All regular verb forms are based on it; forms that are not so constructed are chiefly historical relics."[^stem-focus]

[^stem-focus]: Oberlies §44, p. 200. The claim is historical and structural at once, and it is what licenses a reference grammar to teach the stem before the root. Thānuttamo p. 86 reaches the same practical rule from the tradition, quoting Collins p. 79: "Pāḷi follows the convention that it — generally — forms the present indicative, the imperative and the optative from the active stem and everything else from the root." That sentence is the reason this chapter contains the imperative and the optative and the next one does not.

That gives the reader a procedure, not a fact to memorise. A dictionary gives a verb in its third person singular, *gacchati* 'goes'. Remove the *-ti* and what is left is the stem, *gaccha-*, and the stem is what every form in this chapter is built on:

| Dictionary form | Stem | Gloss |
|---|---|---|
| *gacchati* | *gaccha-* | goes |
| *deseti* | *dese-* | teaches |
| *karoti* | *karo-* | does, makes |
| *jānāti* | *jānā-* | knows |
| *suṇāti* | *suṇā-* | hears |

Look down the stem column and one feature is immediately visible: the stems end either in a short *-a* or in a long vowel. A reader needs no other division. Oberlies distinguishes two conjugations on this basis: one, much the larger, with the stem in *-a*; the other with the stem in a long vowel, "most commonly *-e*, fairly often *-ā*, rarely *-ī* or *-o*".[^two-conjugations] The rare *-ū* type is a handful of forms of one verb, *brūti* 'says', which a reader meets often enough in verse to recognise and never needs to conjugate.

[^two-conjugations]: Oberlies §44, p. 201. His "second" conjugation is the old athematic presents, made uniform by suppressing the alternations that Old Indic had between strong and weak stems, so *sunomi … sunoma*, *jahāmi … jahanti* with one stem where Sanskrit had two. The *-o* type is *karoti* and a very few others; the *-ī* type is smaller still.

#### Why the stems differ: vowel gradation

The stems do not differ arbitrarily, and two mechanisms account for nearly all of it. The first is **vowel gradation**: the systematic lengthening of a root vowel that runs through the whole of Pāli word-formation, nominal as well as verbal.[^gradation-placement] A root vowel appears in one of three grades:

[^gradation-placement]: Perniola makes vowel gradation §60 (pp. 73–74), inside his verb chapter and two sections ahead of the conjugation classes, which open on p. 75, because there the phenomenon has visible effects. Ñāṇatusita likewise sets his gradation table at the head of the verb sheet, and so Appendix A.3 opens with it. It is truly a fact about phonology, and it is taught here, not in Part I, because in Part I there is nothing yet for it to explain.

| Zero grade | *Guṇa* grade | *Vuddhi* grade |
|---|---|---|
| *i*, *ī* | *e*, *ay* | *āy*, *e* |
| *u*, *ū* | *o*, *av* | *āv*, *o* |
| *a* (or none) | *a* | *ā* |

The *guṇa* 'quality' grade adds an *a* to the vowel; the *vuddhi* 'growth' grade adds another. So the root *bhū-* 'be' in the *guṇa* grade is *bhav-*, giving *bhavati* 'is'; *jut-* 'shine' gives *jotati* 'shines'; *nī-* 'lead' gives *nayati* 'leads'. Push the same roots to the *vuddhi* grade and they yield the causatives of Chapter 9: *bhāveti* 'develops', literally 'causes to be', and *kāreti* 'makes another do'. The vowel *a* is already at the *guṇa* grade and so does not change there, and so the largest class of verbs shows no gradation at all.[^perniola-gradation]

[^perniola-gradation]: Perniola §60 (p. 73), with the worked series *lih-* → *lehati* 'licks', *jut-* → *jotati*, *bhū-* → *bhavati*, *kṛ-* → *karoti*. He adds the limiting rule at p. 74: only a final vowel, long or short, and a medial short vowel followed by one consonant are liable to be raised. So *jīv-* stays *jīvati* (the medial vowel is long by nature) and *nind-* stays *nindati* (long by position).

#### Why the stems differ: the conjugation classes

The second mechanism is that most verbs insert something between the root and the ending. The tradition calls these class affixes *vikaraṇa*, and sorts every root in the language by which one it takes. *Suṇāti* has its long *-ā-* because the root *su-* 'hear' takes the affix *-ṇā-*; *jhāyati* has its *-ya-*; *muñcati* has a nasal pushed inside the root itself.

| Class | Name | Affix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *bhūvādigaṇa* | *-a-* | *pacati* 'cooks' |
| 2 | *rudhādigaṇa* | nasal infix + *-a-* | *muñcati* 'releases' |
| 3 | *divādigaṇa* | *-ya-* | *jhāyati* 'meditates, burns' |
| 4 | *svādigaṇa* | *-ṇu-*, *-ṇā-*, *-uṇā-* | *suṇāti* 'hears' |
| 5 | *kiyādigaṇa* | *-nā-* | *jānāti* 'knows' |
| 6 | *gahādigaṇa* | *-ppa-*, *-nhā-* | *gaṇhāti* 'takes' |
| 7 | *tanādigaṇa* | *-o-*, *-yirā-* | *karoti* 'does' |
| 8 | *curādigaṇa* | *-e-*, *-aya-* | *deseti* 'teaches' |

The grammars do not agree on how many classes there are, and the disagreement instructs more than it troubles. Kaccāyana and the *Saddanīti* give the eight above, each with its own rule; the *Padarūpasiddhi* folds the sixth into the fifth and gets seven; Perniola gives ten, because he follows the Sanskrit division and says so ("the Pali verbal system is directly connected with the Sanskrit verbal system; hence we can follow the Sanskrit division of the verbs into classes"); Warder arrives at seven on a different cut again.[^class-counts] They count the same forms and divide them at different joints, as happens whenever a classification is imposed on a continuum that has been eroding for centuries. This book does not depend on the number.

[^class-counts]: Thānuttamo pp. 85–87 for the eightfold scheme, with Kacc 445–452 against the classes in order, and p. 87 n. 133 for the observation, quoting Warder p. 79, that "sometimes it is not easy to decide whether to class a verb as an independent seventh conjugation root or as the causative form of some other verb of perhaps widely divergent meaning." Perniola's ten are at §62 ff. (p. 75). The parallel with the three-way disagreement about the size of the alphabet, in Chapter 1, is exact: a traditional enumeration, a Western one, and a difference of framework, not of fact.

What the classes are *for*, in a book about reading, is to stop a reader being surprised. *Suṇāti*, *jānāti*, *karoti* and *deseti* look nothing like each other and behave identically once the stem is isolated. The class explains the stem; the stem is what carries the endings; the endings are the same for all of them.

### Voice

Before the paradigms, one distinction that Appendix A.3 presents in a second column and that the reader will otherwise take for a misprint.

Pāli inherits two sets of personal endings. The tradition calls them *parassapada*, 'the word for another', and *attanopada*, 'the word for oneself': what Western grammars call the **active** and the **middle**.[^pada-terms] In principle the middle marks an action that returns to the agent, done for oneself instead of passing to something else. In practice it barely survives. Oberlies says the middle "survives only in some forms" and that its function has been partly taken over by the causative; Duroiselle is blunter, and describes what a reader will find:

[^pada-terms]: Thānuttamo pp. 82–84, following Kacc 406–407, where the active endings are the "former six personal suffixes" (*pubbakāni cha padāni*) and the middle the "latter six" (*parāni cha padāni*): a purely positional description, as befits their place in a traditional table.

> It must here be remarked that the Reflective Voice has lost very much of its importance, and that the distinction between Active and Reflective has been almost if not altogether effaced, and that the choice between the Active or Reflective is mostly determined now by metrical exigencies.

So the middle is largely a verse form, chosen for its sound, not its sense, and the reader's practical need is only to recognise it. A third singular in *-te* where *-ti* was expected, a first plural in *-mhe* or *-māse*, a third plural in *-re*: these are middle endings, not errors.

- Pare ca na vijānanti, mayamettha **yamāmase**. (MN 128 — M iii 154)
- *pare* others (m. nom. pl.) · *ca* and · *na* not · *vijānanti* understand · *mayaṁ* we · *ettha* here · *yamāmase* let us restrain ourselves (1 pl. imperative middle)[^yamamase-sense]
- Others do not understand that here **we must restrain ourselves**.

Whatever *yamāmase* means (and its grammar is settled where its sense is not), it is the middle voice, first person plural, and it is a verse line, which is where such forms live.[^yamamase] The same verse is Dhammapada 6, and whoever has met it in either place has met the *attanopada*, named or not.

[^yamamase]: The form is canonical, with six occurrences in the root texts. The neighbouring *saṁyamāmase* and *bhaṇāmase* at SN 1 show the same ending on two further verbs, likewise in verse. The simple present *yamati* is **not** attested in the root texts at all: this verb reaches the canon in this one form and in the compound *saṁyamāmase*, which is a caution about reading a dictionary headword as though it were a word one will meet.

[^yamamase-sense]: The sense is contested, and the rendering above is the translators', not the commentaries'. Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi render the MN 128 line "That here we should restrain ourselves". The commentary on that verse reads it the other way: *mayaṁ yamāmase … nassāma satataṁ samitaṁ maccusantikaṁ gacchāmā*, "we perish, we go continually into the presence of death" (Papañcasūdanī on MN 128, Ps IV 205). The Dhammapada commentary on Dhp 6 says the same with *uparamāma vinassāma* (Dhp-a I 65), and then offers a second reading, *yamāmase bhaṇḍanādīnaṁ vuddhiyā vāyamāmā*, 'we strive for the growth of quarrels and the like' (Dhp-a I 66). DPD gives the headword *yamati* the meaning 'dies', citing this very verse, which is the commentaries' first reading. The root √yam leans towards restraint: its Pāli derivatives are overwhelmingly the vocabulary of restraint (*saṁyama*, *saññata*, *vāyamati* 'makes effort'), and no other word in the family means 'die'. This chapter does not depend on the choice, since the form is a first person plural middle either way, and the disagreement is recorded here, not resolved.

There is a second distinction that is easy to run together with this one and must not be. Voice in the sense of active against passive is carried by the *stem*, not by the endings. A passive stem is made with an affix *-ya-* and then takes whatever personal endings it needs, active-looking ones included. That is Chapter 9's subject; what matters here is that *vuccati* 'is called' has an ordinary-looking *-ti* and is passive, so the endings alone will never tell a reader whether a verb is active or passive.[^stem-carries-voice]

[^stem-carries-voice]: Thānuttamo p. 81, citing Oberlies (2019) p. 555 and Warder p. 51: "it is the stem which indicates if a verb is active or passive … and not the two sets of personal voice markers." He adds the traditional analysis of the mismatch, in which an active-looking ending on a passive stem is a "reversed *attanopada*" form: historically, a middle ending that has taken active dress. It is a tidy account of a real oddity, and a reader needs only its consequence.

### The present indicative

The endings, active and middle, are these:

| Person | Active | Middle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-mi* | *-e* |
| 2 sg. | *-si* | *-se* |
| 3 sg. | *-ti* | *-te* |
| 1 pl. | *-ma* | *-mhe*, *-mase* |
| 2 pl. | *-tha* | *-vhe* |
| 3 pl. | *-nti* | *-nte*, *-re* |

Perniola calls these the **primary** endings, and the term is useful across the whole of Part III: the primary endings serve the present indicative *and* the future, the secondary endings serve the aorist and the conditional, the optative takes both, and the imperative has its own.[^primary-secondary] Hold that rule and the future in the next chapter is simply the present endings on a different stem, not a new paradigm to learn.

[^primary-secondary]: Perniola §57 (p. 71), which sets out the four sets (primary and secondary, each in an active and a middle column) and then assigns them: "The present indicative and the future take the primary personal endings. The aorist and the conditional take the secondary personal endings. The optative takes both the primary and the secondary personal endings. The imperative takes special endings." It is the single most economical page any source consulted has offered on the Pāli verb.

Attached to a stem in *-a*, the active endings give:

|     | singular | plural |
|-----|----------|--------|
| 1st | *gacchāmi* 'I go' | *gacchāma* 'we go' |
| 2nd | *gacchasi* 'you go' | *gacchatha* 'you go' |
| 3rd | *gacchati* 'he, she, it goes' | *gacchanti* 'they go' |

Two changes to the stem vowel are visible there, and neither is arbitrary. **The stem vowel lengthens before *-mi* and *-ma***, so the two first-person forms have *gacchā-* where the rest have *gaccha-*.[^kacc-478] And in the third plural the stem vowel is absorbed into the ending, so that a stem in a long vowel appears with a short one: *jānāti* but *jānanti*, *suṇāti* but *suṇanti*. These alternations are a rule and not an accident of the verbs, and it applies everywhere in the chapter, the imperative and the optative included.

[^kacc-478]: Kacc 478, reported at Thānuttamo p. 90, which gives the lengthening before *-hi*, *-mi* and *-ma*; and Duroiselle p. 154 for the complementary rule that the stem vowel drops before an ending that begins with a vowel. The same two rules account for the imperative *gacchāhi* beside *gacchatu* below.

The three other stem types take the same endings without further comment:

|     | *dese-* | *karo-* | *jānā-* |
|-----|---------|---------|---------|
| 1 sg. | *desemi* | *karomi* | *jānāmi* |
| 2 sg. | *desesi* | *karosi* | *jānāsi* |
| 3 sg. | *deseti* | *karoti* | *jānāti* |
| 1 pl. | *desema* | *karoma* | *jānāma* |
| 2 pl. | *desetha* | *karotha* | *jānātha* |
| 3 pl. | *desenti* | *karonti* | *jānanti* |

Because each person has its own ending, the personal pronoun is not needed and is usually absent. *Dhammaṁ desenti* is 'they teach the Dhamma' with no *te* in it; the *-nti* has already said 'they'. When a pronoun does appear it adds something (contrast, or emphasis of the kind set out in Chapter 5), and it should be noticed, not translated away.

#### What the present tense does

The present indicative states a fact, and its plainest use is what its name suggests: something is the case now. English has to choose between 'he teaches' and 'he is teaching'; Pāli does not, and either may be the right rendering.[^present-progressive]

[^present-progressive]: Thānuttamo pp. 90–92 lists eight senses for the present indicative, attributed to Collins pp. 81–83, Duroiselle (§611, p. 320 in the 1921 edition), Hendriksen p. 13 n. 2 and Perniola pp. 349–350. Three of them are given here, and the remaining five (interrogative use at the head of a sentence, the recent past, the hypothetical, and the future with the particles *yāva*, *pure*, *purā* and with *kadā*) are real but too thinly attested in the four Nikāyas to earn a section in a chapter this size.

Beyond that it does two things a reader must be ready for. The first is to state what is always the case. A general truth has no particular time, and Pāli puts it in the present:

- Jātassa maraṇaṁ **hoti**, jāto dukkhāni phussati; (SN 5.6 — S i 132)
- *jātassa* for one who is born (m. gen. sg.) · *maraṇaṁ* death (n. nom. sg.) · *hoti* is · *jāto* one born (m. nom. sg.) · *dukkhāni* sufferings (n. acc. pl.) · *phussati* encounters
- For one who is born there **is** death; one born encounters sufferings.

No action in that line is happening at the moment of speaking. *Hoti* means 'is the case', not 'is dying now', and the sentence reaches into the future (for one who is born there *will* be death) without any future form in it.

The second is the **historic present**, and it is very common. Pāli narrates past events in the present tense, where English can do so only in a consciously colloquial register.[^historic-present] Every sutta opens this way:

[^historic-present]: Thānuttamo p. 92 marks it "very common". The English parallel is real but stylistically marked — "so I walk into his office and say…" — which is why a translator almost always renders it as a past. Bodhi does exactly that in the line below, giving "was living" for *viharati*.

- ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā kurūsu **viharati** kammāsadhammaṁ nāma kurūnaṁ nigamo. (MN 10 — M i 55)
- *ekaṁ samayaṁ* at one time · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *kurūsu* among the Kurus (m. loc. pl.) · *viharati* dwells · *kammāsadhammaṁ nāma* named Kammāsadhamma · *kurūnaṁ* of the Kurus (m. gen. pl.) · *nigamo* a town (m. nom. sg.)
- At one time the Blessed One **was dwelling** among the Kurus, at a town of the Kurus named Kammāsadhamma.

The verb is present and the event is centuries past. To render *viharati* as 'dwells' here is an error of English, not of grammar; but to conclude from the tense that the Buddha is described as presently resident is to misread the sentence.

### The imperative

The imperative commands, and it also invites, blesses, permits and entreats: a range much wider than the English term suggests. Its endings go on the same present stem:

| Person | Active | Middle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-mi* | *-e* |
| 2 sg. | *-hi*, or the bare stem | *-ssu* |
| 3 sg. | *-tu* | *-taṁ* |
| 1 pl. | *-ma*, *-mu* | *-āmase*, *-mhase* |
| 2 pl. | *-tha* | *-vho* |
| 3 pl. | *-ntu* | *-ntaṁ*, *-ruṁ* |

Three of the active endings are identical to the present indicative's (the first person singular and plural and the second plural), so *gacchāma* is 'we go' or 'let us go' and only the context decides.[^impv-syncretism] On the stem *gaccha-*:

[^impv-syncretism]: The overlap is real and total in those three cells, and it is not a defect of the table: the first-person imperative is a hortative ('let me', 'let us'), which is close enough to a statement of intention that the two never needed separate forms. The second plural *gacchatha* covers both 'you go' and 'go!' the same way.

|     | singular | plural |
|-----|----------|--------|
| 1st | *gacchāmi* 'let me go' | *gacchāma* 'let us go' |
| 2nd | *gacchāhi*, *gaccha* 'go!' | *gacchatha* 'go!' |
| 3rd | *gacchatu* 'let him go' | *gacchantu* 'let them go' |

Look at the second person singular. It has two forms: the ending *-hi* with the stem vowel lengthened before it, and the bare stem with no ending at all. Both are ordinary; *gaccha* and *gacchāhi* are the same command.[^impv-2sg] Only stems in *-a* take the bare form, because otherwise nothing would distinguish it from the stem itself.

[^impv-2sg]: Kacc 479, at Thānuttamo p. 88: the *a* of the stem is lengthened before *-hi*, and the ending is "sometimes elided altogether". Both *gaccha* and *gacchāhi* are well attested in the root texts, as are *karohi* and *suṇohi*, where the *-o-* stem shows the ending clearly.

The commonest use is the plain one, and the Buddha's most quoted instruction to enquirers is a string of them:

- **Etha** tumhe, kālāmā, **mā** anussavena, **mā** paramparāya … **mā** samaṇo no garūti. (AN 3.65 — A i 189)
- *etha* come! (2 pl. imperative) · *tumhe* you (nom. pl.) · *kālāmā* Kālāmas (voc. pl.) · *mā* do not · *anussavena* by oral transmission (m. inst. sg.) · *paramparāya* by lineage (f. inst. sg.) · *samaṇo* the ascetic (m. nom. sg.) · *no* our · *garu* teacher · *ti* end-quote
- **Come**, Kālāmas: **do not** go by oral transmission, **nor** by lineage … **nor** by thinking 'the ascetic is our teacher'.

The third person is where the range widens, because a third-person imperative in English needs a paraphrase ('let him', 'may he'), and the Pāli carries no such politeness marker of its own. It covers instruction, invitation, blessing and entreaty alike, and who is speaking to whom decides which is meant.[^impv-senses] The best-known instance in the canon is a benediction:

[^impv-senses]: Thānuttamo pp. 92–93 lists ten senses (command, prohibition, advice, invitation, wish, curse, benediction, entreaty, reflection, hope), with an illustration for all but prohibition and wish, which he names and leaves bare. His illustrations are largely Kaccāyana's and the commentaries', so the classification is used here and the examples are not.

- Sukhino va khemino hontu, sabbasattā **bhavantu** sukhitattā. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 25)[^sabbe-satta]
- *sukhino* happy (m. nom. pl.) · *va* indeed · *khemino* safe (m. nom. pl.) · *hontu* let them be (3 pl. imperative) · *sabba·sattā* all beings (m. nom. pl.) · *bhavantu* let them be (3 pl. imperative) · *sukhit·attā* happy in themselves (m. nom. pl.)
- May they be happy and safe; **may** all beings **be** happy in themselves.

[^sabbe-satta]: The editions divide the words differently here. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints the compound *sabbasattā*; the Buddha Jayanti, the Syāmese and the Pali Text Society all print *sabbe sattā*, two words, which is also how the line is set in most chanting books. Nothing depends on it (the sense and the grammar are identical, and *sabbe* is simply the same stem uncompounded), but if you know the verse by heart in one form, do not conclude that the other is a mistake. No edition varies *sukhitattā*.

Two imperatives in one line, *hontu* and *bhavantu*, from the two stems of the same verb 'to be'. Neither is a command to anyone. The mood is the same one that orders a monk to go; the sense is entirely from the setting.

The singular is at its most formal in the Vinaya, where every act of the Sangha opens with the same four words:

- **Suṇātu** me, bhante, saṅgho. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196)
- *suṇātu* let it listen (3 sg. imperative) · *me* to me (1 sg. dat.) · *bhante* venerable sirs (voc.) · *saṅgho* the Sangha (m. nom. sg.)
- "**Let** the Sangha **listen** to me, venerable sirs."

No command is given here either, and none could be: a monk has no standing to order the Sangha. The sentence is a request for the floor, and it opens every *kammavācā*, 'act-statement', in the Vinaya: the formula by which a community does anything formally at all.[^kammavaca] A nun's version differs in one word, *ayye* for *bhante*. So in two examples the third person has moved from benediction to formal request with no change in the form, the point this section makes and the reason the ten senses are a list, not a paradigm.

[^kammavaca]: *Kammavācā* has 11 root-text occurrences and the accusative *kammavācaṁ* 14, and every one of them stands in a Vinaya file. It is one of the few technical terms of the tradition that is canonical, not commentarial, as one would expect of a word naming a procedure the canon has to legislate.

### The optative

The optative, also called the potential, covers what may, might, could, would or should happen. Its endings are added to the present stem, and unlike the imperative's they are unmistakable, because every one of them carries *-eyy-* or its contraction:

| Person | Active | Middle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-eyyaṁ*, *-e* | *-eyyaṁ* |
| 2 sg. | *-eyyāsi*, *-e* | *-etho* |
| 3 sg. | *-eyya*, *-e* | *-etha* |
| 1 pl. | *-eyyāma* | *-emase* |
| 2 pl. | *-eyyātha* | *-eyyavho* |
| 3 pl. | *-eyyuṁ* | *-eraṁ* |

On the stem *gaccha-* these give *gaccheyyaṁ* 'I might go', *gaccheyyāsi*, *gaccheyya*, *gaccheyyāma*, *gaccheyyātha*, *gaccheyyuṁ*. The short forms in *-e* alongside the full ones in *-eyya-* are contractions of the same ending and mean the same thing; the tradition also records an optative in *-yā*, which survives in a few common verbs: *vajjā* 'he would say', *dajjā* 'he would give'.[^opt-ya]

[^opt-ya]: Thānuttamo p. 93, reporting from Collins p. 85 that double formations also occur, *-eyya* being added to a base already in *-yā* to give *dajjeyya*. That double form is a grammarians' construction and should not be looked for in the suttas: *dajjā* itself has 44 occurrences in the root texts, while *dajjeyya* and *dajjeyyaṁ* appear only in the grammatical literature and *dajjeyyāti*, the form as Thānuttamo prints it, nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all. Appendix A.3 carries the contracted optative forms in its own column; both series are Ñāṇatusita's.

The optative's commonest job in the canon is the conditional clause, where it is regularly preceded by *sace*, *ce* or *yadi*, all meaning 'if':

- **Sace** maṁ, bhante, evaṁ **puccheyyuṁ** … (DN 9 — D i 200)
- *sace* if · *maṁ* me (acc. sg.) · *bhante* venerable sir (voc.) · *evaṁ* thus · *puccheyyuṁ* they should ask (3 pl. optative)
- **If** they were to **ask** me thus, venerable sir …

Its second use is the polite or provisional statement, and in the Buddha's speech the mood is used most this way: a rule stated as what one *should* do, an enquiry framed as what one *might* find. The Kālāma Sutta puts an optative directly after the imperatives quoted above, and the shift is the point of the passage: having told them what not to rely on, the Buddha moves to what they may come to know for themselves:

- Yadā tumhe, kālāmā, attanāva **jāneyyātha** … (AN 3.65 — A i 189)
- *yadā* when · *tumhe* you (nom. pl.) · *kālāmā* Kālāmas (voc. pl.) · *attanā* by oneself (m. inst. sg.) · *va* just, only · *jāneyyātha* you should know (2 pl. optative)
- But when you **come to know** for yourselves …

One caution, against seeing a distinction that is not there. The imperative and the optative overlap in use to the point that Oberlies calls them "syntactically often exchangeable".[^impv-opt-overlap] A wish may appear in either; a mild instruction may appear in either. The forms are always distinguishable and the senses frequently are not, so having parsed *gaccheyya* correctly as an optative you still have to decide from the context whether it is 'he might go', 'he should go' or simply 'let him go'.

[^impv-opt-overlap]: Oberlies (2019) p. 399, quoted at Thānuttamo p. 92. The same page adds, from a personal communication of Oberlies's dated 30 October 2020, that imperatives occur chiefly in main clauses, the only structural cue that separates the two when the sense will not.

### *Atthi* and *hoti*

One verb is irregular enough in the present to be learnt whole, and it is among the most frequent words in the canon. *Atthi* 'is, exists' does not take a stem in the way the verbs above do; the endings attach to the root itself, with the results that Old Indic left behind:

|     | singular | plural |
|-----|----------|--------|
| 1st | *asmi*, *amhi* 'I am' | *amha* 'we are' |
| 2nd | *asi* 'you are' | *attha* 'you are' |
| 3rd | *atthi* 'he, she, it is; there is' | *santi* 'they are' |

Its imperative is *atthu*, *santu*, and its optative is *siyā* or *assa*, plural *siyuṁ* or *assu*, the last of these among the most common verb forms in the whole canon, since a very large part of the Buddha's conditional and hypothetical speech runs through it.[^atthi-optative]

[^atthi-optative]: *Siyā* has 3,843 occurrences in the root texts and *assa* 812, against 1 for the imperative *atthu*. Appendix A.3's optative endings account for both series: *siyā* is the *-yā* type mentioned above, and *assa* the ordinary *-eyya* contracted onto the root *as-*.

Two practical notes about this paradigm, because the table above is tidier than the page a reader will meet. The first-person forms are usually printed joined to the word before them. Standing alone, *asmi* occurs just once in the root texts; joined, it is everywhere: *ahamasmi* 'I am' forty-five times, and *asmī'ti* sixty-five.[^asmi-sandhi] Search a text for *asmi*, find nothing, and you have met a fact about how the editions set sandhi, not about the language.

[^asmi-sandhi]: Counted across the root texts of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. The same pattern is visible in the doctrinal term *asmimāna*, 'the conceit "I am"', where the form is embedded in a compound. Chapter 2's rules for junction are what undo it.

And the doublets the handbooks print beside these are real, although they do not occur as words of their own. The first singular is often given as *asmi* or *amhi*, the first plural as *amha* or *amhā*. *Amhi* and *amhā* do not occur as words in the root texts at all, as one would expect of a pair of grammarians' constructions. They are hidden by the sandhi just described, and hidden more completely, because a joined *amhi* gives up its own initial vowel and leaves only *-mhi* behind. *Kho* + *amhi* gives *khomhi* 'I am indeed', which stands in the root texts a hundred and thirty-four times; *na* + *amhi* gives *nāmhi* 'I am not', which is how the Buddha asks to be left undisturbed for a fortnight.[^amhi-amha] So the first person singular has two living forms, not one. The first plural does not, and there the handbooks are nearly right: *amhā* has two occurrences in the whole of the root texts against *amha*'s eight, and is the rarer word by a long way.

[^amhi-amha]: Counted across the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. As words standing alone in the root texts: *asmi* 1, *amhi* 0, *amha* 1, *amhā* 0. Joined, *amhi* occurs in 136 distinct words and 495 places in the root texts: *khomhi* 134, *pattomhi* 'I have attained' 36, *somhi* 27, *nāmhi* 5, the last at SN 54.11, *nāmhi kenaci upasaṅkamitabbo*, 'I am not to be approached by anyone'. Joined *asmi* is commoner still, its two largest hosts being the *anattā* formula's *esohamasmi* and *nesohamasmi*, 157 and 193. *Amhā*'s two root-text occurrences are *jhāyinomhā* 'we are meditators' (AN 6.46) and *dassanakāmamhā* 'we wish to see' (Ud 1.10); *amha*'s eight are one bare, six as *khomha* and one as *bhātaromha*. DPD confirms each analysis: *khomhi* as *kho* + *amhi*, *nāmhi* as *na* + *amhi*, s.vv.

The other common verb to name here is *hoti* 'is, becomes', a contraction of *bhavati*, which supplies most of the ordinary copula and whose imperative *hotu* 'so be it' a reader meets constantly. *Hoti* and *bhavati* say that A is B; *atthi* says that A exists, or that there is an A. The distinction is not absolute, but it is the safe assumption.

Beyond these, a minority of verbs are irregular in the present and a much larger number are irregular in the past. Since almost all of that irregularity falls in the aorist, the irregular verbs are gathered in the next chapter, where they can be met alongside the tense that makes them irregular.
