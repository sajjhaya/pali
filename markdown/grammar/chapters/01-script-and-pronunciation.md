## Script and Pronunciation

Pāli is a language that was spoken for centuries before anyone wrote it down, and it has no script of its own. That fact governs everything in this chapter. The alphabet below is a set of borrowings, not a Pāli invention; the spellings in the editions are the grammarians', not the Buddha's; and the pronunciation this chapter teaches is a reconstruction, better attested at some points than at others.

None of that makes the sounds unimportant. They matter more here than in a written language, because the texts were composed to be heard and held in memory, and a great deal of their structure (the pairing, the repetition, the metre of the verses) is audible. A reader who never says the words aloud reads half the text.

### The language before the script

The early discourses were carried orally for some centuries before being committed to writing, and when they were written the scribes used whatever script was to hand. Manuscripts survive in Sinhala, Burmese, Thai and Khmer letters, all of them descended from the old Indian Brāhmī, and a few in Brāhmī itself. None of these is *the* Pāli script; each is a local script pressed into service.

What they have in common is that they are not alphabets in the European sense. Each consonant sign carries an inherent *a*, so the sign transliterated *k* is read *ka* unless something marks otherwise, and so the tradition names the letters *ka*, *kha*, *ga*, not *k*, *kh*, *g*. The technical term for such a system is an **abugida**.[^abugida]

[^abugida]: Thānuttamo p. 14, correcting Gornall (2014: 511), who calls Pāli's writing a syllabary; the correction is Ānandajoti's, from a personal communication Thānuttamo reports. The distinction matters because a syllabary has an unanalysable sign per syllable, while an abugida builds the syllable out of a consonant sign and a vowel mark, as the *saṁyoga* rules below presuppose.

In the nineteenth century European scholars began transcribing Pāli into the Roman alphabet, and with the new letters came something the manuscripts had never had: spaces between words, capitals and full stops. That is a larger change than it sounds. A manuscript line runs unbroken, and dividing it is already an act of interpretation: the reader must decide where one word ends and the next begins, and in a language full of sandhi that is the hard part.

The homage that opens most recitations will serve to show it. Written the way a manuscript writes it, in Roman letters and without the division, it reads:

*namotassabhagavatoarahatosammāsambuddhassa*

And as the editions print it:

- Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa. (MN 91 — M ii 140)
- *namo* homage · *tassa* to him · *bhagavato* to the Blessed One · *arahato* to the perfected one · *sammāsambuddhassa* to the fully awakened one
- Homage to him, the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened one.

Nothing has been added but the spaces, and the spaces are the whole difficulty. This book gives Pāli in Roman letters throughout, with the divisions the editors supply; the chapter on sandhi is about what happens at the joins those spaces conceal.

Roman is only one of the alphabets this sentence lives in, and it is the newest. Here is the same line, at the same place in the same edition, as it is set in Sinhala, in Thai and in Myanmar:[^script-sample]

| | |
|---|---|
| Sinhala | නමො තස්‌ස භගවතො අරහතො සම්‌මාසම්‌බුද්‌ධස්‌ස |
| Thai | ฯ นโม ตสฺส ภควโต อรหโต สมฺมาสมฺพุทฺธสฺสฯ |
| Myanmar | ။ နမော တဿ ဘဂဝတော အရဟတော သမ္မာသမ္ဗုဒ္ဓဿ။ |
| Roman | Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa |

[^script-sample]: All four are the homage that opens the Majjhimapaṇṇāsa, the middle fifty suttas of the Majjhima Nikāya, in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana of the Vipassana Research Institute. The Institute publishes that one edition in fifteen scripts, so the four are the same text at the same place, and the Roman line is the text this book quotes from throughout. The Thai *paiyannoi* (ฯ) opening and closing the line is that tradition's punctuation, not part of the Pāli.

Read the rows against each other and the abugida shows itself. The *-mo* of *namo* is one consonant sign with a vowel mark on it in all three Asian scripts and two separate letters only in Roman. In Thai the mark for *o* is written to the left of the consonant it is pronounced after, which shows as clearly as anything could that these are not alphabets running left to right one sound at a time. The clusters of *sammāsambuddhassa* behave the same way: each is a single stacked or joined shape, where Roman writes two characters and trusts the reader to know it is one sound.

Of the four, only Roman is an alphabet, and its apparatus of macrons, dots and digraphs is the price of making an alphabet serve as an abugida. Hold on to that, because the next section is a list of forty-one letters, and the list is a European convenience, not something the tradition would recognise as the structure of its own writing.

One thing has to be said before the list, because everything after it depends on the answer. The orthography of the printed editions is not a transcript of ancient speech. Oberlies §2 puts it flatly: our texts follow the rules of the Pāli grammarians of the twelfth century, and that orthography is "historical and not phonetical": it records how a word was understood to be built, not how it was said. So a spelling in this book is evidence about a word's structure and only indirectly about its sound. Where this chapter reconstructs a pronunciation, the reconstruction matters, and where the sources disagree about one, this chapter says so instead of choosing.

### The alphabet

The Pali Text Society's dictionary sets out its alphabet in this order, and it is the order this book follows:[^ped-alphabet]

*a ā i ī u ū e o ṁ — k kh g gh ṅ — c ch j jh ñ — ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh ṇ — t th d dh n — p ph b bh m — y r l ḷ v s h*

[^ped-alphabet]: Rhys Davids and Stede, *PTS Pāli–English Dictionary*, front matter: "Pāli alphabet used in this dictionary". Two notes there: the original print uses **ŋ** for the niggahīta, and it "is not distinguished in the original print" from *n*; see the box on *ṅ* below.

Forty-one letters, then. That count is the most often given, and on it the tradition is least united.

Eight of the forty-one are vowels (*a, ā, i, ī, u, ū, e, o*), and on this point alone every source agrees.[^vowel-inventory] The remainder divide into five groups of five, each group sharing a place in the mouth, plus seven that belong to no group, plus the niggahīta.

[^vowel-inventory]: Oberlies §3.1, p. 17, citing Saddanīti IV 1.1.2; Perniola §1, p. 1; Thānuttamo p. 15. Oberlies adds what Pāli has *lost* against Old Indo-Aryan: the vowels *ṛ*, *ṝ* and *ḷ*, and the diphthongs *ai* and *au*. The lost vowels reappear in Pāli as *a*, *i* or *u* (Sanskrit *kṛta* is Pāli *kata*, *śṛgāla* is *sigāla*, *pṛcchati* is *pucchati*: Perniola §1b, p. 2), so a Sanskritist meets many Pāli words as familiar forms with an unexpected vowel.

The tradition calls the five groups the *vaggā*, and names each after its first member: the *ka*-group, the *ca*-group, the *ṭa*-group, the *ta*-group and the *pa*-group. The seven that belong to none of them (*y, r, l, v, s, h, ḷ*) it calls simply *avaggā*, ungrouped.

Because the Roman alphabet has only twenty-six letters, the extra sounds are written with diacritics (the dots and bars) and with digraphs, pairs of Roman characters standing for a single Pāli sound. Every combination with *h* listed in the alphabet above is a digraph; every other combination with *h* is two letters. So *dukkha* has five letters and not six, because *kh* is one; but *amhi* has four, because *mh* is an *m* followed by an *h*, not a digraph. The tradition makes the same point from the other end: the aspirates are not conjunct consonants, because each is a single sound.[^samyoga]

[^samyoga]: Thānuttamo p. 14, citing Kaccāyana 2–3 and 6–7 and Ānandajoti (2004: 16). A *saṁyoga* is a cluster of consonants with no vowel between them, and in the traditional analysis it includes geminates: *kkh*, *dda*, *ss* as well as *ndh*, *mba*.

Two rules govern which consonants may form such a cluster, and between them they account for most of the clusters a reader will meet. Only the first and second members of a group may combine, and only the third and fourth, and only in that order: *k* with *kh* but never *kh* with *k*. The nasal that closes each group may attach to any member of its own group, including itself.[^conjunct-rules] That is the general fact behind the distribution of *ṅ* below.

[^conjunct-rules]: Thānuttamo p. 15, after Ñāṇadhaja and Rūpasiddhi. He records one exception, on Yindee's authority: *ṅ* alone cannot geminate with itself, so no Pāli word contains *ṅṅ*.

> **Variants of *ṅ***
>
> The letter *ṅ* occurs only before *k*, *kh*, *g* or *gh*, and those four are never preceded by *n*. The distribution is therefore predictable in both directions, and some works exploit that by writing simply *n* and leaving the reader to infer the *ṅ* from what follows: *sangha* for *saṅgha*. The PTS dictionary does this, and says so in its own front matter.
>
> The reason is the conjunct rule above: a group nasal attaches to members of its own group, so a nasal before a guttural can only be the guttural nasal. Leaving it unwritten loses nothing and gains nothing. This book writes *ṅ*.

A small difference of letters makes a different word, and the differences that trip an English speaker are those English does not use: the length of a vowel, the doubling of a consonant, the retroflex against the dental, the aspirate against the plain.

Every term in that list is about *sound*, which is a fair indication of how much the tradition cared about it. The sentence in the edition begins *Bhavañhi soṇadaṇḍo*, 'For you, sir Soṇadaṇḍa', and that first word is *bhavaṁ* + *hi*, with the niggahīta assimilated to *ñ* before the *h*. The rule behind it is below.

### How the sounds are made

The early grammarians analysed each sound by three parameters: its **place** (*ṭhāna*), the point in the mouth where it is formed; its **instrument** (*karaṇa*), the part of the tongue or lip that forms it; and its **mode** (*payatana*), the action of the breath and the voice meanwhile.[^three-parameters] Modern descriptions usually collapse the first two, and for most sounds nothing is lost. For three of them something is.

[^three-parameters]: Thānuttamo p. 17, after Rūpasiddhi and Ñāṇadhaja. The table below keeps place across the rows and mode across the columns, in the ordinary way, and names the instrument only for the three sounds where it changes the answer.

The Pāli letters by place and mode of pronunciation.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| guttural (*kaṇṭhaja*) | *a* | *ā* | *k* | *kh* | *g* | *gh* | *ṅ* | *h* |
| palatal (*tāluja*) | *i* | *ī* | *c* | *ch* | *j* | *jh* | *ñ* | *y* |
| retroflex (*muddhaja*) | | | *ṭ* | *ṭh* | *ḍ* | *ḍh* | *ṇ* | *r*, *ḷ* |
| dental (*dantaja*) | | | *t* | *th* | *d* | *dh* | *n* | *l*, *s* |
| labial (*oṭṭhaja*) | *u* | *ū* | *p* | *ph* | *b* | *bh* | *m* | |

The five principal places run from the back of the mouth forward. The **gutturals**, or velars, are made at the back, near the throat. The **palatals** are made at the hard palate, the middle of the roof of the mouth. The **retroflexes** (the tradition calls them *muddhaja*, 'head-born', and older grammars call them cerebrals) are made with the tip of the tongue curled back towards the palate. The **dentals** are made with the tongue against the back of the front teeth. The **labials** are made at the lips.

Three sounds have two places, not one, and the tradition names each of them. *E* is guttural-palatal and *o* is guttural-labial, a statement of what they are, not a curiosity of classification: *e* is the merger of *a* with *i*, and *o* the merger of *a* with *u*, so each keeps both of its parents' places. *V* is dental-labial, made with the lower lip against the upper teeth. Filing *v* with the labials, as most modern tables do, is close enough for a first approximation, but the grammarians say otherwise.

The niggahīta has its own place, the nose, and it is discussed on its own below.

#### The vowels

Of the eight vowels, *ā*, *ī* and *ū* are long and *a*, *i* and *u* are short: twice the duration and half of it. The tradition measures this in *mattā*, and defines the unit concretely: one *mattā* is the time it takes to pronounce a short vowel, which it glosses as the time it takes to blink once. A long vowel is two.[^matta] A consonant on its own is half a *mattā*.

[^matta]: Thānuttamo pp. 15–16, citing Kaccāyana 4, Rūpasiddhi and Ñāṇadhaja, and the commentary to the Parivāra (Sp VII 1399, which he cites by the page of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition). He records one refinement before the metre chapter: in verse a short vowel may be given a *mattā* and a half. Oberlies §3.2, p. 17, uses the same unit under its Sanskrit name, *mora*.

*E* and *o* are the awkward pair. They are long by nature, and shorten when a cluster of consonants follows: long in *vane* 'in the forest' and *putto* 'a son', where nothing follows; long in *kāmesu* and *odanaṁ*, where a single consonant follows; but short in *mettā* 'loving-kindness' and *bhāseyya* 'he should speak', where a doubled consonant follows and the vowel has to give up its length to make room. Perniola states the same rule from the syllable's side: *e* and *o* are long at the end of a syllable, as in *e-so* and *do-so*, and short when they are closed by a consonant, as in *met-tā* and *pot-thakaṁ*.

That much is settled. Where the rule stops is not.

> **How many letters, and how long is *e*?**
>
> Grammars differ, and the difference is old. It depends on two questions, and answering them differently gives a different count of the alphabet.
>
> *Is *ḷh* a letter?* It is written as two characters even in the Asian scripts, but it stands for a single sound. Perniola counts it among his liquids and so reaches **42 letters**: eight vowels, thirty-three consonants, and the niggahīta counted separately. The PTS dictionary's list and Kaccāyana's both omit it and reach **41**.
>
> *Are short *e* and short *o* the same letters as long *e* and long *o*?* Kaccāyana holds that *e* and *o* are simply long. Buddhappiya, in the *Padarūpasiddhi* and within Kaccāyana's own tradition, holds that they shorten before geminates but not before conjuncts formed with a group nasal, so *bhāseyya* has a short *e*, but *meṇḍo* 'ram' and *soṇḍo* 'drunkard' have long ones, because *ṇḍ* is a group nasal with a stop of its own class and not a geminate. Moggallāna rejects the compromise: a sound cannot be long and then, in certain places, short, so short *e* and short *o* must be different letters altogether. Counting them separately gives him **43**.[^moggallana]
>
> This book follows the majority and counts forty-one, and teaches the rule as Perniola and Buddhappiya give it. A different count in another grammar is not an error.

[^moggallana]: Thānuttamo p. 16, the only source consulted that states all three positions and attributes each. He names the class at p. 15 instead of leaving it as "other clusters": "Exceptions for *e* and *o*: occurrences before conjuncts with end-group nasals are long".  Gornall (2014: 519) for Moggallāna's argument. Perniola's count is at his p. 1, the PTS list in the dictionary's front matter, Kaccāyana's at Kacc 5. *Meṇḍo* and *soṇḍo* are both canonical, 4 and 2 occurrences respectively in the root texts.

Two of the vowels are historically compounds, and the fact is still visible. *E* arises from *a* plus *i* and *o* from *a* plus *u*, so *upa* + *ikkhati* gives *upekkhati*, and the stock phrase for a well-provisioned place, *satiṇakaṭṭhodakaṁ* 'with grass, wood and water', has *kaṭṭha* + *udaka* joined as *-kaṭṭhodaka-*. The same merger, running in reverse, produces the *ava* ~ *o* and *aya* ~ *e* variants in the box above: *avakāso* and *okāso* are the same word at two stages of one change. Sanskrit spells the older stage (*maitrī* for *mettā*, *auṣadha* for *osadha* 'medicine'), so the correspondence is easiest to see from that side.[^gliding]

[^gliding]: Perniola §1b, p. 2, calls *e* and *o* diphthongs and derives them from *a-i* / *ā-i* and *a-u* / *ā-u*; the tradition's *kaṇṭhatāluja* and *kaṇṭhoṭṭhaja* say the same thing in the language of articulation. Both are now usually pronounced as pure vowels, not glides, and it is likely that both pronunciations existed.

#### The consonants

Twenty-five of the thirty-three consonants sit in the five groups, and within each group the five members differ by mode alone. The **voiced** are made with the vocal cords vibrating and the **voiceless** without; the **aspirates** are made with a distinct pulse of breath after the closure and the **non-aspirates** without; the **nasals** are made with the breath escaping through the nose, not the mouth. Read across a row of the table and the five columns are: voiceless plain, voiceless aspirate, voiced plain, voiced aspirate, nasal.

The seven ungrouped consonants are *y*, *r*, *l*, *v*, *s*, *h* and *ḷ*. *Y* and *v* are semivowels, and the label means something: they are what *i* and *u* become when a vowel follows, a fact the next chapter uses constantly. *S* is the only sibilant; Pāli has lost Sanskrit's *ś* and *ṣ*. *H* is doubly classified: on its own it is a guttural, and when it appears as the second element of a digraph, or after *y*, *r*, *l* or *v*, the tradition calls it *uraja*, 'chest-born', and treats it as the mark of aspiration, not as a consonant in its own right.

Two cross-cutting distinctions matter for the chapter on sandhi and are best met here. Perniola groups the first two columns of each row together with *s* as **hard** and the rest as **soft**, which is voicing under another name; and he ranks the consonants by strength (mutes, then the sibilant, then the nasals, then *l*, *v*, *y*, *r*), because when two consonants collide the stronger survives.

No Pāli word ends in a consonant. Every word ends in a vowel or in the niggahīta. That is the fact that makes external sandhi tractable: there are only so many ways two words can meet.

### The niggahīta

The niggahīta is written *ṁ* in this book, *ṃ* in many others, and *ŋ* in the older ones. It is one sound, not three, and the three symbols are three transliteration conventions: *ṁ* is the ISO 15919 form, *ṃ* the IAST form used by the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition and by the later Pali Text Society volumes, and *ŋ* the form the original PTS dictionary prints.[^niggahita-symbols] The choice requires only consistency, and this book is consistent.

[^niggahita-symbols]: Thānuttamo pp. 23–24 sets out the schemes, including the ASCII conventions (ITRANS `M`, `N`, `.m`; Velthuis `.m`) that a reader will meet in older electronic texts. The PTS dictionary's front matter states its own use of ŋ. In the Asian scripts the niggahīta is a diacritic, not a letter at all, usually a dot or a small circle written above or beside the syllable.

Its name is a description. *Niggahīta* is the past participle of *niggaṇhāti*, 'to press down, to restrain', and the tradition explains that what is restrained is the organ of articulation: the mouth is not fully opened, so the breath escapes partly through the nose.[^niggahita-etymology] It never stands alone, and it never follows a long vowel: it is founded on, and takes up, the three short vowels *a*, *i* and *u*.

[^niggahita-etymology]: Thānuttamo p. 24, quoting the *Padarūpasiddhi*: the letter is called *niggahīta* because the *karaṇa* is restrained (*niggahīta*) by an unopened mouth (*mukhenāvivaṭena*), and because it rests on (*nissāya*) the short vowels *a*, *i*, *u*. The traditional grammarians list it among the **consonants** (Kacc 2–3, 6), not as a third category beside vowels and consonants.

Before a consonant, the niggahīta is regularly replaced in writing by the nasal of that consonant's own group: the homorganic nasal, made at the same place as the sound that follows.[^homorganic]

| *p, ph, b, bh* | *m* | *ahaṁ* + *api* → *ahampi* |

[^homorganic]: Thānuttamo pp. 23–24, citing Kaccāyana 31, 466 and 537. The table covers the five groups; *h*, which belongs to none of them, takes the palatal nasal: *evaṁ* + *hi* gives *evañhi*, 329 occurrences in the root texts, and *bhavaṁ* + *hi* gives the *bhavañhi* that opens the example above (DN 4 — D i 114).

That much is orthography, and it is not always applied: editions often leave the niggahīta written and let the reader supply the nasal. What the niggahīta actually *sounded* like is a different question, and a harder one.

> **Grammars differ: what the niggahīta was**
>
> Two accounts are current and neither can be dismissed.
>
> On the first, the niggahīta is a **nasalised vowel**: *aṁ*, *iṁ* and *uṁ* are the vowels of French *genre*, *vin* and *un*, and the symbol marks nasality, not a segment of its own. Thānuttamo takes this view, with Collins, Geiger, Deokar and Levman behind him, and adds an argument from the neighbours: nasalised vowels are native to the Dravidian and Munda languages spoken in the region, so the feature would not have been foreign.
>
> On the second, the two things are distinct and the scripts cannot show it. Oberlies distinguishes a vowel followed by an *anusvāra*, which is a pure nasal sound after a vowel that is **not** itself nasalised, from a truly nasalised vowel, the tradition's *sānunāsika*. The first should belong to long syllables and the second to short ones; but no script of the Pāli tradition has a sign for the second, so the printed editions confuse them.
>
> One point is not in dispute, and a reader can check it: a syllable closed by a niggahīta is **heavy**. That is a fact about the metre of the verses, not a reconstruction, and the next section builds on it.

The living traditions have settled the question differently from one another and from both accounts above: Sri Lanka and Thailand pronounce a final niggahīta as *ng*, Burma as *m*. Neither is likely to be the ancient value, and neither is wrong to use. The point is made rather sharply in the Vinaya commentary's directions for giving the refuges: *buddhaṁ saraṇaṁ gacchāmi* may be given as one connected utterance with the ends nasalised, or broken up with the *m* fully sounded, and both are valid.[^tisarana]

[^tisarana]: Thānuttamo p. 25, quoting the commentary on how the refuges are to be given (Sp V 969). He supplies the tradition's contrast term a page earlier, at p. 24, from a different commentarial passage (the Parivāra commentary on *pattakallaṁ* against *pattakallā*, Sp VII 1400), where *vimutta* 'released' names a vowel spoken with the mouth open and no nasalisation, the direct opposite of *niggahīta*, 'restrained'. That a commentary on monastic procedure should turn on the distinction is the best evidence there is that it was audible.

### Syllables and their weight

Dividing a word into syllables is needed for pronunciation, and needed again for the verses. The working rules are three.

A break falls **between two adjacent consonants**: *at·tā*, *duk·khaṁ*, *am·hi*. A break falls **before a single consonant**: *a·ku·sa·lo*, *bha·ga·vā*. And a digraph is a single consonant, so *adhi* divides *a·dhi* and not *ad·hi*, while *amhi* (where *mh* is two letters) divides *am·hi* and not *a·mhi*. The same goes for *ḷh*: *daḷha* divides *da·ḷha*.

Every syllable is then either **light** or **heavy**. A syllable is light if it ends in a short vowel, and heavy in every other case: if it ends in a long vowel, or in a consonant, or in the niggahīta. A heavy syllable takes about twice as long to say as a light one, and it is this alternation, not stress, that gives the chanting its rhythm.

Behind the definition is a law with consequences. A syllable may not hold more than two *mattā*. A short vowel is one, a long vowel two, and a vowel closed by a consonant is two as well, so a long vowel in a closed syllable would make three, and Pāli does not allow it.[^mora-law] The pressure has to escape somewhere, and it escapes in three regular ways, all of which a reader will meet constantly:

[^mora-law]: Oberlies §3.2, pp. 17–18, who states it as the 'law of *mora*'; Thānuttamo p. 15 gives the same law in the tradition's terms and notes that it is a strong tendency, not an exceptionless rule: *svākkhāto* 'well proclaimed', a long vowel before a cluster, occurs 136 times in the root texts.

A long vowel in an open syllable may shorten while the following consonant doubles, the total staying at two: *dīyati* beside *diyyati* 'is given', *dāṭhā* beside *daṭṭhā* 'fang'. A short vowel in a closed syllable may lengthen while the cluster simplifies, which is the same trade run backwards: *kattabbaṁ* beside *kātabbaṁ* 'to be done', *kattuṁ* beside *kātuṁ* 'to do'. And a nasal may be exchanged for vowel length, since a short vowel plus a nasal comes to the same two *mattā* as a long vowel alone, and so Sanskrit *viṁśati* 'twenty' is Pāli *vīsati*, and Sanskrit *siṁha* 'lion' is Pāli *sīha*.[^three-changes]

[^three-changes]: Perniola §3, pp. 4–5, who derives the first two from the two-*mattā* ceiling and alone among the sources read for this chapter presents them as one phenomenon, not as separate lists; his own examples for the third are not attested in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and are replaced here with Oberlies's. Oberlies treats the same material historically, under *metathesis quantitatum* (§3.3), compensatory lengthening (§3.4), where *kātuṁ* < *kartum* is his, and the replacement of a long vowel by *-Vṁ-* (§3.5), where *vīsati* < *viṁśati* and *sīha* < *siṁha* are his, together with the intra-Pāli pair *mahisa* ~ *mahiṁso* 'buffalo'. Of that last pair only *mahiṁso* is canonical in this edition, so it is left in the footnote.

None of this has to be memorised. It has to be recognised, so that a form met in a text is seen as the same word in a different guise, not as a new one.

> **Where the rules do not settle it**
>
> Some clusters may be divided either way, and no simple rule decides: *a·brah·ma* or *ab·rah·ma*, and likewise with *by*, *vy*, *dv*, *nh* and *tv*. In prose it makes no difference to the sense. In verse the metre decides, and the division exists for the metre; that is the business of the chapter on metre.

The recollection of the Buddha will show the weights in place. The syllable dots are added here; heavy syllables are in bold, light ones plain.

i·ti·pi **so** bha·ga·**vā** a·ra·**haṁ** **sam·mā·sam·bud·dho** **vij·jā**·ca·ra·ṇa·**sam·pan·no** su·ga·**to** **lo**·ka·vi·**dū** a·**nut**·ta·**ro** pu·ri·sa·**dam**·ma·**sā**·ra·thi **sat·thā** **de**·va·ma·**nus·sā·naṁ** **bud·dho** bha·ga·**vā**. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

"Such is the Blessed One: perfected, fully awakened, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, holy, knower of the world, supreme guide for those fit to be trained, teacher of gods and humans, awakened, blessed."

Two features stand out there. The heavy syllables cluster at the ends of the epithets and in their compounded cores, so the line moves in bursts, not evenly, and that makes it possible to chant. And *de-* in *devamanussānaṁ* is heavy, though only a single *v* follows it: the *e* is long because the syllable is open, and taking it as short produces a limp where the line has none.

Two last practical points. Double consonants are pronounced double, as in English *unnecessary*, *not to*, *say yes*, not as the single consonants they look like to an English eye. And stress is not a feature of Pāli in the way it is of English. There is no consensus on where an ancient speaker put it, and probably the knowledge is simply lost; syllable length carries the timing of the line, as stress does in English. Give every syllable its proper length and you will be understood, whatever you do about stress.

### What the chapter is for

Almost everything above is a description of sound, in a book about a language nobody speaks and a canon most people now meet on a page. It is fair to ask why it comes first.

The answer is that in Pāli the sound *is* the morphology. Vowel length distinguishes a nominative plural from an accusative singular; a doubled consonant is the difference between an active verb and a passive one; the niggahīta is the ending of half the cases in the language. An English reader's eye slides over all three, because none of them is meaningful in English, and to miss *dhammā* against *dhammaṁ* is to miss more than a nicety of pronunciation. It is to miss the case.

This is also the one chapter whose subject the tradition itself treated as a discipline. These texts were held for centuries by people who knew them as sound, and the *sajjhāya* that carried them was exact about quantity because the meaning depended on it. Reading aloud is therefore not a devotional addition to the study of this grammar. It is the fastest way to notice that you have read an ending wrong.
