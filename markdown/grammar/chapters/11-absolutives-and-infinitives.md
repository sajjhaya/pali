## Absolutives and Infinitives

A brahmin farmer, meeting the Buddha at the edge of his field, tells him to go and earn his food as everyone else does:

- Ahaṁ kho, samaṇa, **kasāmi** ca **vapāmi** ca, **kasitvā** ca **vapitvā** ca bhuñjāmi. (SN 7.11 — S i 172)
- *ahaṁ* I (nom. sg.) · *kho* indeed · *samaṇa* ascetic (m. voc. sg.) · *kasāmi* I plough (1 sg. pres.) · *ca* and · *vapāmi* I sow (1 sg. pres.) · *kasitvā* having ploughed (absol.) · *vapitvā* having sown (absol.) · *bhuñjāmi* I eat (1 sg. pres.)
- "I, ascetic, **plough** and **sow**, and **having ploughed** and **sown**, I eat."

The first half of that sentence is two finite verbs joined by *ca*, which says something at the outset: Pāli can coordinate finite verbs, and does. The second half is the same two actions said again in the form Pāli reaches for far more readily: two indeclinable forms leading into a third, not two more finite verbs. That form is the **absolutive** (*pubbakiriyā*, 'the prior action'), also called the gerund, and it names an action completed before the finite verb it depends on.[^ger-vs-grd]

[^ger-vs-grd]: The gerund (ger.) is this chapter's absolutive. It is not the **gerundive** (grd.), Chapter 10's name for the future passive participle. The terms differ by one letter and name different things: an action already complete, against an obligation not yet discharged.

The preference is strong enough to shape how a sutta sentence looks on the page. Long stretches of narrative in the canon consist of a chain of absolutives with a single finite verb at the end, and the practical consequence for a reader is a reading strategy, not a rule to memorise: when a sentence will not resolve, find the finite verb first (it is usually last) and read everything in front of it as a sequence of completed actions leading up to it.

### The absolutive

An absolutive takes no ending for case, gender, number or person. It is an indeclinable, and so it cannot agree with anything; it modifies the action of the clause instead of describing any noun in it. Oberlies counts eight suffixes that form it, of which two account for nearly everything a reader will meet; the remainder are in Appendix A.4.[^abs-eight-suffixes]

[^abs-eight-suffixes]: Oberlies §58, p. 265, lists *-(i)tvā*, *-(i)tvāna(ṁ)*, *-(t)tu(ṁ)*, *-tūna*, *-(i)yă*, *-(i)yāna(ṁ)*, *-eyya* and *-aṁ*, noting that all of them can be added to the present stem. Appendix A.4 gives Ñāṇatusita's own grouping of the same material, which differs in arrangement, not in content.

#### The *-tvā* type

The commonest suffix by a wide margin is *-tvā*, joined to the root or the present stem, often through a connecting *-i-*:

| Form | From | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *katvā* / *karitvā* | *karoti* | having done |
| *gantvā* | *gacchati* | having gone |
| *datvā* / *daditvā* | *dadāti* / *deti* | having given |
| *vatvā* | *vadati* | having said |
| *sutvā* / *suṇitvā* | *suṇāti* | having heard |
| *hitvā* / *jahitvā* | *jahati*[^jahetva-unattested] | having abandoned |

[^jahetva-unattested]: A third variant, *jahetvā*, is sometimes printed beside these two. It does not occur in the root texts of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all, and has a single commentarial attestation, a gloss in the Mahāniddesa-aṭṭhakathā (Nidd-a II 286). *Hitvā* (186 root-text occurrences) and *jahitvā* (85) are both ordinary.

Many verbs offer two absolutives (*katvā* beside *karitvā*, *sutvā* beside *suṇitvā*), and the choice between them carries no difference in meaning. The longer variant *-tvāna* belongs almost entirely to verse, and the formula introducing a sutta's closing stanzas puts it there:

- Idaṁ **vatvāna** sugato athāparaṁ etadavoca satthā. (DN 16 — D ii 90)
- *idaṁ* this (n. acc. sg.) · *vatvāna* having said (absol.) · *sugato* the Well-Gone One (m. nom. sg.) · *atha* then · *aparaṁ* further · *etad* this (n. acc. sg.) · *avoca* said (3 sg. aor.) · *satthā* the Teacher (m. nom. sg.)
- "**Having said** this, the Well-Gone One, the Teacher, then said this further."

#### The *-(i)ya* type

A second suffix, *-ya*, attaches mostly, though not only, to verbs carrying a prefix. Where the root ends in a long *ā*, the suffix simply follows it; where it ends in a consonant or a short vowel, the resulting cluster assimilates, and the outcome is a doubled consonant that can look nothing like a suffix at all:

| Form | From | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *āgamma* | *āgacchati* | having come |
| *upādāya* | *upādiyati* | having taken up |
| *pahāya* | *pajahati* | having abandoned |
| *paccakkhāya* | *paccakkhāti* (*paṭi* + *ā* + √*khā*) | having renounced |
| *samatikkamma* | *samatikkamati* | having passed beyond |
| *upasampajja* | *upasampajjati* | having entered upon |
| *pecca* | *peti* (*pa* + *eti*) | having died |

The last of these shows the assimilation at its most opaque: *pa* + *i* + *tya* gives *pecca*, in which nothing of the suffix survives as a recognisable syllable.[^ya-assimilation] Keep *pecca* apart from *paṭicca*, which the footnote names beside it: the two differ only in their prefix and are otherwise the same formation from the same root, so *pecca* is *pa-* 'onward' (one has *gone on*, and Sanskrit writes it *pretya*), while *paṭicca* is *paṭi-* 'back' and belongs to *pacceti*, Sanskrit *pratītya*.[^pecca-paticca] The reduplication that a present stem may carry does not transfer (*pajahati* has *pahāya*, built on the bare root), the behaviour Chapter 10 noted for the gerundive *pahātabba*.

[^ya-assimilation]: Oberlies §58(5), p. 265: roots in short vowels insert a *-t-* before the suffix, and the cluster is then assimilated. The *-cca* outcome is the most visible product of the rule, in *paṭicca*, *pecca*, *vivicca* and *sakkacca*.

[^pecca-paticca]: DPD, s.v. *pecca*: the gerund of *peti*, *pa* + √*i* > *e* + *tya*, Sanskrit *pretya*; and s.v. *paṭicca*: the gerund of *pacceti*, *pati* + √*i* + *tya*, Sanskrit *pratītya*. The distinction is easy to lose because both are *-cca* absolutives of the same root and only the prefixes separate them; the Sanskrit settles it, *pra-* against *prati-*.

Either type negates with *a-*, becoming *an-* before a vowel, as the participle does in Chapter 10. The first rule of the Pātimokkha turns on two of them in a row, one of each type:

- Yo pana bhikkhu bhikkhūnaṁ sikkhāsājīvasamāpanno sikkhaṁ **apaccakkhāya** dubbalyaṁ **anāvikatvā** methunaṁ dhammaṁ paṭiseveyya … pārājiko hoti asaṁvāso. (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 23)
- *yo pana bhikkhu* whatever monk (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhūnaṁ* of the monks (m. gen. pl.) · *sikkhāsājīvasamāpanno* having undertaken the training and way of life (m. nom. sg.) · *sikkhaṁ* the training (f. acc. sg.) · *apaccakkhāya* not having renounced (absol., negated) · *dubbalyaṁ* his weakness (n. acc. sg.) · *anāvikatvā* not having disclosed (absol., negated) · *methunaṁ dhammaṁ* sexual intercourse (m. acc. sg.) · *paṭiseveyya* should engage in (3 sg. opt.) · *pārājiko* expelled (m. nom. sg.) · *hoti* is · *asaṁvāso* excluded from the community (m. nom. sg.)
- "Whatever monk, having undertaken the monks' training and way of life, **without having renounced** the training and **without having disclosed** his weakness, engages in sexual intercourse … is expelled and excluded from the community."

*Apaccakkhāya* is the negation of the *paccakkhāya* in the table above, and *anāvikatvā* of *āvikaroti* 'makes clear'.[^neg-absolutive] The negation gives the rule its whole force: the two absolutives describe two things the monk could have done and did not, not things he did. A negated absolutive names the road not taken, and here it is the road that would have made the act no offence at all, since a monk who renounces the training first is no longer bound by it.

[^neg-absolutive]: DPD parses *apaccakkhāya* as the gerund of *na paccakkhāti*, literally 'not speaking against', and *anāvikatvā* as *na* + *an* + *āvi* + *katvā*, the absolutive of the compound verb *āvikaroti*. *Apaccakkhāya* has 11 root-text occurrences and *anāvikatvā* 9, and both are rare outside this rule and the passages that quote it. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and the Pali Text Society both print the single *p* at this rule, and so does DPD in its own citation of the line. A doubled *appaccakkhāya* is canonical elsewhere, but it is not the reading here.

#### Whose action is it?

In most sentences the absolutive's unexpressed agent is the subject of the main verb, and a chain of them shares that one subject throughout. The formula closing a teaching encounter is the standard case:

- Atha kho bhagavā soṇadaṇḍaṁ brāhmaṇaṁ dhammiyā kathāya **sandassetvā samādapetvā samuttejetvā sampahaṁsetvā** uṭṭhāyāsanā pakkāmīti. (DN 4 — D i 126)
- *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *soṇadaṇḍaṁ brāhmaṇaṁ* the brahmin Soṇadaṇḍa (m. acc. sg.) · *dhammiyā kathāya* with a talk on the Dhamma (f. inst. sg.) · *sandassetvā* having instructed (absol.) · *samādapetvā* having roused (absol.) · *samuttejetvā* having inspired (absol.) · *sampahaṁsetvā* having gladdened (absol.) · *uṭṭhāya* having risen (absol.) · *āsanā* from the seat (n. abl. sg.) · *pakkāmi* departed (3 sg. aor.) · *ti* (quotation)
- "Then the Blessed One, **having instructed, roused, inspired and gladdened** the brahmin Soṇadaṇḍa with a talk on the Dhamma, rose from his seat and departed."

Five absolutives, one aorist. Every one of the five is the Buddha's action, and the sentence does not need to say so once.

The rule that follows from this, that the absolutive's agent is the main verb's subject, is the one most pedagogical grammars give, and it is right about the great majority of sentences. It is not, however, exceptionless, and the Pāli commentators noticed the exception and gave it a name. Consider the sentence that closes the description of the liberated person:

- Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sabbaso nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṁ **samatikkamma** saññāvedayitanirodhaṁ **upasampajja** viharati. Paññāya cassa **disvā** āsavā parikkhīṇā honti. (MN 25 — M i 160)
- *puna caparaṁ* furthermore · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *sabbaso* entirely (adv.) · *nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṁ* the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception (n. acc. sg.) · *samatikkamma* having passed beyond (absol.) · *saññāvedayitanirodhaṁ* the cessation of perception and feeling (m. acc. sg.) · *upasampajja* having entered upon (absol.) · *viharati* dwells (3 sg. pres.) · *paññāya* with wisdom (f. inst. sg.) · *ca* and · *assa* for him, his (m. gen./dat. sg.) · *disvā* having seen (absol.) · *āsavā* the defilements (m. nom. pl.) · *parikkhīṇā* exhausted (m. nom. pl. pp.) · *honti* are (3 pl. pres.)
- "Furthermore, monks, a monk, **having passed** entirely beyond the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, **having entered** the cessation of perception and feeling, dwells there. And **having seen** with wisdom, his defilements are exhausted."

The first sentence is ordinary: *samatikkamma* and *upasampajja* both belong to *bhikkhu*, the subject of *viharati*. The second is not. The subject of *honti* is *āsavā*, 'the defilements', and the defilements do not do the seeing. The seer is the monk, present in the sentence only as *assa*, a form that is genitive and dative alike and cannot be told apart here.[^assa-case]

[^assa-case]: *Cassa* is *ca* + *assa*. The third-person pronoun's genitive and dative are identical in the singular (Appendix A.2), so the case cannot be settled from the form, and the sentence does not settle it either. For the construction both readings come to the same: whichever case it is, it is not the nominative, and it is not *āsavā*.

The tradition analysed this directly. Dhammapāla, commenting on the Visuddhimagga, sets out the ordinary construction and then turns to this one with the phrase *asamāne pana kattari*, "but when the agent is not the same", and gives two illustrations: the sentence above, and a made-up one that the grammatical tradition passes from hand to hand, *pisācaṁ disvā bhayaṁ hoti*, "having seen a goblin, fear arises."[^asamane-kattari] The example is well chosen, because in English the mismatch passes unnoticed. Fear does not see the goblin. A person does, and the fear results.

[^asamane-kattari]: Visuddhimagga-mahāṭīkā, Paññābhūminiddesa, on the exposition of dependent origination (¶580). *Kattari* is itself a locative absolute (Kaccāyana's own grammatical idiom, Ākhyātakappa 445), so *asamāne kattari* is literally 'the agent being not the same'. Later ṭīkās turn the phrase into a single adjective and call such a form *asamānakattuka*, 'having a different agent'. The *pisāca* sentence is the commentator's illustration, not a canonical quotation.

A second commentary is blunter still. Glossing the same line, the Pañcapakaraṇa-mūlaṭīkā says outright: *na āsavā paññāya passanti*, "the defilements do not see with wisdom", and explains that they are called 'exhausted having seen' because their exhaustion depends on the seeing.[^mulatika-asava] Hold on to that last point, because it shows something the grammar alone would not. Here the absolutive marks *why*, and not merely what happened first. The commentaries gloss *disvā* in this construction with *dassanahetu*, 'by reason of seeing', and set beside it a standing pair of illustrations: *sappiṁ pivitvā balaṁ hoti, sīhaṁ disvā bhayaṁ hoti*, "having drunk ghee, strength arises; having seen a lion, fear arises."[^hetu-attha] Neither strength nor fear performs the action in front of it. Both are its result.

[^mulatika-asava]: Pañcapakaraṇa-mūlaṭīkā, on the Puggalapaññatti's first exposition, the *Ekakaniddesa* (¶24); the same gloss recurs, in nearly the same words, in the Dīgha-nikāya ṭīkā on the *Mahānidānasutta* (Sv-pṭ II 152).

[^hetu-attha]: Udāna-aṭṭhakathā on the *Soṇasutta*, Ud 5.6 (Ud-a 314), which gives all three examples together as the model for the verse's own *disvā* and *ñatvā*, and says those are to be taken as causal in sense (*hetuatthatā*). The Visuddhimagga-mahāṭīkā gives the pair as *ghataṁ pivitvā balaṁ hoti, sīhaṁ disvā bhayaṁ hoti*, calling the *-tvā* form *hetuattha* (Saṅkhārupekkhāñāṇakathā, ¶802); its *pisāca* sentence is at ¶580. The ṭīkā on MN 111 repeats the pair and cites the mahāṭīkā for it (¶96), and the Sāratthadīpanī on the Vinaya's *Sudinnabhāṇavāra* repeats it without a reference (¶24); both describe the form as *hetuattha* 'causal in sense' and *asamānakattuka* 'of a different agent'.

So the rule has two halves. Ordinarily the absolutive's agent is the subject of the main verb, and to assume so is right nearly every time. Where the main clause's subject cannot plausibly have performed the action (where it is an abstract noun like *bhayaṁ* or *āsavā*, and the sentence describes a consequence, not a sequence), the agent is someone else, usually named in the genitive or dative, and the absolutive gives a cause.

The absolutive can also carry the weight of a conditional clause, with no verb in the conditional mood anywhere in the sentence:

- Mañhi, bhante, aññatitthiyā sāvakaṁ **labhitvā** kevalakappaṁ nāḷandaṁ paṭākaṁ parihareyyuṁ. (MN 56 — M i 379)
- *maṁ* me (acc. sg.) · *hi* indeed · *bhante* venerable sir (m. voc. sg.) · *aññatitthiyā* followers of another sect (m. nom. pl.) · *sāvakaṁ* as a disciple (m. acc. sg.) · *labhitvā* having gained (absol.) · *kevalakappaṁ* the whole of (adj., acc. sg.) · *nāḷandaṁ* Nāḷandā (f. acc. sg.) · *paṭākaṁ* a banner (n. acc. sg.) · *parihareyyuṁ* they would carry around (3 pl. opt.)
- "For if followers of another sect **were to gain** me as a disciple, venerable sir, they would carry a banner around the whole of Nāḷandā."

Chapter 8 gave the optative as the ordinary vehicle for the *apodosis*, 'the "then" half', of a conditional sentence. Here the *protasis*, the "if" half, has no verb of its own at all; an absolutive stands in for it.

#### Absolutives that have stopped being verbs

A number of absolutives have hardened into other parts of speech, and are best learned as vocabulary, not parsed each time. The largest group functions as postpositions, governing a case of its own:

| Form | From | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *uddissa* | *uddisati* | for, on account of (+ acc.) |
| *paṭicca* | *pacceti* | dependent on, because of (+ acc.) |
| *ārabbha* | *ārabhati* | concerning, about (+ acc.) |
| *nissāya* / *upanissāya* | *nissayati* | depending on, close to (+ acc.) |
| *upādāya* | *upādiyati* | out of, in consequence of |
| *vivicca* | *viviccati* | secluded from |
| *ṭhapetvā* | *ṭhapeti* | except for, apart from (+ acc.) |

- Cakkhuñca **paṭicca** rūpe ca uppajjati viññāṇaṁ, cakkhuviññāṇantveva saṅkhyaṁ gacchati. (MN 38 — M i 259)
- *cakkhuṁ* the eye (n. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *paṭicca* dependent on (postpos. + acc.) · *rūpe* forms (n. acc. pl.) · *uppajjati* arises (3 sg. pres.) · *viññāṇaṁ* consciousness (n. nom. sg.) · *cakkhuviññāṇaṁ* eye-consciousness (n. nom. sg.) · *tu* but · *eva* just · *saṅkhyaṁ* reckoning (f. acc. sg.) · *gacchati* goes (3 sg. pres.)
- "**Dependent on** the eye and forms, consciousness arises; it is reckoned as just eye-consciousness."

*Paṭicca* is the most consequential word in this list. It is an absolutive used as a preposition, and it names the doctrine on which the whole of the canon's causal analysis is built: *paṭiccasamuppāda*, dependent origination.

*Ṭhapetvā*, literally 'having set aside', has become the ordinary word for *except*, and it is how the Vinaya's definitions carve out what they do not cover:

- **Ṭhapetvā** gāmañca gāmūpacārañca avasesaṁ araññaṁ nāma. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)
- *ṭhapetvā* except for (postpos. + acc.) · *gāmaṁ* an inhabited area (m. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *gāmūpacāraṁ* the access to an inhabited area (m. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *avasesaṁ* the remainder (n. nom. sg.) · *araññaṁ* wilderness (n. nom. sg.) · *nāma* is called
- "**Except for** an inhabited area and its access, the remainder is called 'wilderness'."

Nothing in that sentence is being set anywhere. The verb has given up its own sense and kept only the case it governs, which is the process the whole table records.

A second group has become adverbs outright. *Sakkaccaṁ* 'respectfully, carefully' (112 root-text occurrences) is the absolutive of *sakkaroti*, and *asañcicca* 'unintentionally' (98) is the absolutive of a negated *sañceteti*. Neither is felt as a verb any longer.

The second matters in the Vinaya, and a rule read together with its exemption shows why. The rule against killing runs *Yo pana bhikkhu **sañcicca** pāṇaṁ jīvitā voropeyya, pācittiyaṁ* (Bu Pc 61 — Vin iv 124), 'whatever monk should **intentionally** deprive a living being of life: an offence entailing confession'. The list of exemptions that follows it opens *Anāpatti — asañcicca* (Bu Pc 61), 'there is no offence: unintentionally'. One negated absolutive makes the difference between an offence and none, and it is an adverb in both places, agreeing with nothing and governing nothing. Oberlies notes that the process can go one step further and yield an ordinary noun: *upanidhā* 'comparison' is a feminine noun abstracted from the absolutive *upanidhāya*.[^abstracted-nouns]

[^abstracted-nouns]: Oberlies §58, *rem.* (b), p. 269, which gives a noun stem *asañcicca-* alongside *upanidhā-*. The adverbial *asañcicca* quoted above is the absolutive itself, not that derived noun; the two should be kept apart.

### The infinitive

The infinitive names the bare idea of a verb, with no indication of when it happens: English "to do." Like the absolutive it is indeclinable, and the two share an origin: both are frozen case-forms of an old action noun in *-tu-*, the infinitive from its accusative or dative, the absolutive from its instrumental. The commonest suffix is *-tuṁ*:

| Form | From | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *kātuṁ* | *karoti* | to do |
| *gantuṁ* | *gacchati* | to go |
| *desetuṁ* | *deseti* | to teach |
| *bhāvetuṁ* | *bhāveti* | to develop |
| *sotuṁ* | *suṇāti* | to hear |

One consequence of that shared origin shows on the page. When an infinitive is compounded with *kāma-* 'wanting', it drops its final *-ṁ*: *daṭṭhuṁ* 'to see' plus *kāma* gives *daṭṭhukāmo*, 'wanting to see'. The pattern is very productive (the root texts carry 138 distinct words ending in *-ukāmo*), and recognising it on sight matters, because if you meet *sotukāmo* or *gantukāmo* and look for a stem *sotuka-* you will not find one.[^kama-compounds]

[^kama-compounds]: Oberlies §57 n. 4, p. 263, citing Geiger §207, with *jīvitukāma-* (Dhp 123) and *daṭṭhukāma-* (Sn 685); DPD gives the construction of each as *jīvituṁ + kāma* and *daṭṭhuṁ + kāma*. *Daṭṭhuṁ* belongs to Oberlies's third absolutive suffix, *-(t)tu(ṁ)*, which also supplies the bare absolutive *daṭṭhu* 'having seen': one more sign of how close the two categories are.

Pāli reaches for the infinitive less often than English reaches for "to," because the cases already cover much of the same ground. The dative of an action noun in *-āya* serves the same purpose, and so, less often, does the accusative; Ñāṇatusita lists the dative among the infinitive formations outright.[^dative-infinitive] Chapter 15 takes up the overlap.

[^dative-infinitive]: Appendix A.4 gives "5. (an)āya (dative)" in its list of infinitive suffixes. Oberlies §57, p. 264, gives one example of each, glossing them 'go out in order to see …' (accusative) and *na ca mayaṁ labhāma bhagavantaṁ dassanāya*, "and we did not get an opportunity to see the Blessed One" (dative).

#### Infinitive syntax

An infinitive ordinarily shares its agent with the main verb, and usually precedes it. It attaches most naturally to a predicate of ability, worthiness or ease, and those predicates are frequently impersonal, with no grammatical subject at all. Where that happens, the agent appears in the instrumental, even though the infinitive itself is read actively:

- Na dāni sukaraṁ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke **pariccajituṁ**. (MN 76 — M i 524)
- *na* not · *dāni* now · *sukaraṁ* easy (n. nom. sg.) · *amhehi* by us (1 pl. inst.) · *lābhasakkārasiloke* gain, honour and fame (m. acc. pl.) · *pariccajituṁ* to give up (inf.)
- "It is not easy for us now **to give up** gain, honour and fame."

There is no nominative anywhere in that sentence for *amhehi* to compete with, and that makes room for it: the instrumental marks the one by whom the giving-up would be done. It is the case, with the same function, that Chapter 9 gave for the agent of a passive, appearing here beside an infinitive that is not passive at all.

The contrast between the two forms this chapter has treated is easiest to see where the canon sets them side by side. A monk considers giving up the training:

- Yannūnāhaṁ sikkhaṁ **paccakkhāya** hīnāyāvattitvā bhoge ca **bhuñjeyyaṁ** puññāni ca **kareyyan**ti. (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)
- *yaṁ nūna* what if · *ahaṁ* I (nom. sg.) · *sikkhaṁ* the training (f. acc. sg.) · *paccakkhāya* having renounced (absol.) · *hīnāya* to the lower (n. dat. sg.) · *āvattitvā* having returned (absol.) · *bhoge* possessions (m. acc. pl.) · *ca* and · *bhuñjeyyaṁ* I might enjoy (1 sg. opt.) · *puññāni* merits (n. acc. pl.) · *kareyyaṁ* I might make (1 sg. opt.) · *ti* (quotation)
- "What if I, **having renounced** the training and returned to the lower life, **were to enjoy** possessions and **make** merit?"

The Buddha's reply, a few lines earlier in the same discourse, uses the same two verbs and puts neither in the optative:

- Sakkā bhoge ca **bhuñjituṁ** puññāni ca **kātuṁ**. (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)
- *sakkā* it is possible (indecl.) · *bhoge* possessions (m. acc. pl.) · *ca* and · *bhuñjituṁ* to enjoy (inf.) · *puññāni* merits (n. acc. pl.) · *kātuṁ* to make (inf.)
- "It is possible **to enjoy** possessions and **to make** merit."

Two absolutives and two optatives in the first; two infinitives and no finite verb at all in the second.[^sakka-indecl] The monk frames a course of action he might take and wants; the answer states, flatly, what can be done. Between them they use most of what this chapter contains.

[^sakka-indecl]: *Sakkā* is an indeclinable meaning 'it is possible', historically an old optative, and it takes an infinitive to complete it. It carries no person or number of its own, so the sentence has no finite verb.

**Exercises for this chapter are in the workbook**, under "Exercises: Absolutives and Infinitives", with worked answers at the back of that volume.

**Principal sources.** Oberlies §§57–58 for the suffix inventories and for which formations are really rare; the Visuddhimagga-mahāṭīkā and the Pañcapakaraṇa-mūlaṭīkā for the tradition's analysis of the absolutive whose agent is not the sentence's subject; Thānuttamo for the absolutives frozen into postpositions.
