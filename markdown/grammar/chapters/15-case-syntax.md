## Case Syntax

The declension chapters taught what the endings are. This one is about what they do, and an ending does less than a beginner hopes and more than a translator often notices. Wijesekera put the stakes clearly in his introduction: "A considerable number of wrong translations (sometimes doctrinally important) have been found to contain errors which could only be detected through a more intense study of the Pāli case syntax."[^cs-wijesekera-aim] That is a strong claim from a careful man, and this chapter takes it as its warrant.

[^cs-wijesekera-aim]: Wijesekera, *Syntax of the Cases in the Pāli Nikāyas*, Introduction, p. vii. The thesis was submitted to the School of Oriental and African Studies in 1936 and published by the Postgraduate Institute of Pali and Buddhist Studies, Kelaniya, in 1993.

### One sentence, four accusatives

Before any theory, a sentence every reader of the canon has met a hundred times.

- Atha kho bhagavā kismiñcideva **pakaraṇe** bhikkhu**saṅghaṁ** paṇāmetvā pubbaṇhasamay**aṁ** nivāsetvā pattacīvar**am**ādāya kapilavatth**uṁ** piṇḍ**āya** pāvisi. (SN 22.80 — S iii 91)
- *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *kismiñcideva* for some or other (nt. loc. sg.) · *pakaraṇe* affair, occasion (nt. loc. sg.) · *bhikkhusaṅghaṁ* the Saṅgha of monks (m. acc. sg.) · *paṇāmetvā* having dismissed · *pubbaṇhasamayaṁ* in the morning (m. acc. sg., adverbial) · *nivāsetvā* having dressed · *pattacīvaraṁ* bowl and robe (nt. acc. sg.) · *ādāya* having taken · *kapilavatthuṁ* Kapilavatthu (nt. acc. sg.) · *piṇḍāya* for alms (m. dat. sg.) · *pāvisi* he entered
- Then the Blessed One, having dismissed the Saṅgha of monks **over some matter or other**, dressed **in the morning**, took **his bowl and robe**, and entered **Kapilavatthu for alms**.

Four accusatives, with three different functions. *Bhikkhusaṅghaṁ* and *pattacīvaraṁ* are ordinary objects, the things dismissed and taken. *Kapilavatthuṁ* is not an object at all: nothing is done to Kapilavatthu, and the accusative marks it as the place the going is towards. *Pubbaṇhasamayaṁ* is not even a participant in the action: it is the time at which the action happened, and it functions as an adverb, as the Digital Pali Dictionary in fact files it.[^cs-pubbanhasamayam] Beside them stands one dative, *piṇḍāya*, the purpose of entering, neither the thing entered nor the direction.

[^cs-pubbanhasamayam]: DPD gives two entries: *pubbaṇhasamaya*, masculine noun, 'early morning'; and *pubbaṇhasamayaṁ*, "ind, adv, comp, acc sg of *pubbaṇhasamaya*": the accusative singular listed as an indeclinable in its own right, because it has hardened into one. The freezing of case forms into adverbs is treated in the chapter on indeclinables.

So the *-aṁ* on a noun does not mean 'object'. It means: this word is in the second case, and now look at the verb. That is the whole method of this chapter, and everything else in it is an elaboration.

### Eight endings, six roles

Western grammars of Pāli count eight cases and name them after Latin. The Pāli tradition counts differently, and the difference is not a quibble.

The tradition has eight ***vibhatti***, 'divisions', the sets of endings themselves, numbered, not named: *paṭhamā* the first (nominative), *dutiyā* the second (accusative), and so on to *sattamī* the seventh (locative), with the vocative counted as a variety of the first. Against those eight endings it sets **six** ***kāraka***, 'factors of action', the roles a participant can play in what the verb describes. Aggavaṁsa's definition in the *Saddanīti* is as compressed as a definition gets: *kiriyānimittaṁ kārakaṁ*, "a factor of action is an element of the action"; and again, *kiriyābhisambandhalakkhaṇaṁ kārakaṁ*, "a factor of action is characterised by connection to the action."[^cs-aggavamsa-karaka]

[^cs-aggavamsa-karaka]: Aggavaṁsa, *Saddanīti*, translated with the Pāli in Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students*, pp. 42–51. The six are given there as *kattu-kamma-karaṇa-sampadān'-apādān'-okāsavasena*. Wijesekera §137 reports the same division under the names *kāraka-vibhatti* and *upapada-vibhatti*: the endings that have a definite relation to the action, and the endings that have none.

| *Kāraka* | What it is | Usual case |
|---|---|---|
| *kattā* | the agent — who brings the action about | nominative, or instrumental |
| *kamma* | the object — what the action reaches | accusative |
| *karaṇa* | the means — what the action is done with | instrumental |
| *sampadāna* | the recipient — who receives, or for whom | dative |
| *apādāna* | the point of departure — what is moved away from | ablative |
| *okāsa* | the place — what supports the action | locative |

Two of the eight endings are left over, and the tradition says so explicitly: *na chaṭṭhīvihitattho kārakaṁ, yathā mantanaṁ*: "what is expressed by the sixth case is not a factor of action, nor is address."[^cs-not-karaka] The genitive and the vocative are outside the system, and for a stated reason. A genitive relates one noun to another and reaches the verb only at second hand, if at all. A vocative has no connection to the action at the moment it is uttered: it makes someone available to be addressed, and only then can they be told to go or to eat.

[^cs-not-karaka]: *Saddanīti*, in Collins p. 50. The reason follows there: *kiriyāsambandhābhāvā n' esā kārakatā sambhavati*, "there is no factor of action here because there is no connection with the action."

One caution about the tradition's evidence, since this chapter quotes it repeatedly. The sentences the grammarians use to illustrate their rules are constructed, not quoted. Wijesekera says so in his introduction ("in laying down rules they merely follow the precedence of Sanskrit writers, particularly of Pāṇinī") and again at §30, that both Pāli grammarians "for the most part merely repeat the conventional illustrations of Pāṇinī (e.g., *kaṭaṁ karoti*)." That judgement can now be checked instead of taken on trust. The two tables below (the fourfold locative from the *vutti* on Kaccāyana, and the genitive's relations from the *Saddanīti*) carry fourteen illustrations between them, and not one of the fourteen occurs in a root text; twelve are confined to the grammatical literature and the commentarial texts, and two occur nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all.[^cs-grammarians-examples] They are quoted here because the analysis they illustrate is excellent, and they are labelled as the tradition's throughout. Every canonical example in this chapter carries a citation; the grammarians' do not, because there is no source to cite.

[^cs-grammarians-examples]: Checked form by form. The two that occur nowhere are *ucchusu raso* 'juice in sugarcane' and *bhūmisu manussā* 'people on the earth'. Pāṇini's own mat is instructive: *kaṭaṁ karoti* 'he makes a mat' is the stock Sanskrit example of the object, and the mat is still there in Kaccāyana's *kaṭe nisīdati* 'he sits on the mat' for the locative, four centuries and one language later.

It predicts the structure of the rest of the chapter. The six cases that are *kāraka*s are read against the verb; the genitive is read against the noun beside it. So the genitive most often defeats a beginner, and the grammars' lists of its uses are much longer and vaguer than their lists for the other cases. Wijesekera, who is not much given to complaint, says of the tradition's own treatment of the genitive that it "is not so clear-cut and exhaustive as of the others."[^cs-gen-vague]

[^cs-gen-vague]: Wijesekera §137. Pāṇini's definition of the genitive is *ṣaṣṭī śeṣe*, "the sixth case, for the remainder", which the *Kāśikā* explains as meaning: if none of the other cases applies, use this one. Kaccāyana does better with *sāmismiṁ chaṭṭhī* (Kacc 303), the sixth case denotes the possessor, but then needs six further rules and still does not cover the ground.

Read that way, the sentence the chapter opened with sorts itself out. Each word is set against the verb that governs it, not against the verb at the end of the sentence:

| Word | Case, and what it does | Read against |
|---|---|---|
| *bhagavā* | nominative: the agent, *kattā* | all four verbs |
| *bhikkhusaṅghaṁ* | accusative: the object, *kamma* | *paṇāmetvā* |
| *pubbaṇhasamayaṁ* | accusative: time when | *nivāsetvā* |
| *pattacīvaraṁ* | accusative: the object, *kamma* | *ādāya* |
| *kapilavatthuṁ* | accusative: the goal of motion | *pāvisi* |
| *piṇḍāya* | dative: purpose | *pāvisi* |

Only two of the rows name a *kāraka*, and that is the instructive thing about them. The object and the agent are the tradition's *kamma* and *kattā*. The goal, the time and the purpose are uses of a case, which the sections below take one at a time, and what tells them apart is the word in the right-hand column, not the ending. *Pattacīvaraṁ* and *kapilavatthuṁ* end alike and do different work because *ādāya* takes a thing and *pāvisi* takes a place. *Pubbaṇhasamayaṁ* is set against *nivāsetvā*, the action it stands in front of, though the morning is the time of everything after it as well.

### Why the cases run together

The other thing to know before reading any of the cases in detail is that several of them have partly merged, and the merger is in the *endings*, not in anybody's analysis. This is the single most useful structural fact in the chapter and it is nowhere stated as a rule; it has to be assembled out of three of Wijesekera's introductory sections.[^cs-mergers]

[^cs-mergers]: Wijesekera §§116, 138 and 162 respectively, for the instrumental–ablative, the dative–genitive, and why the locative survived. He notes at §162 that Pāli preserved a distinct locative where Greek, Latin and Germanic did not, and that this was possible because the other two mergers had already relieved the pressure.

| Cases | Where they are identical | Where they stay apart |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumental and ablative | throughout the plural (*-ehi*, *-āhi*, *-ūhi*); and in the *-ā* singular of the *a*-stems | the *a*-stem singular *-ena* against *-asmā*, *-amhā* |
| Dative and genitive | everywhere, in both numbers | one form only: the *a*-stem dative singular *-āya* |
| Feminine *ā*-stems | *-āya* is instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive **and** locative in the singular | nowhere, in the singular |
| Locative | — | distinct in every declension |

Where the forms merged, the functions bled into each other, and Pāli now uses each of the pair in senses that properly belong to the other. Where the forms stayed apart, the functions stayed comparatively clean. So a beginner finds the accusative and the locative easiest, while the instrumental, ablative, dative and genitive seem to shade into one another: they do, and the reason is morphological. It also explains why the feminine *ā*-stems are the hardest nouns in the language to read, a point the declension chapter made about the forms and this chapter now uses.

Perniola ends his own chapter on the cases with the same observation, arrived at from the other direction: "Paying attention to the uses of the cases, one may have noticed how the same idea can be expressed by means of different cases. This indistinctness of the cases started already in Sanskrit and became much more common in Pali."[^cs-perniola-indistinct]

[^cs-perniola-indistinct]: Perniola §266, p. 337, the last words of his chapter XIX.

### The nominative

The nominative marks the **grammatical subject**: the word that changes number when the verb changes number. That much is mechanical, and the declension chapters have already used it.

It does not mark the agent. The two coincide in an active sentence and come apart in a passive one, and Pāli's fondness for the passive makes this a daily problem, not an occasional one. The tradition sees it clearly: Aggavaṁsa divides agents into the *abhihitakattā*, the agent "expressed" by the verb's own ending, and the *anabhihitakattā*, the agent the verb ending does not express and which must therefore be named separately, in the instrumental.[^cs-abhihita]

[^cs-abhihita]: *Saddanīti*, in Collins pp. 43–44, with the examples *puriso maggaṁ gacchati* (expressed) against *sūdena paciyati odano* and *ahinā daṭṭho naro* (unexpressed). Wijesekera §29 makes the related point that Indian grammar did not distinguish subject from agent at all, using *kattā* for both.

The formula every Buddhist knows is a passive sentence and shows this:

- **Svākkhāto** bhagav**atā** dhamm**o** sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko paccattaṁ veditabbo viññūhi. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)
- *svākkhāto*<sub>a</sub> well proclaimed (m. nom. sg., past participle) · *bhagavatā* by the Blessed One (m. instr. sg.) · *dhammo*<sub>a</sub> the teaching (m. nom. sg.) · *sandiṭṭhiko*<sub>a</sub> visible here and now · *akāliko*<sub>a</sub> timeless · *ehipassiko*<sub>a</sub> inviting inspection · *opaneyyiko*<sub>a</sub> leading onwards · *paccattaṁ* individually (adverbial acc.) · *veditabbo*<sub>a</sub> to be known (m. nom. sg., gerundive) · *viññūhi* by the wise (m. instr. pl.)
- The teaching is well proclaimed **by the Blessed One**, visible here and now, timeless, inviting inspection, leading onwards, to be known individually **by the wise**.

*Dhammo* is the nominative and *bhagavatā* is the agent, and they are different words. Equate nominative with agent and you will render this as though the Dhamma proclaimed something.

**Predicative nominative.** A second nominative can stand beside the first as what the subject is said to be, whether or not a form of *hoti* or *atthi* is present:

- Sīle patiṭṭhāya **naro** **sapañño**, cittaṁ paññañca bhāvayaṁ. (SN 1.23 — S i 13)
- *sīle* in virtue (nt. loc. sg.) · *patiṭṭhāya* having established himself · *naro*<sub>a</sub> a person (m. nom. sg.) · *sapañño*<sub>a</sub> wise (m. nom. sg.) · *cittaṁ* mind (nt. acc. sg.) · *paññañ* < *paññaṁ* wisdom (f. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *bhāvayaṁ*<sub>a</sub> developing (m. nom. sg., present participle)
- A **wise person**, established in virtue, developing mind and wisdom …

Where no verb appears at all the sentence is called **nominal**, and a form of *hoti* is understood. English does this too, though rarely and mostly in proverbs.

### The accusative

The accusative is the *kamma*, the object; Kaccāyana's definition is *yaṁ karoti taṁ kammaṁ*, "what one does is the object" (Kacc 282). Wijesekera notes that this covers verbs of making rather well and other verbs less well, and that Moggallāna, who says instead that the object is "what is done by, or intimately connected with, the action of the agent", comes nearer to the case's function.[^cs-acc-definitions]

[^cs-acc-definitions]: Wijesekera §30, with Pāṇini I.4.49 (*karturīpsitatamaṁ*, "what is most desired by the agent") and Moggallāna II.2 for comparison. His judgement of all three is that they "try to explain the connection between the agent and the object" instead of defining the object, "due to the common characteristic of all Indian grammarians, namely, dealing with syntax only from the point of view of the agent."

Five uses cover nearly everything a reader meets.

**The object proper**, which needs no illustration beyond the sentence at the head of this chapter.

**The internal object**: a noun from the same root as the verb, or of the same meaning, which gives the action something to be measured by more than it adds a participant. English has a little of this in 'sing a song', 'dream a dream'; Pāli has a great deal:

- Atha kho bhagavā imaṁ **udānaṁ** udānesi. (SN 56.11 — S v 424)
- *atha kho* then · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *imaṁ* this (nt. acc. sg.) · *udānaṁ* inspired utterance (nt. acc. sg.) · *udānesi* uttered
- Then the Blessed One **uttered this inspired utterance**.

The construction is far commoner than its oddity in English suggests, and Perniola's observation about it holds: the internal object is almost never bare, but is qualified by a pronoun or an adjective, which is what gives the phrase its content.[^cs-internal-object]

[^cs-internal-object]: Perniola §249, which opens on p. 310 and draws its conclusion on p. 311: "the word which expresses the object of the verb is never alone but always qualified by a pronoun, an adjective, a noun." He adds that *kappeti* is the verb to watch, since it takes a range of internal objects and must be translated by whichever English verb the object calls for: *seyyaṁ kappeti* 'lies down', *vāsaṁ kappeti* 'takes up residence', *jīvitaṁ kappeti* 'makes a living'.

**The goal of motion**, as *kapilavatthuṁ* above. Pāli has three ways of saying where the going goes (accusative, dative and locative), and they are not interchangeable in emphasis, though they overlap. The accusative is the plain one.

Extent in time and space, and **time when**. *So cattāro māse parivasati*, "he lives on probation **for four months**" (DN 16 — D ii 152); *dvādasa yojanāni*, 'twelve leagues'; and *ekaṁ samayaṁ*, 'on one occasion', which opens most of the suttas in the canon.

Two accusatives with one verb, where the second is what the object is called or made:

- Tam**ahaṁ** brūmi **brāhmaṇaṁ**. (Dhp 385 — Dhp p. 55)
- *taṁ*<sub>a</sub> him (m. acc. sg.) · *ahaṁ* I (nom.) · *brūmi* I call · *brāhmaṇaṁ*<sub>a</sub> a brahmin (m. acc. sg.)
- **Him** I call **a brahmin**.

The verbs that behave this way are those meaning to call, to name, to appoint, to choose; and, separately, verbs of asking and telling, which take an accusative of the person as well as of the thing. When such a verb is turned passive the person becomes nominative and the thing stays accusative: *Buddho tena pañhaṁ puṭṭho*, "the Buddha was asked a question by him."[^cs-double-acc]

[^cs-double-acc]: Perniola §251, pp. 313–315. His list of verbs taking two accusatives is *yacati, vatti* (from *vac*), *vadati, pucchati, duhati*, and he notes the fixed phrase *Buddhaṁ saraṇaṁ gacchāmi* as a further instance. The first is printed with a short *a*, as is the *khiraṁ* of his example below — two more of the vowel-quantity slips his profile records; read *yācati* and *khīraṁ*. Two entries are citation forms, not words found in the canon. *Duhati* 'milks' is illustrated by an uncited specimen, *gāviṁ khīraṁ duhati*, the grammarians' stock sentence and not a canonical one (the root is real and the canon has *duhanti* and *duhāmase*, but not this). And *vatti* 'says' is a form the chapter on absolutives already had reason to distrust: under that spelling the texts have an aorist of a different verb.

### The instrumental

The *karaṇa*, "that by which an action is done" (Kacc 290). Brugmann's name for it is better than the Latin one and Wijesekera adopts it: the **with-case**, since 'with' in English covers both the instrument and the company.[^cs-with-case]

[^cs-with-case]: Wijesekera §61, citing Brugmann's *Mit-Kasus*. He observes at §62 that although modern grammarians treat the instrumental of agent as a variety of the instrumental of means, "there is however a fundamental difference both grammatically and logically between the two": the agent is not an auxiliary but the doer, and the Indian grammarians who keep *kattu-kāraka* and *karaṇa-kāraka* apart are right to.

**Means.** Aggavaṁsa divides these into internal and external (*taṁ duvidhaṁ ajjhattikabāhiravasena*), and the division is doctrinal as much as grammatical, because the internal means are the sense faculties. *Cakkhunā rūpaṁ passati* 'he sees a form with the eye', *manasā dhammaṁ vijānāti* 'he cognises a mental object with the mind'; against *hatthena kammaṁ karoti* 'he does work with the hand', *pharasunā rukkhaṁ chindati* 'he cuts a tree with an axe'.[^cs-internal-external] The internal set is the more important for a reader of the suttas, because the whole analysis of experience is phrased in it.

[^cs-internal-external]: *Saddanīti*, in Collins p. 46. Here the tradition does quote: his two internal examples occur in the root texts and his two external ones do not, which is what one would expect of a grammarian who reaches for the canon when it has a sentence to hand and constructs one when it does not.

Agent of a passive or causative verb, as *bhagavatā* above.

**Accompaniment**, usually reinforced by *saddhiṁ* or *saha*, though the bare instrumental will do it. The reinforcement is so nearly universal in Pāli that the bare sociative reads as archaic.

**Cause and manner.** *Kenaci karaṇīyena* 'on account of some business'; *tuṇhībhāvena* 'in silence'; *sirasā vandati* 'pays homage with the head'.

**Route and time.** The instrumental of the way through which motion passes (*yena dvārena nikkhami* 'the gate by which he went out') and of the time within which something happens.

**Direction, in the *yena … tena* construction.** This is one of the most frequent sentence patterns in the canon and belongs here, not under the accusative:

- Atha kho aññatarā devatā abhikkantāya rattiyā abhikkantavaṇṇā kevalakappaṁ jetavanaṁ obhāsetvā **yena** bhagavā **ten**upasaṅkami. (SN 1.1 — S i 1)
- *atha kho* then · *aññatarā devatā* a certain deity (f. nom. sg.) · *abhikkantāya rattiyā* when the night was far gone (f. loc. sg. absolute) · *abhikkantavaṇṇā* of surpassing beauty · *kevalakappaṁ* the whole of (m. acc. sg.) · *jetavanaṁ* Jeta's Grove (nt. acc. sg.) · *obhāsetvā* having illuminated · *yena* by which, where (m. instr. sg.) · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *tena* by that, there (m. instr. sg.) · *upasaṅkami* < *upa* + *saṁ* + √*kam* + *i* — he approached (aor.)
- Then, late in the night, a certain deity of surpassing beauty, lighting up the whole of Jeta's Grove, **went up to** the Blessed One.

Literally it is "by which the Blessed One, by that he approached": a relative and correlative pair in the instrumental, with the person in the nominative, not in any case governed by the verb. English has nothing corresponding and the idiom simply has to be learnt.

Comparison, and the words that take it. The second term of a comparison may go into the instrumental (*na tena seyyo* 'none better than he'), and three common words govern it outright: *alaṁ* 'enough of', *kiṁ* 'what is the use of', and *attho* 'there is need of': *puññena attho* 'there is need of merit'.

**With *sakkā* and its relatives.** The impersonal *sakkā* 'it is possible', *labbhā* 'it is obtainable', *dujjānaṁ* 'it is hard to know', *sukaraṁ* 'it is easy to do' take an infinitive and put the person in the instrumental. Poṭṭhapāda's question and the Buddha's reply are the construction twice over, once in each person:[^cs-sakka]

[^cs-sakka]: Perniola §253 N.B., p. 316. This is the ordinary Pāli way of saying "it is possible for someone to do something", and no construction with a nominative would serve instead.

- **Sakkā** panetaṁ, bhante, **mayā** ñātuṁ … **Dujjānaṁ** kho etaṁ, poṭṭhapāda, **tayā** … (DN 9 — D i 187, 24.3)
- *sakkā* it is possible (ind.) · *pana* but · *etaṁ* this (nt. acc. sg.) · *bhante* sir (voc.) · *mayā* by me, for me (1st instr. sg.) · *ñātuṁ* to know (inf.) · *dujjānaṁ* hard to know (nt. nom. sg.) · *kho* indeed · *poṭṭhapāda* Poṭṭhapāda (voc.) · *tayā* by you, for you (2nd instr. sg.)
- "But is it possible **for me** to know this, sir?" … "That is hard **for you** to know, Poṭṭhapāda …"

### The dative

The *sampadāna*, whose name means giving-over and whose commonest sense is therefore the recipient. In Pāli the case is nearly extinct in form. Only the *a*-stem singular in *-āya* is a true dative; everywhere else the genitive endings serve for both, and Perniola accordingly declines to discuss any dative but the *-āya* one, classing the rest under the genitive.[^cs-dative-form]

[^cs-dative-form]: Perniola §259, p. 325: "In Pali only the thematic stems have a true dative case in *-āya*." Wijesekera §138 gives the history: the genitive had begun to encroach on the dative as early as the Brāhmaṇas, went further in the Epics, and in Prākrit the dative is obsolete altogether but for traces in the dialect of the dramas.

**Purpose** is what the *-āya* dative overwhelmingly expresses, and the formula that says what the Dhamma is for is seven of them in a row:

- Etaṁ **nibbidāya** **virāgāya** **nirodhāya** **upasamāya** **abhiññāya** **sambodhāya** **nibbānāya** saṁvattati. (DN 9 — D i 189)
- *etaṁ* this (nt. nom. sg.) · *nibbidāya* to disenchantment (f. dat. sg.) · *virāgāya* to dispassion (m. dat. sg.) · *nirodhāya* to cessation · *upasamāya* to peace · *abhiññāya* to direct knowledge · *sambodhāya* to awakening · *nibbānāya* to extinguishment · *saṁvattati* leads
- This leads **to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to peace, to direct knowledge, to awakening, to extinguishment.**

**Recipient**, with verbs of giving; and, hardly distinguishable from it, the person for whose advantage or disadvantage something happens. Wijesekera keeps a separate heading for the **sympathetic dative**, the dative of the person whose interest is engaged, not whose benefit is at stake, and traces the whole dative-genitive collapse back to it: the sympathetic use began with the enclitics *me* and *te*, spread to proper names, and finally to nouns generally.[^cs-sympathetic] The chapter on pronouns has already met the result of that history in *evaṁ me sutaṁ*, where the tradition itself declines to say whether *me* is instrumental or genitive.

[^cs-sympathetic]: Wijesekera §§91 and 102, drawing on Havers for the Vedic rule that "with pronouns of the first and second persons the sympathetic dative is employed, but the genitive with all other pronouns, nouns and participles."

The recipient is at its plainest in the formal acts of the Sangha, because a legal text has to say who receives. The procedure for a robe that has been forfeited ends with the Sangha handing it back:

- Yadi saṅghassa pattakallaṁ, saṅgho imaṁ cīvaraṁ **itthannāmassa bhikkhuno** dadeyya. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196)
- *yadi* if · *saṅghassa* for the Sangha (m. dat. sg.) · *pattakallaṁ* the right time has come (nt. nom. sg.) · *saṅgho* the Sangha (m. nom. sg.) · *imaṁ*<sub>a</sub> this (nt. acc. sg.) · *cīvaraṁ*<sub>a</sub> robe (nt. acc. sg.) · *itthannāmassa*<sub>b</sub> of such-and-such a name (m. dat. sg.) · *bhikkhuno*<sub>b</sub> to the monk (m. dat. sg.) · *dadeyya* should give (3 sg. opt.)
- "If the Sangha is ready, let the Sangha give this robe **to the monk so-and-so**."

Agent, object, recipient and a verb of giving, in that order, and every one of the datives ends in the genitive's *-assa* or *-uno*. The same rule's opening sentence coordinates three more of them: *nissajjitabbaṁ saṅghassa vā gaṇassa vā puggalassa vā*, "it is to be relinquished to the Sangha, or to a group, or to an individual" (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196). Not one true dative ending appears in either sentence. Take every *-assa* for a possessive and you turn the recipients into owners.

Purpose expressed as a verbal noun, where the dative of an action noun stands in for an infinitive: *kassapaṁ bhagavantaṁ … dassanāya upasaṅkamissāma*, "let us approach the Blessed One Kassapa in order to see him" (MN 81 — M ii 46). Chapter 11 met the same interchange from the infinitive's side.

**The dative that carries a doctrine.** The last clause of the arahant formula is a dative, and getting it wrong changes what the sentence claims:

- ‘Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṁ brahmacariyaṁ, kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ, nāparaṁ **itthattāyā**’ti pajānāti. (DN 2 — D i 84)
- *khīṇā* exhausted (f. nom. sg.) · *jāti* birth (f. nom. sg.) · *vusitaṁ* lived out · *brahmacariyaṁ* the spiritual life · *kataṁ* done · *karaṇīyaṁ* what was to be done · *na* not · *aparaṁ* further · *itthattāya* for this state of being (nt. dat. sg.) · *ti* end of quotation · *pajānāti* he understands
- He understands: 'Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived out, what was to be done has been done, **there is no further coming to this state of being**.'

*Itthatta* is an abstract noun, *ittha* 'thus, here' plus *-tta*, 'the state of being thus'; *itthattāya* is its dative singular. The proof lies in a passage where the same dative governs a verb of coming-into-being (*api nu kho nāmarūpaṁ itthattāya abhinibbattissathā ti*, "would name-and-form be produced into this state of being?", DN 15 — D ii 63), not in the formula, where the word stands alone, and also in the commentary, which glosses the formula's *itthattāya* with a string of further datives, *itthabhāvāya … kilesakkhayāya*.[^cs-itthattaya] Rhys Davids read the phrase cosmologically and rendered it "after this present world there is no beyond". It is about coming back, not about a world.

[^cs-itthattaya]: Wijesekera §96, giving the argument and the correction. The commentarial gloss, in the words of the Papañcasūdanī on MN 4: *nāparaṁ itthattāyāti idāni puna itthabhāvāya evaṁsoḷasakiccabhāvāya, kilesakkhayāya vā maggabhāvanākiccaṁ me natthīti* (Ps I 128). The Sumaṅgalavilāsinī on DN 2 gives the same string of datives with *kilesakkhayabhāvāya vā kattabbaṁ* for the last member (Sv I 226). DPD gives the construction as *ittha* + *tta*, Sanskrit *itthatva*, and cites the formula itself. The form is printed in the canon almost always as *itthattāyāti*, the final vowel lengthened before *ti*, so the bare word is almost never found.

### The ablative

The *apādāna*, literally 'a taking away from': Kaccāyana defines it as that "from which there is a moving away, or fear, or a taking" (Kacc 273). Speyer's remark, which Wijesekera endorses, is that the ablative is the exact opposite of the dative: the one is *from*, the other is *for*.[^cs-abl-dat]

[^cs-abl-dat]: Wijesekera §§116–117. He adds that the ablative in Pāli "is on the whole an adverbal case, there being hardly any adnominal uses" (§119), which matters, because an ablative in a sentence is then almost always to be read against the verb and almost never against the noun beside it.

**Starting point**, whether of place, person or thing, and by easy extension **origin** and **descent**.

**Separation, release, abstention**: the sense in which the precepts are phrased:

- **Musāvādā** veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ samādiyāmi. (Kp 2)
- *musāvādā* from false speech (m. abl. sg.) · *veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ* < *veramaṇī* abstaining + *sikkhāpadaṁ* training rule — the training rule of abstaining (nt. acc. sg.) · *samādiyāmi* I undertake
- I undertake the training rule of abstaining **from false speech**.

The ablative here is governed by the *veramaṇī* buried inside the compound, not by *samādiyāmi*, and so the phrase survives being compounded at all. Thānuttamo lists 'abstention from' as one of the ablative's senses and names *ārati* 'abstinence' as another noun that takes it. Wijesekera would want it added that adnominal ablatives in the Nikāyas are few and mostly presuppose a verb that has dropped out.[^cs-abl-adnominal]

[^cs-abl-adnominal]: Thānuttamo p. 67 for the sense and for *ārati*; Wijesekera §119 for the caution. Perniola §257(c), pp. 322–323, files the whole group (separation, freedom, release, warding off) under the verb, and gives only verbal examples.

**Cause and reason**, which is the ablative that carries dependent origination:

- **Jātipaccayā** jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)
- *jātipaccayā* with birth as condition (m. abl. sg.) · *jarāmaraṇaṁ* ageing and death (nt. nom. sg.) · *sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā* sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness and distress (m. nom. pl.) · *sambhavanti* come to be
- **With birth as condition**, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness and distress come to be.

**Comparison**, the second term standing in the ablative: *suttā jāgaritaṁ seyyo*, "waking is better **than sleep**" (Iti 47 — It p. 41).

**Point of view**, and this one has a signature. When the ablative means 'as, in terms of, regarded from the side of', it is very often formed with the suffix *-to* instead of an ordinary ending, and the chapter on nouns has already introduced that suffix. The Mahāpadāna Sutta's list of what the Buddha recollects is a string of them: *jātitopi anussarati, nāmatopi anussarati, gottatopi anussarati*, "he recollects them **by birth**, **by name**, **by clan**" (DN 14).[^cs-abl-to]

[^cs-abl-to]: Perniola cites the same passage at §257(d) with the note that "in this sense, the ablative is usually formed with the suffix *-to*." Wijesekera §118 observes that *-to* is not a proper case ending even in Sanskrit and is used in the Nikāyas with every kind of stem, mostly adverbially. Every occurrence in the passage is printed with the enclitic *pi* joined on (*gottatopi*, not *gottato*), and so the bare form appears unattested until one looks for it as part of a longer word.

### The locative

The *okāsa* in Kaccāyana's terminology and the *adhikaraṇa* in Pāṇini's, but the tradition's preferred word is ***ādhāra***, 'support': the place holds the action up as well as being where it happens.[^cs-adhara] The metaphor deserves to be taken seriously, because it unifies uses that otherwise look unrelated.

[^cs-adhara]: Wijesekera §161, giving Kacc 280 *yo'dhāro tam okāsaṁ* and Moggallāna II.34 *sattamyādhāre*, and noting that Kaccāyana uniquely prefers *okāsa* 'space, opportunity' to *ādhāra*. Aggavaṁsa's gloss in Collins p. 49 spells out the metaphor: in 'Devadatta sits on the mat', the mat supports Devadatta and so supports the sitting.

The tradition divides the locative fourfold, and the division is a good one:

| Type | What it means | The tradition's example |
|---|---|---|
| *byāpika* | pervading — the support is permeated by what it supports | *tilesu telaṁ* 'oil in sesame seeds' |
| *opasilesika* | in contact — two separate things touching | *kaṭe nisīdati* 'he sits on the mat' |
| *sāmīpika* | neighbouring — in the vicinity of | *sāvatthiyaṁ viharati* 'he stays at Sāvatthī' |
| *vesayika* | domain — the sphere a kind of thing occupies | *jalesu macchā* 'fish in the waters' |

Every one of those examples is a grammarians' sentence, not a canonical one. The *vutti* offers eight illustrations across the four types; six of them (*tilesu telaṁ*, *kaṭe nisīdati*, *jalesu macchā*, *dadhimhi sappi*, *ākāse sakuṇā*, *gaṅgāyaṁ vajo*) occur outside the root texts only, five of those six in the grammatical literature alone; and the remaining two, *ucchusu raso* and *bhūmisu manussā*, occur nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all.[^cs-vutti-examples] That is normal for a rule illustrated by a rule-book and it does not weaken the analysis, but a reader should know that the Buddha did not say these familiar sentences. The canon supplies its own instances readily enough: *ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā sāvatthiyaṁ viharati* is the *sāmīpika* type, and it opens hundreds of suttas.

[^cs-vutti-examples]: The fourfold division is from the *vutti* on Kacc 280, reported at Wijesekera §161 and given with Aggavaṁsa's expansion in Collins pp. 49–50. Checked form by form against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana: none of the eight occurs in a root text; five occur only in the grammarians' own works, one (*ākāse sakuṇā*) in a sub-commentary besides, and two nowhere at all.

Beyond place, three uses account for most locatives a reader meets.

**Time when.**

- Ajjeva **rattiyā pacchime yāme** tathāgatassa parinibbānaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 139)
- *ajja* today · *eva* just · *rattiyā* of the night (f. gen. sg.) · *pacchime* last (m. loc. sg.) · *yāme* in the watch (m. loc. sg.) · *tathāgatassa* of the Tathāgata (m. gen. sg.) · *parinibbānaṁ* final extinguishment (nt. nom. sg.) · *bhavissati* will be
- This very day, **in the last watch of the night**, the Tathāgata's final extinguishment will take place.

The genitive *rattiyā* inside the locative phrase has the ordinary partitive sense of a genitive: the watch is a part of the night.

**Reference**: 'with regard to', the *nimitta-sattamī* of the grammarians. The analytical suttas use this locative most:

- Evaṁ passaṁ, bhikkhave, sutavā ariyasāvako **rūpasmimpi** nibbindati, **vedanāyapi** nibbindati, **saññāyapi** nibbindati, **saṅkhāresupi** nibbindati, **viññāṇasmimpi** nibbindati. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)
- *evaṁ* thus · *passaṁ* seeing (m. nom. sg., present participle) · *bhikkhave* monks (voc. pl.) · *sutavā* learned (m. nom. sg.) · *ariyasāvako* noble disciple (m. nom. sg.) · *rūpasmiṁ* regarding form (nt. loc. sg.) · *pi* also · *nibbindati* becomes disenchanted · *vedanāya* regarding feeling (f. loc. sg.) · *saññāya* regarding perception (f. loc. sg.) · *saṅkhāresu* regarding volitional formations (m. loc. pl.) · *viññāṇasmiṁ* regarding consciousness (nt. loc. sg.)
- Seeing thus, monks, a learned noble disciple becomes disenchanted **with form**, disenchanted **with feeling**, disenchanted **with perception**, disenchanted **with volitional formations**, disenchanted **with consciousness**.

That sentence is the whole chapter in one line. Three of the five words are unmistakably locative: *rūpasmiṁ*, *saṅkhāresu*, *viññāṇasmiṁ* carry endings that can only be locative. Two of them, *vedanāya* and *saññāya*, are feminine *ā*-stems whose *-āya* could be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locative, and the words themselves do not say which. They are locative because the other three are, and because *nibbindati* takes a locative. The construction decides, and the ending only narrows.

**Contact, with verbs of seizing.** Where English uses 'by', Pāli puts the part taken hold of in the locative:

- Seyyathāpi, bho gotama, puriso purisaṁ narakapapātaṁ patantaṁ **kesesu** gahetvā uddharitvā thale patiṭṭhapeyya. (DN 12 — D i 234)
- *seyyathāpi* just as · *bho gotama* good Gotama (voc.) · *puriso* a man (m. nom. sg.) · *purisaṁ* a man (m. acc. sg.) · *narakapapātaṁ* into a pit (m. acc. sg.) · *patantaṁ* falling (m. acc. sg., present participle) · *kesesu* by the hair (m. loc. pl.) · *gahetvā* having seized · *uddharitvā* having pulled out · *thale* on firm ground (nt. loc. sg.) · *patiṭṭhapeyya* would set down
- Just as, good Gotama, a man might seize **by the hair** another man falling into a pit, pull him out, and set him on firm ground.

Both locatives in that sentence are the *opasilesika* type of the table above, contact between two separately established things: the hand on the hair and the man on the ground.

### The genitive, which is not a role

Everything so far has been read against the verb. The genitive is read against the noun beside it, and so the tradition leaves it out of the *kāraka*s.

Aggavaṁsa's own list of what the genitive can express is the most useful thing any source says about it, because it is short, and because it makes plain that 'possession' is only the commonest member of a set:[^cs-gen-relations]

[^cs-gen-relations]: *Saddanīti*, in Collins p. 50, with the note that the genitive "is usually known, by synecdoche, by one relation it expresses, ownership, *sāmi*."

| Relation | Sense | The tradition's example |
|---|---|---|
| *sāmi* | ownership | (named, not illustrated) |
| *bhacca* | maintained by | *rañño puriso* 'the king's man' |
| *santaka* | belonging to | *rañño raṭṭhaṁ* 'the king's realm' |
| *samīpa* | in the vicinity of | *ambavanassa avidūre* 'not far from the mango grove' |
| *samūha* | accumulation of | *dhaññānaṁ rāsi* 'a heap of grain' |
| *avayava* | part of | *rukkhassa sākhā* 'a branch of a tree' |
| *vikāra* | alteration of | *suvaṇṇassa vikati* 'a thing made of gold' |

*Sāmi*, ownership, is the only relation Aggavaṁsa names without illustrating: it is the sense he takes the genitive to be known by, "by synecdoche", and the other six are what he wants added to it. His reason for putting the whole set outside the *kāraka*s is also the clearest short statement of what a genitive is. When a man is called *rañño puriso*, it is because the king provides and the man receives that the phrase means anything — and neither the providing nor the receiving is in the sentence.

To that, Western grammar adds two divisions the tradition does not make, and they matter. A **subjective** genitive names the doer of the action the head noun describes; an **objective** genitive names what it is done to. The phrase *tathāgatassa dassanaṁ* could be either (the Tathāgata's seeing of something, or somebody's seeing of the Tathāgata), and in the only place it occurs in the canon, a second genitive settles it:

- Idaṁ pacchimakaṁ, ānanda, **tathāgatassa** **vesāliyā** dassanaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 122)
- *idaṁ* this (nt. nom. sg.) · *pacchimakaṁ* last (nt. nom. sg.) · *ānanda* Ānanda (voc.) · *tathāgatassa* of the Tathāgata (m. gen. sg., subjective) · *vesāliyā* of Vesālī (f. gen. sg., objective) · *dassanaṁ* seeing (nt. nom. sg.) · *bhavissati* will be
- This, Ānanda, will be **the Tathāgata's** last sight **of Vesālī**.

Two genitives on one noun, one on each side of it, with opposite functions. The endings do not show which is which; the world does, because a city cannot look at a person.

Genitives also nest, and then the reader has to decide which noun each one hangs on. The Vinaya defines the precincts of a village by measuring them, and the measurement is a chain:

- Parikkhittassa **gāmassa** indakhīle ṭhitassa **majjhimassa purisassa** leḍḍupāto. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)
- *parikkhittassa*<sub>a</sub> enclosed (m. gen. sg.) · *gāmassa*<sub>a</sub> of an inhabited area (m. gen. sg.) · *indakhīle* at the gate-post (m. loc. sg.) · *ṭhitassa*<sub>b</sub> standing (m. gen. sg. p.p.) · *majjhimassa*<sub>b</sub> of middling stature (m. gen. sg.) · *purisassa*<sub>b</sub> of a man (m. gen. sg.) · *leḍḍupāto* a stone's throw (m. nom. sg.)
- "**A stone's throw of a man of middling stature standing at the gate-post of an enclosed inhabited area.**"

Five of the seven words are genitives, and they do not all attach to the same thing. *Majjhimassa purisassa* belongs to *leḍḍupāto*: it is his throw. *Parikkhittassa gāmassa* belongs to *indakhīle*: it is the village's gate-post, and *indakhīle* is a locative inside the phrase that *ṭhitassa* heads. One genitive phrase therefore sits inside the other, at a depth no ending records. What can sensibly own what settles it. A village has a gate-post. It does not have a stone's throw.

*Ṭhitassa* deserves a second look, because it looks like the construction Chapter 16 calls the genitive absolute, a past participle in the genitive with a genitive noun beside it. It is not one. An absolute detaches its subject from the main clause, and this man is not detached from anything: he is the man whose throw is being measured, and the participle agrees with him as an adjective would.

The **partitive** genitive names the whole out of which a part is taken, and turns up regularly with superlatives and with words meaning 'one of'. The sentence that records the Buddha's last disciple is one:

- Aññataro kho panāyasmā subhaddo **arahataṁ** ahosi. (DN 16 — D ii 153)
- *aññataro* a certain one (m. nom. sg.) · *kho pana* moreover · *āyasmā* the venerable (m. nom. sg.) · *subhaddo* Subhadda (m. nom. sg.) · *arahataṁ* of the arahants (m. gen. pl.) · *ahosi* became
- And the venerable Subhadda became one **of the arahants**.

Pāli has no verb 'to have', and this is the gap the genitive fills. What English says with 'have', Pāli says with 'of me there is':

- **Puttā matthi dhanaṁ matthi**. (Dhp 62 — Dhp p. 9)
- *puttā* sons (m. nom. pl.) · *matthi* < *me* of me (1st gen./dat. sg.) + *atthi* there is · *dhanaṁ* wealth (nt. nom. sg.)
- '**I have sons, I have wealth**' —

Literally, "of me there are sons, of me there is wealth." Note that *me* here could be read as either genitive or dative and the sentence does not settle it; this is the merger described at the head of the chapter, met in a real sentence.[^cs-dhp62-division]

[^cs-dhp62-division]: The editions divide the line differently. The Pali Text Society prints *puttā m'atthi dhanaṁ m'atthi*, marking the elided *me* with an apostrophe; the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and the Buddha Jayanti join the whole of it, *dhanammatthi*. The words are the same either way and only the division moves. But the joined spelling buries the *me* the line is quoted here to show, and so the division above is the Pali Text Society's.

**The genitive with verbs.** A list of verbs governs the genitive where English would use an object or a preposition, and it has to be learnt as a list. The commonest are those of fearing, trembling, remembering, envying, believing, filling and thinking lightly of:[^cs-gen-verbs]

[^cs-gen-verbs]: Perniola §261, p. 330. His list is *hoti, pātubhavati, pūrati, dussati, tasati, bhāyati, pihayati, sarati, sumarati, jānāti, maccharāyati, saddahati* and two more. Three of them need a note. He prints *sunāti* with a dental *n* where the canon has *suṇāti* throughout: a slip, since the retroflex is exceptionless in nearly seven hundred occurrences. He prints *appamaññati* for the verb of the Dhammapada line he cites, and there is no such word: DPD gives *avamaññati*, *ava* + *mañña* + *ti*, √*man*, 'thinks lightly of', where his form would have to be built on *appa-* 'little'. And *pihayati* 'envies' is a citation form absent from the canon, which has the plural, *devāpi tesaṁ pihayanti* 'even the gods envy them' (Dhp 181 — Dhp p. 27), twenty-six times in the root texts against none for the singular. The first is an error, the second is an error, and the third is the ordinary hazard of citing a verb by its third person singular: three different matters, and only the second changes what a reader should believe.

- Sabbe tasanti **daṇḍassa**, sabbe bhāyanti **maccuno**. (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19)
- *sabbe* all (m. nom. pl.) · *tasanti* tremble · *daṇḍassa* at the rod (m. gen. sg.) · *bhāyanti* fear · *maccuno* death (m. gen. sg.)
- All tremble **at the rod**, all fear **death**.

The genitive of the agent with a past participle, where the instrumental would be equally correct and is commoner: *ñāto yasassī titthakaro sādhusammato bahujanassa*, "well known, renowned, a founder of a sect, held in high regard **by many people**" (DN 2 — D i 47).

### The vocative

The vocative addresses, and no more. The tradition excludes it from the *kāraka*s because at the moment of address there is no action yet to be connected to: the calling makes someone available to be told to go or to eat.

Two facts about its behaviour need stating, because both are close enough to exceptionless to be useful. A vocative is never the first word of a sentence in the canonical language, and usually stands second or third; and a vocative may be dropped without breaking the grammar, which is the practical mark of its standing outside the system.

- Kathañca, **mahārāja**, bhikkhu sīlasampanno hoti? (DN 2 — D i 63)
- *kathaṁ* how · *ca* and · *mahārāja* great king (m. voc. sg.) · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *sīlasampanno* accomplished in virtue (m. nom. sg.) · *hoti* is
- And how, **great king**, is a monk accomplished in virtue?

Perniola records the exceptions to the first rule (*bhikkhavo*, *bhadante*, *āvuso*, *ambho*, used to catch the attention of the person addressed and therefore free to lead) and gives the social range of the forms of address, which is a small ethnography of the canon: monks are addressed as *bhikkhu* or by name, lay disciples address monks as *bhante*, gods address them as *mārisa*, kings and queens as *mahārāja* and *devī*.[^cs-vocative]

[^cs-vocative]: Perniola §246, pp. 304–305. He also notes the construction in which a person of authority is addressed by an honorific vocative while a second word referring to the same person stands in the nominative as the subject of the verb: *desetu bhante Bhagavā dhammaṁ*, "may the Blessed One, sir, teach the Dhamma."

### Where case syntax is treated elsewhere

**The endings themselves** are in the chapters on nouns and on gender and adjectives, along with the ablative in *-to* and the collapse of the feminine *-āya* that this chapter has leaned on so heavily. Which case an indeclinable governs (*saddhiṁ* and *saha* the instrumental, *aññatra* and *vinā* the instrumental, *yāva* and *antarā* the accusative, *upari* and *heṭṭhā* the genitive) is in the chapter on indeclinables, together with the absolutives that have hardened into postpositions and take the accusative. Case forms frozen into adverbs are there too. **The absolute constructions** (locative, genitive, and the rarer accusative and nominative) are in the chapter on clause syntax, because they are dependent clauses; here belongs only the observation that they are named after their case because the case marks them off, and that the locative is by a wide margin the commonest of the four. And **the compound** is a case relation with the ending taken off: a *tappurisa* resolves into two words in two cases, so the chapter on compounds spends its longest section on identifying which case the first member would have worn.

### When the ending will not decide

Everything this chapter has set out is a set of tendencies, and it is important to say so before a reader treats it as a set of rules. Wijesekera, having spent three hundred pages classifying, ends his account of the genitive by warning against his own categories: divisions such as the genitive of possession, of material, of origin, subjective and objective "are made merely for the sake of convenience; they do not imply that absolute categories are possible."[^cs-conveniences] The classifications arrange what has been observed. The language does not follow them.

[^cs-conveniences]: Wijesekera §139.

So the practical question is what to do with an ending that could be several cases. Two steps, in this order.

**Look at the verb first.** Most of the time the verb settles it, because most verbs take one case and not another. *Nibbindati* takes a locative; *tasati* takes a genitive; *saṁvattati* takes a dative; *viramati* takes an ablative. When a form could be any of five cases, the verb it hangs on is generally the only witness that will testify.

**Then look for the parallel.** Pāli is a language of lists, and a form whose case is unclear is very often standing in a series with forms whose case is not. That is how *vedanāya* was settled two sections ago: it stood between *rūpasmiṁ* and *saṅkhāresu*.

Where neither helps, say that the sentence is open. The tradition does so when it has to. Its treatment of *evaṁ me sutaṁ*, the commonest sentence in the canon, is to list the three senses in which *me* is found and then decline to choose between two of them for this passage.[^cs-evam-me] To demand that Pāli be more determinate than its own commentators found it is to assert things the language does not say: the failure Wijesekera's introduction warned about.

[^cs-evam-me]: Treated in full in the chapter on pronouns, with the commentarial text.

One last example, and it is the reason the feminine *ā*-stems were called the hardest nouns in the language:

- **Taṇhāya** jāyatī soko, **taṇhāya** jāyatī bhayaṁ. (Dhp 216 — Dhp p. 32)
- *taṇhāya* from craving (f., an ending that may be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locative) · *jāyatī* is born · *soko* sorrow (m. nom. sg.) · *bhayaṁ* fear (nt. nom. sg.)
- **From craving** is sorrow born, **from craving** is fear born.

*Taṇhāya* could be five cases and is one. It is ablative, of cause, and we know it from *jāyati*, which takes an ablative of the source, not from the word, and from the four verses standing immediately before it in the same chapter of the Dhammapada, which build the identical sentence five times over:

| Dhp | The word | The ending |
|---|---|---|
| 212 | *piyato* | *-to*, ablative and nothing else |
| 213 | *pemato* | *-to*, ablative and nothing else |
| 214 | *ratiyā* | *-āya*/*-iyā*, any of five cases |
| 215 | *kāmato* | *-to*, ablative and nothing else |
| 216 | *taṇhāya* | *-āya*, any of five cases |

Three of the five carry an ending that can only be ablative, and two carry an ending that could be almost anything. The two ambiguous ones sit inside a sequence built by the three unambiguous ones, and so are read as those are. That is how a case is decided in practice: a word placed against everything around it, not a rule applied to a word.
