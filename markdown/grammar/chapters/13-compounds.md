## Compounds

After the last twelve chapters a reader can decline a noun, conjugate a verb and resolve a sandhi, and will still be stopped on the first page of almost any sutta by a word twenty letters long that is in no dictionary. Compounding is the single largest obstacle between a competent beginner and an ordinary page of canon, and it is an obstacle of a peculiar kind: nothing about it is difficult, and there is a great deal of it.

The mechanism is the one English uses in *blackbird* and *grasshopper*: two words made into one, with the meaning of the whole not always the sum of the parts.[^cpd-warder-english] Pāli does it constantly, at any length, and with no space, hyphen or capital to mark the join. So the work is always the same three questions, in order. Where does the compound divide? What is the relation between the parts? And what does the whole word do in the sentence it stands in?

[^cpd-warder-english]: The English parallels are Warder's, p. 77, and they are the best teaching device any source offers: *grasshopper*, *fourteen*, *blackbird*, *whitewashed*, *alongside* and *twelvemonth* correspond one for one to the six Pāli classes, which he then teaches across Lessons 13, 15, 16, 19, 25 and 26 in that order. The order is itself a claim about how often a reader meets each, and it is right.

This chapter answers them in that order, because that is the order in which a reader actually needs them, and not in the order in which the grammars present the material.

### One word, one inflection

A **compound**, *samāsa*, 'putting together', is two or more words joined so that the result behaves as a single word. The tradition names two properties, and between them they cover almost everything a reader needs to know about the outside of a compound: ***ekapadatta***, 'the fact of being one word', and ***ekavibhattitā***, 'the fact of having one inflection'.[^cpd-ekapadatta]

[^cpd-ekapadatta]: Collins p. 129, translating *Saddanīti* ch. 23. He sets *samāsapada*, 'words in a compound', against *vyāsapada*, 'words in separation', and *vākyapada*, 'words in a sentence': a three-way contrast that puts compounding in its place as one of the language's synthetic processes, not a curiosity of vocabulary.

The second is the practically useful one. Only the last member takes an ending; everything before it stands in its bare stem form. So *dukkhakkhandha* 'mass of suffering' declines as *dukkhakkhandho*, *dukkhakkhandhaṁ*, *dukkhakkhandhassa*, and never as *dukkhassakhandho*. The gender of the whole is normally the gender of the last member: *mahājanakāyo* is masculine because *kāya* is, *buddhasāsanaṁ* neuter because *sāsana* is.

Two consequences follow that beginners regularly get wrong.

The uninflected members carry no number. *Buddhasāsana* is 'the teaching of the Buddha' and could as easily be 'of the Buddhas'; nothing in the form decides it. Only context does.

The uninflected members carry no case either, and since most compounds encode a case relation, this is the reader's central difficulty, not a footnote to it. *Dukkhakkhandha* means 'mass *of* suffering', but the genitive that says so is not written anywhere. It has to be recovered.

- jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)
- *jātipaccayā* with birth as condition · *jarāmaraṇaṁ* old age and death · *sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā* < *soka* sorrow · *parideva* lamentation · *dukkha* pain · *domanassa* dejection · *upāyāsa* distress · *sambhavanti* come to be
- With birth as condition, old age and death come to be, and sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and distress.

Four words in Pāli, thirteen in English, and the last of them is a five-member compound written solid. It is also perfectly regular: only *-upāyāsā* is inflected, and its masculine plural ending governs the whole.

One consequence of *ekavibhattitā* deserves stating outright, because it disorients anyone checking their work against a text. The members of a compound are stems, and a stem is not a word. Of the five members of *sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā*, four are effectively unfindable in the root texts in the form the compound prints them: the compound contains *soka*, *parideva*, *domanassa* and *upāyāsa*, and a page of canon has *soko*, *paridevo*, *domanassaṁ*, *upāyāso*. Only *dukkha* has an independent life, because it is also an adjective. The same holds of *suriya* inside *candimasūriyā*, which occurs nowhere in the canon as *suriya* and constantly as *suriyo*. Searching a text for a member as a compound spells it will usually find nothing, and that is correct behaviour for a compound.

### Permanent and impermanent compounds

Before the classes, one distinction that no Western grammar makes and that matters more to a reader with a dictionary than any of them. Aggavaṁsa divides compounds by whether they are permanent or made on the spot.[^cpd-nicca]

[^cpd-nicca]: Collins pp. 130–131, translating *Saddanīti* ch. 23: *sabhāvato niccasamāso aniccasamāso ti duvidho*, "a compound is by nature either permanent or impermanent". His two classes of permanent compound are those ending in an *upapada* or accessory word, and those beginning with an absolutive.

A ***nicca samāsa***, a 'permanent compound', is one whose members do not occur apart in that sense: the word exists only compounded. *Kammakāro* 'one who does the work' is a permanent compound, and so is *paṭiccasamuppādo* 'dependent origination', whose first member is an absolutive that cannot stand as the subject of anything. So are proper names built the same way: *Arindamo*, 'he who tames his enemies'.

An ***anicca samāsa***, an 'impermanent compound', is assembled for the occasion and could always have been said as separate words. These are the vast majority.

The practical value is this. A permanent compound has a dictionary entry; an impermanent one does not, and looking for one wastes time. When a long word fails to resolve in the dictionary, ask whether the word was ever meant to be there before suspecting the dictionary.

### The seam

Splitting comes first, because no other step can be taken until it is done. Three changes happen at the join, and all three are regular.

#### What happens to the first member

The first member appears in its stem form, and for the vowel stems that is the end of the matter: *assadamako* 'horse-trainer' from *assa*, *bhikkhusaṅgho* 'the assembly of monks' from *bhikkhu*. A reader gets stuck on the consonant stems and a few special cases, and Perniola's account of them is the fullest anywhere.[^cpd-perniola-first]

[^cpd-perniola-first]: Perniola §124(a)–(l), pp. 157–159. Warder gives the two commonest rules in a single footnote to p. 77: "Words in *-ant* have the weak stem *-at*, those in *-an* drop the *-n*." Geiger does not cover this ground at all. His §33, "At the end of the first member of a compound", treats the *phonology* of the join (a short vowel lengthened, *sakhībhāva*; a long one shortened, *dāsigaṇa*, from *dāsī* + *gaṇa*; the second member's initial consonant doubled instead) and says nothing about which stem a word presents there. His contents page carries "vowels in compounds" as its only entry on compounding outside the infinitives in *-kāma* at §207, which is the same silence Oberlies keeps and for the same reason: the tradition had classified compounds once and completely, so a historical grammar had nothing left to add.

| Stem type | In a compound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *-in* | drops the nasal, *-i* | *hatthin* → *hatthi-* in *hatthigavāssavaḷavaṁ* 'elephants, cattle, horses and mares' |
| *-an* | drops the nasal | *rājan* → *rāja-* in *rājaputto* 'a king's son' |
| *-ant* | weak stem *-at*, or *-ā* | *mahant* → *mahā-* in *mahāmuni* 'great sage'; *sant* → *sa(d)-* in *saddhammo* 'the true teaching' |
| *-ar* | usually *-u* | *pitar* → *pitu-* in *piturakkhitā* 'protected by the father'; *mātar* → *mātu-* in *mātughātako* 'matricide' |
| *-as* | usually *-o*; sometimes passes to another stem | *rajas* → *rajo-* in *rajopatho* 'a dusty road'; but *vacas* → *vacī-* in *vacīkammaṁ* 'verbal action' |
| long final vowel | often shortened | *mettā* → *metta-* in *mettacitto* 'with a mind of loving-kindness' |
| *go* | *gav-* before a vowel | *go* + *assa* → *gavassaṁ* 'cattle and horses' |

Two of these deserve a second look.

The kinship terms desert the *-ar* rule inside a *dvanda*. *Pitar* becomes *pitu-* in *piturakkhitā* 'protected by the father', as the rule says; but 'mother and father' is *mātāpitaro* and 'father and children' is *pitāputtā*, with the long nominative kept instead.[^cpd-matapitaro] It is a small irregularity, and it sits in two of the commonest words in the canon.

[^cpd-matapitaro]: Perniola §124(g), p. 158, states the *-u* rule and illustrates it with *piturakkhita* and *mātughātaka*; the *dvanda* exception is in the unnumbered paragraph, not the subsection, that closes the page beneath it, where he gives *mātāpitaro* and *pitāputtā*. A second such paragraph, at the head of p. 159, adds the alternative stems *matti-* and *petti-*; his illustration *mattisambhavo* 'born from a mother' occurs only in the commentaries, so the alternative stems are recorded here without an example.

Before a form of *bhū* or *kar*, a stem in *-a* very often becomes *-ī-*. This is a productive pattern, not an exception to be memorised, and so a reader meets many words in *-ībhūta* and *-īkata*: *sammukha* + *bhāva* gives *sammukhībhāvo* 'the fact of being face to face'; *bahula* + *kata* gives *bahulīkato* 'made much of'; *sīta* + *bhūta* gives *sītibhūto* 'become cool'.[^cpd-ibhuta] Both of the first two are common canonical words, *bahulīkata* in several hundred places in the root texts. Meeting an unfamiliar word in *-īkata*, split it there and look up what precedes.

[^cpd-ibhuta]: Perniola §124(a), p. 158, where the rule is stated for "some stems in *-a*". The texts suggest it is stronger than that: the *-ī-* forms are ordinary and the uncompounded alternatives largely are not.

#### What happens at the join

Ordinary sandhi applies at a compound seam, and Chapter 2's patterns are the patterns: *suñña* + *agāra* gives *suññāgāraṁ* 'an empty dwelling', *mahā* + *ogha* gives *mahogho* 'the great flood', *anu* + *addhamāsaṁ* gives *anvaddhamāsaṁ* 'every fortnight'.

What is peculiar to the compound seam is that the ordinary assimilation rules are *suspended* there, and Oberlies alone says why. The rules that would flatten a consonant cluster inside a word "are partly annulled at the seam of compounds in order not to obscure the initial sound of the posterior member".[^cpd-oberlies-seam] That is a functional explanation, and it is a statement about the reader's own problem: the language protects the beginning of the second member so that it stays findable. *Duccaritaṁ* keeps its *c*, *duttaro* its *t*, *uggacchati* its *g*. Had the rules applied without restraint, none of those words could be looked up.

[^cpd-oberlies-seam]: Oberlies §20, p. 111, in full: "The rules of §16–18 are partly annulled at the seam of compounds in order not to obscure the initial sound of the posterior member, especially if a verb(al derivative)". His examples include *ussāha-* 'effort' (Sanskrit *utsāha-*), *uyyāna-* 'park' (*udyāna-*) and *duccarita-* 'misbehaviour' (*duścarita-*).

The other thing that happens is **doubling**. An initial consonant of the second member is frequently doubled after a short vowel, so *dukkha* + *khandha* is *dukkhakkhandho* and *mahā* + *phala* is *mahapphalaṁ*, with the *ā* shortened, since a long vowel and a following double consonant cannot both stand.[^cpd-doubling] A great many compounds are only mysterious until this is recognised; *āsavakkhayo* is *āsava* + *khaya*, *sappañño* is *sa* + *pañña*, *bahussuto* is *bahu* + *suta*.

[^cpd-doubling]: Oberlies §14.16, "Doubling of a consonant at the seam of compounds", pp. 91–92, and §24, p. 122, where he adds that the gemination "is, however, often analogical". Perniola §126, p. 160, approaches the same forms from the other side, as Sanskrit consonant sandhi surviving in compounds derived from or modelled on Sanskrit: *bahussuto* has its double *-ss-* from Sanskrit *śr-*.

#### Rhythm

One further pressure shapes compounds and is invisible unless someone points at it. A sequence of three short syllables is avoided, in compounds as elsewhere, and the language will lengthen a vowel or insert a consonant to break one up.[^cpd-rhythm] That explains the odd-looking *-m-* in *aṅgamaṅgāni* 'limb after limb': *aṅga* + *aṅgāni* would give three shorts in a row, and the inserted *-m-* prevents it. Perniola prints the word in his list of repeated compounds and does not say where the *-m-* came from; it came from there.

[^cpd-rhythm]: Oberlies §24 *rem.*, p. 123: "Also in compounds the sequence of three short syllables is avoided", with *agha-m-miga-* 'wild dangerous beast' among his examples. Thānuttamo p. 128 reports the same pressure under the name Anālayo gives it (2009, p. 741), the waxing-syllable principle, as one factor in the order of members in a *dvanda*.

The same pressure explains something that would otherwise look like free variation: why *dhammavinayo* has its members in that order and not the other. A two-syllable word before a three-syllable one is the natural shape, and Perniola observes independently that the shorter word tends to come first in a *dvanda*.[^cpd-order] Where the order of a compound's members looks arbitrary, rhythm is the first thing to try.

[^cpd-order]: Perniola §128(g), p. 163, whose own illustration of the rule is *dhammavinayo* itself: "as first member of the *samāsa* we more easily find the word with the smaller number of syllables, or the word which begins with a vowel and ends in *-a*". He adds one real semantic constraint: where the two members name events that follow one another in time, the order is the natural one: *jātijaraṁ*, *jātimaraṇaṁ*.

### Where a compound ends and two words begin

Not always where the edition puts the space. *Ekamantaṁ* 'to one side' looks like a compound and is not one: it is the sandhi of two separate words, *ekaṁ antaṁ*, which the editions happen to print joined. It is an instance of something general.

Oberlies puts it in one sentence: "Words which stand in close syntactical juncture are sometimes treated like members of a compound."[^cpd-juncture] His examples are *na-ccaje*, *na-ppajahanti*, *tatra-ssu*: the negative particle and its verb, doubling the verb's initial as a compound join would. Compounding and habitual sandhi are two points on one scale, not two separate operations, and where a text falls on that scale is often an editor's decision, not the language's.

[^cpd-juncture]: Oberlies §24, p. 122. He adds that such cases "served as pattern for other units", citing *muni-ppakāsayi* at Sn 251.

> **Word division is editorial**
>
> Because the scale is continuous, editions divide differently, and a form that cannot be found in one edition may be ordinary in another. Chapter 2 makes the same point about *āyusā*, which is invisible in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana because the line is printed joined.
>
> The practical rule for a reader is to treat a failed lookup as a question about word division before treating it as a question about the word. Try the string as part of what precedes it; try it as two words; and where the question is really about spelling and not the language, check a second edition instead of the same one again, because a single edition shows only what one editor chose.

### The six classes

The tradition counts eight kinds of compound and then reduces them to two.[^cpd-eight] Learn the reduction, because it is a real claim about the language, not a filing system: a compound is either *copulative*, in which the members are equals joined by 'and', or *determinative*, in which the first member qualifies the second. Everything else is subdivision.

[^cpd-eight]: Collins p. 131, translating *Saddanīti*. His eight are *dvanda*, *digu*, *tappurisa*, *upasagganipātapubbaka* (prepositional), *kammadhāraya*, *bahubbīhi*, *avyayībhāva*, and syntactic compounds, the last of which he marks as a category from Western scholarship, not from the tradition. His reduction: the descriptive includes the numerical, the dependent includes the prepositional, and both kinds can have adverbial and possessive uses.

The six classes below are the working set: Perniola's and Bodhi's, and the ones a reader will see named in a commentary or a translator's note.

*Dvanda*, *tappurisa*, *kammadhāraya*, *digu*, *avyayībhāva*, *bahubbīhi*: these are technical vocabulary of the grammatical tradition and not words of canonical Pāli. In the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, *samāsa* occurs fourteen times, *bahubbīhi* forty-three, *digu* twenty-two, and *tappurisa*, *kammadhāraya* and *dvanda* once or twice each; every one of those occurrences is in the grammatical and later literature, and none is in a sutta. *Avyayībhāva* does not occur at all, and the spelling *abyayībhāva* once.

Metalanguage behaves so, and it is said here because a reader trained to check forms against the canon deserves to know why these particular words are exempt. They are the tradition's tools for describing the language, not part of it. This book keeps the Pāli names instead of translating them, on Bodhi's reasoning: "copulative", "descriptive", "determinative" and "possessive" are "either inadequate or misleading (or both)", and four short Pāli words serve a reader better than four long English approximations.[^cpd-bodhi-names]

[^cpd-bodhi-names]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses in Pāli*, "General Introduction", p. 15, where the quoted words stand; "Compounds", p. 28, restates the choice. Collins §6.2, p. 131, makes the same point from the other end: *tatpuruṣa*, *karmadhāraya* and *bahuvrīhi* are now in the *Oxford English Dictionary* as English words, which settles the question of whether they can be used in English prose.

#### *Dvanda* — the members joined by 'and'

A ***dvanda*** ('pair') is a compound whose members would, if written separately, have been linked by *ca* 'and', or, less often, by *vā* 'or'.

- Yāvatā, ānanda, candimasūriyā pariharanti, disā bhanti virocanā, tāva sahassadhā loko. (AN 3.80 — A i 227)
- *yāvatā* as far as · *candimasūriyā* < *candimā* moon · *suriya* sun · *pariharanti* revolve · *disā* the directions · *bhanti* shine · *virocanā* radiating · *tāva* so far · *sahassadhā* thousandfold · *loko* the world
- As far as the moon and sun revolve and light up the directions with their radiance, so far extends the thousandfold world-system.

*Candimasūriyā* is nominative plural and the two nouns inside it are singular: one moon, one sun. The number of a *dvanda* says nothing about the number of its members, the first of the two points this class demands.

The second is that a *dvanda* may be plural or singular, and the choice is meaningful. The tradition names the two kinds, and Bodhi keeps the names: an ***itaritara*** *dvanda* is declined as a plural in the gender of the last member, and a ***samāhāra*** or 'collective' *dvanda* is declined as a **neuter singular**, treating the members as one thing.[^cpd-itaritara] *Candimasūriyā* above is the first kind. *Muttakarīsaṁ* 'urine and faeces', *abhijjhādomanassaṁ* 'longing and dejection', *thinamiddhaṁ* 'dullness and drowsiness', *uddhaccakukkuccaṁ* 'restlessness and remorse' are the second, and three of those are items in standard lists, as one would expect, because a collective singular suits a list-item.

[^cpd-itaritara]: Bodhi, "Compounds", p. 28, who names both. Perniola §128, pp. 162–163, reaches the same distinction by a different route, as four observations about when the plural is used (multiplicity, not collectivity, takes it, *satta rattindivāni*; nouns for human and divine beings take it almost always, *devamanussā*; animals go either way; and most of the rest are singular), inside a section that runs to seven subsections and closes on gender, agreement and word order. The paragraph above them carries something neither the tradition's names nor Bodhi's account does: the number of the compound says nothing about the number of each member, so *samaṇabrāhmaṇā* may mean one of each, one of either and many of the other, or many of both, and only the context decides. Collins p. 131 adds that a collective *dvanda* is occasionally declined in the gender of its last member instead of as a neuter; *chandarāgo* 'desire and lust' is his instance, and it is a common one.

Repetition is a *dvanda* too, and a productive one: *punappunaṁ* 'again and again', *aṅgamaṅgāni* 'limb after limb', *samasama* 'quite equal'.

#### *Tappurisa* — the first member in a case relation

A ***tappurisa*** ('his man', itself an example: 'the man *of* him') is a compound in which the first member stands to the second in a case relation, any case except the nominative and the vocative. The classification is by that recovered case, and the names are the ordinals: *dutiyā-tappurisa* for an accusative relation, *tatiyā-* for an instrumental, and so on.[^cpd-tappurisa-names]

[^cpd-tappurisa-names]: Thānuttamo pp. 128–129, with Kacc 327; Duroiselle pp. 249–251. The English names (accusative *tappurisa*, instrumental *tappurisa*) are Bodhi's ("Compounds", p. 30) and are used here, because a reader does not need the Pāli ordinal to hold the idea.

| Relation | Compound | Resolved |
|---|---|---|
| accusative | *lokavidū* 'knower of the world' | *lokaṁ vidū* |
| instrumental | *paññāvimutti* 'liberation by wisdom' | *paññāya vimutti* |
| ablative | *yogakkhemaṁ* 'security from bondage' | *yogā khemaṁ* |
| genitive | *bhikkhusaṅgho* 'the assembly of monks' | *bhikkhūnaṁ saṅgho* |
| locative | *kāmataṇhā* 'craving for sensual pleasures' | *kāmesu taṇhā* |

The genitive relation is much the commonest, so an unfamiliar *tappurisa* should be tried as a genitive first. *Dukkhasamudayo*, *dukkhanirodho*, *rāgakkhayo*, *dosakkhayo*, *mohakkhayo*: the origin of suffering, the cessation of suffering, the destruction of lust, of hatred, of delusion.

The locative deserves its own note, because English translation conceals it. *Kāmataṇhā*, *bhavataṇhā*, *vibhavataṇhā* are rendered 'craving for sensual pleasures, for existence, for non-existence', which sounds like a dative; but Pāli words for desire take their object in the locative, so the relation inside the compound is locative and not dative.[^cpd-locative-tappurisa] The same holds for *sakkāyadiṭṭhi*: words for views also take the locative.

[^cpd-locative-tappurisa]: Bodhi, "Compounds", p. 31. He is candid about the one that will not settle: *bojjhaṅga* 'factor of awakening' looks like a dative *tappurisa*, "a factor *for* awakening", which the suttas' own explanation seems to support, while the commentaries treat it as a genitive, the factors that constitute the awakening event. He records both and decides neither, and this book follows him.

- Idaṁ kho pana, bhikkhave, dukkhasamudayaṁ ariyasaccaṁ— (SN 56.11 — S v 421)
- *idaṁ* this · *kho pana* now · *bhikkhave* monks · *dukkhasamudayaṁ* < *dukkha* suffering + *samudaya* origin, a genitive *tappurisa* · *ariyasaccaṁ* < *ariya* noble + *sacca* truth, a *kammadhāraya*
- Now this, monks, is the noble truth of the origin of suffering—

Two compounds in four words, of two different classes, and they are nested one inside the other: *dukkhasamudayaṁ* qualifies *ariyasaccaṁ*, a compound serving as an adjective, the *bahubbīhi* use taken up below.

#### The compound that keeps its ending

The rule that only the last member is inflected has a named exception, and the exception is not rare. In an ***alutta*** compound (from *alutta*, 'not elided') the first member keeps the case ending it would have had as a separate word.[^cpd-alutta]

[^cpd-alutta]: Duroiselle p. 251, whose heading is *Alutta Tappurisa*; Perniola §134(b), p. 167, tabulates them by case. Collins p. 130 derives the distinction from *Saddanīti*'s own pair: *saddasamāsana*, "compounding of words", which happens with elision of the endings, against *atthasamāsana*, "compounding of meanings", which happens without it.

| Case kept | Compound | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| accusative | *kālaṅkato* | 'time-done', i.e. dead |
| accusative | *antakaro* | 'one who makes an end' |
| accusative | *sabbañjaho* | 'one who has given up everything' |
| genitive | *gavampati* | 'lord of cattle' |
| genitive | *disampati* | 'lord of the regions', i.e. a king |
| locative | *manasikāro* | 'work in the mind', i.e. attention |
| locative | *antevāsī* | 'one dwelling within', i.e. a resident pupil |
| locative | *pubbenivāso* | 'former abode', i.e. a past life |

Three of these are among the commonest words in the canon: *manasikāro* in a hundred and thirty-seven places in the root texts, *pubbenivāsaṁ* in two hundred and forty-seven, *kālaṅkato* in two hundred. The practical lesson is narrow and useful: a case ending in the middle of a long word is not a mistake and not a word break. *Manasi* is a locative singular and it is inside a compound.

The grammarians' own two technical terms are of this shape, which is presumably why they noticed the class: *parassapadaṁ* 'word for another' and *attanopadaṁ* 'word for oneself', the active and middle voices, both with their datives intact.[^cpd-pada]

[^cpd-pada]: Collins p. 130, who calls them "two egregious examples of compounds without the elimination of endings", and contrasts them with the ordinary *attahita*, *parahita* 'benefit for oneself, for another', where the endings do go.

#### *Kammadhāraya* — the first member describes the second

A ***kammadhāraya*** is a determinative compound whose two members would, written separately, have stood in **the same case**, because the first is an attribute of the second, not in a case relation to it. *Mahāpuriso* is 'a great man', not 'a man of greatness'; *ariyasaccaṁ* is 'a noble truth'.

The first member may be an adjective (*mahāpuriso*), a noun in apposition (*sālarukkhamūle* 'at the foot of the sal tree'), a past participle (*sammāsambuddho*), or an adverb (*sammādiṭṭhi* 'right view', *musāvādo* 'false speech', *punabbhavo* 'renewed existence').[^cpd-kammadharaya-members] Every factor of the noble eightfold path is an adverb-plus-noun *kammadhāraya*, which is a fair measure of how much doctrine sits inside this one class.

[^cpd-kammadharaya-members]: Perniola §§129–132; Bodhi, "Compounds", p. 30, who supplies the observation about the eightfold path. Perniola, pp. 163–165, divides the class twice over: first by part of speech, which is the division above, and then a second time "from the syntactical point of view": a *kammadhāraya* is a noun compound if its second member is a noun, an adjective compound if its second member is an adjective, and an adverb compound if the whole is used adverbially. The second is the more useful of the two for reading, because it depends on what the compound *does* in a sentence, not on what it is made of.

Two subtypes need naming, because both are common in doctrinal contexts and neither is recoverable from the general rule.

**The *eva* type.** Where the two members are in apposition and the first *is* the second, the tradition supplies an unwritten *eva* 'just, precisely'. *Sīladhanaṁ* is *sīlaṁ eva dhanaṁ*, 'the wealth which is virtue', not 'the wealth of virtue' as a possession.[^cpd-eva-type] The same analysis makes the factors of dependent origination intelligible: *avijjāpaccayā* is 'with the condition that is ignorance', and *satisambojjhaṅgo* is 'the awakening factor that is mindfulness'. Rendering these as genitives ('the condition of ignorance', 'the awakening factor of mindfulness') is not quite wrong and not quite what the compound says.

[^cpd-eva-type]: Thānuttamo p. 130, giving the traditional name *avadhāraṇapubbapadakammadhārayo* and Kacc 324 behind it. Bodhi, "Compounds", p. 30, reaches the same analysis independently for the dependent-origination terms and for *rāgaggi*, *dosaggi*, *mohaggi*: "grammatically the compounds should be resolved as 'the fire that is lust' and so forth".

**The *iti* type.** Where the first member names what the second is *about*, the tradition supplies an unwritten *iti*. The traditional example resolves *khattiyamāno* as *khattiyo iti māno*, 'the conceit "I am a warrior"', not 'the conceit of a warrior'.[^cpd-iti-type] Once seen, this construction is hard to unsee in the compounds built on *asmimāno* 'the conceit "I am"', where the *iti* is understood in the same way and, unusually, the Pāli writes the thing conceived instead of describing it.

[^cpd-iti-type]: Thānuttamo p. 130, *sambhāvanapubbapadakammadhārayo*, where the understood words may be *iti*, *saṅkhāto* 'reckoned as' or *hutvā* 'having been'. His own specimen *khattiyamāno* occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; the type is real and the illustration is the grammarians' own.

A comparison is a *kammadhāraya* too, and the thing compared comes first: *purisājañño* 'a thoroughbred of a man', *cakkaratanaṁ* 'the wheel-treasure', *akkhitārakā* 'star-like eyes'.

#### *Digu* — a numeral in front

A ***digu*** ('two cows') is a *kammadhāraya* whose first member is a numeral. It is a subclass and not a seventh thing, and the only reason to give it a name is that its number behaves in two ways.[^cpd-digu]

[^cpd-digu]: Collins p. 133, translating *Saddanīti*: *samāhāradigu tāva: cattāri saccāni — catusaccaṁ; asamāhāradigu, dasasahassāni cakkavāḷāni — dasasahassacakkavāḷāni*. The second of his two examples is Aggavaṁsa's own and is commentarial, not canonical; the canon's equivalent is *dasasahassilokadhātu*.

A ***samāhāra digu*** collects the things counted into one concept and takes the **neuter singular**: *saḷāyatanaṁ*, 'the sixfold sense base', not 'six sense bases'. An ***asamāhāra digu*** counts them and takes the plural in the gender of the last member: *pañcakkhandhā* 'the five aggregates', *catuddisā* 'the four quarters'.

- nāmarūpapaccayā saḷāyatanaṁ; (SN 12.2 — S ii 2)
- *nāmarūpapaccayā* < *nāma* name + *rūpa* form — a *dvanda* — + *paccaya* condition, an ablative of cause · *saḷāyatanaṁ* < *saḷ* six + *āyatana* sense base, a collective *digu*
- With name-and-form as condition, the sixfold sense base.

Both compounds in that half-line are collective singulars, and the second shows the numeral *cha* 'six' in one of its several compounding forms, *saḷ-*.

#### *Avyayībhāva* — an indeclinable in front

An ***avyayībhāva*** ('becoming indeclinable') has an indeclinable as its first and governing member, and the whole compound is an adverb. It normally has the form of a neuter accusative singular in *-aṁ*, and it does not decline.[^cpd-avyayibhava]

[^cpd-avyayibhava]: Collins p. 134, dividing them by whether the first member is an *upasagga* (verbal prefix) or a *nipāta* (particle), and giving a resolution for each: *upanagaraṁ* = *nagarassa samīpaṁ* 'near the town', *yathāvuḍḍhaṁ* = *vuḍḍhānaṁ paṭipāṭi* 'in order of seniority', *tirokuṭṭaṁ* = *kuṭṭānaṁ tiro* 'outside the walls', *purebhattaṁ* = *bhattassa pure* 'before the meal'. Three of those four are ordinary canonical words; *upanagaraṁ* occurs only in the grammatical literature and is the tradition's specimen, not a word of the canon. Perniola treats the same class at §141 under the name "governing compounds" and adds which case each first member governs.

- Tirokuṭṭesu tiṭṭhanti, (Kp 7)
- *tirokuṭṭesu* < *tiro* across, beyond + *kuṭṭa* wall · *tiṭṭhanti* they stand
- Outside the walls they stand,

Others a reader meets constantly: *yathābhūtaṁ* 'in accordance with what has come to be', *pacchābhattaṁ* 'after the meal', *anuvassaṁ* 'yearly', *paṭisotaṁ* 'against the stream', *bahiddhā* 'outside'. *Yathābhūtaṁ* occurs nearly two thousand times in the root texts, and its meaning is not obvious from its parts: *yathā* 'as' plus *bhūta* 'become', hence 'as it has actually come to be', and it is an adverb, so it modifies the verb of knowing and not the thing known.

#### *Bahubbīhi* — not a seventh class but a use

The last of the six needs stating carefully, because it makes the class intelligible. It is also where the third of this chapter's opening questions gets its answer: the other five classes describe what goes on inside a compound, and this one describes what a compound does outside itself.

A *bahubbīhi* is any compound of any of the preceding classes used as an adjective describing something outside itself, and no separate kind of compound.[^cpd-bahubbihi-use] The word *bahubbīhi* is its own example: *bahu* + *vīhi*, 'much rice', is an ordinary *kammadhāraya* meaning a quantity of grain, but used of a field it means 'having much rice', and the rice is no longer what the word is about. Because such compounds always point outwards, they are also called **exocentric**.

[^cpd-bahubbihi-use]: Three sources say it outright and this is not a minority reading. Collins p. 135: "Possessives (*bahubbīhi*) are not a different kind of compound. They are determinative compounds ending in a noun, which are used and declined as adjectives." Bodhi, "Compounds", p. 31: "The *bahubbīhi* is not a different kind of compound but a compound of any of the preceding types used as an adjective to describe a noun external to itself." Thānuttamo p. 132: "the whole range of compounds discussed above … become attributive compounds when they are used as adjectives." Duroiselle pp. 263–264 demonstrates it by running each class in turn under the heading "used relatively".

The canon demonstrates the point directly, by using one compound both ways in a single passage. The Vinaya's case law on theft runs four consecutive cases in a fixed frame and changes a word at a time; Chapter 8 takes them for their aorists. What moves between the first case and the second is one compound of *theyya* 'theft' and *citta* 'mind':

- … mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā **theyyacittaṁ** uppādesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — "… saw a costly cloth and produced **a thought of theft**."
- … mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā **theyyacitto** āmasi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — "… saw a costly cloth and, **with theft in mind**, touched it."

*Citta* is neuter, and in the first sentence the compound keeps that gender: *theyyacittaṁ* is a neuter accusative, the object of *uppādesi*, and it names a thought. In the second the same two members stand masculine and nominative, agreeing with *bhikkhu*, and they name no thought at all: they describe the monk who has one. Inside the compound nothing changed. Only the ending did.

DPD records the pair as two entries under one construction: *theyyacitta* the neuter noun, 'intention to steal', and *theyyacitta* the adjective, 'intending to steal, with theft in mind', both of them *theyya* + *citta*.[^cpd-theyyacitta] The two uses are two different words to a dictionary, so the same compound is entered twice: this section's point, reached from the other side.

[^cpd-theyyacitta]: DPD, s.v. *theyyacitta*, gives two entries under the same construction *theyya* + *citta*: the neuter noun 'intention to steal; thieving mind', and the adjective 'intending to steal; with theft in mind', literally 'with thieving mind'. *Theyyacitto* has 87 root-text occurrences and *theyyacittaṁ* 2. The masculine can only be the adjectival use, and both occurrences of the neuter accusative are in this Vinītavatthu.

The rule's own word-analysis then explains one such compound with two more of the same shape:

- Theyyasaṅkhātanti **theyyacitto avaharaṇacitto**. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)
- *theyyasaṅkhātaṁ* reckoned as theft (n. acc. sg.) · *ti* (quotation) · *theyyacitto* having theft in mind (m. nom. sg.) · *avaharaṇacitto* having carrying-off in mind (m. nom. sg.)
- "'**Reckoned as theft**' means: **having theft in mind, having carrying-off in mind**."

The gloss does not take the compound apart. It substitutes two compounds built the same way, and both of them are masculine where the word being glossed is neuter, so the analysis has moved from the act to the person who intends it.

Three consequences follow, and all three are practical.

It agrees, so it declines like an adjective. A feminine noun at the end of a *bahubbīhi* qualifying a masculine becomes masculine, and a neuter one likewise: *cittaṁ* is neuter, but a monk with a well-developed mind is *subhāvitacitto*. Almost all of them follow the *-a* declension whatever their last member was.

**It can stand alone.** The noun it describes is often not written, and the compound does duty for it: *khīṇāsavo* 'one whose corruptions are exhausted' needs no *bhikkhu* beside it.

It is usually best rendered with a relative clause. 'A monk who has…', 'the country in which…', 'the tree up which…'. Duroiselle classifies *bahubbīhis* by which case relation the compound bears to the word it qualifies, and although his specimen forms are Sanskrit grammarians' constructions absent from the canon, the scheme is sound.[^cpd-bahubbihi-cases]

[^cpd-bahubbihi-cases]: Duroiselle pp. 260–263, seven kinds, from *paṭhamā-bahubbīhi* (nominative) to *sattamī-bahubbīhi* (locative), each with a resolution formula. Not one of his five illustrative compounds (*chinnahattho*, *āgatasamaṇo*, *ārūḷhanaro*, *niggatajano*, *sampannasasso*) occurs anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, which is recorded here, not passed over; they are the standing examples of the Sanskrit grammatical tradition, reproduced without a mark to say so. The canonical illustrations used above and below were found for this chapter.

- Santi sattā apparajakkhajātikā, assavanatā dhammassa parihāyanti. (SN 6.1 — S i 137)
- *santi* there are · *sattā* beings · *apparajakkhajātikā* < *appa* little + *rajas* dust + *akkha* eye + *jātika* of the kind, hence 'of the kind that has little dust in the eye' · *assavanatā* through not hearing · *dhammassa* the teaching · *parihāyanti* they fall away
- There are beings with little dust in their eyes who are falling away through not hearing the teaching.

*Apparajakkhajātikā* is four members deep and is a *bahubbīhi* by use: nothing in it names a being, and it describes *sattā*. It also shows the *-as* stem rule of the first-member table above, working invisibly in the middle of a real word: *rajas* has shed its *-s*.[^cpd-rajakkha]

[^cpd-rajakkha]: DPD, s.v. *apparajakkhajātika*, whose construction field gives *appa + rajas + akkha + jātika*. Perniola §139(c), p. 172, prints it in the same shape, *apparajakkhā*, and the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *apparajakkha-* seventeen times in the root texts.

A whole family of *bahubbīhis* is built on the interrogative pronoun, and they are easy to miss because they look like nouns:

- Saḷāyatanañcidaṁ, bhikkhave, kiṁnidānaṁ kiṁsamudayaṁ kiṁjātikaṁ kiṁpabhavaṁ? (MN 11 — M i 67)
- *saḷāyatanañcidaṁ* < *saḷāyatanaṁ* the sixfold sense base + *ca* and + *idaṁ* this · *kiṁnidānaṁ* having what as its source · *kiṁsamudayaṁ* having what as its origin · *kiṁjātikaṁ* having what as its birth · *kiṁpabhavaṁ* having what as its production
- And this sixfold sense base, monks — what is its source, what its origin, what its birth, what its production?

Four *bahubbīhis* in a row, each agreeing with *saḷāyatanaṁ*, and each meaning 'having what as its X', not 'what X'.

##### When the members are the other way round

Pāli builds a large family of possessive compounds with the members in what looks like the wrong order, and one source names the phenomenon. Where a *bahubbīhi* says that some part of a person has been done something to, English and the grammars alike expect the participle first and the part second: 'cut-handed', *chinnahattho*. The canon regularly has the reverse: ***hatthacchinno***, hand-cut.[^cpd-inversion]

[^cpd-inversion]: Oberlies §24 *rem.*, p. 123: "Rather common are compounds with an inversion of the expected word-order", with *ambapakka(ṁ)* 'ripe mango fruit', *akkhacchinno* 'whose axle is broken', *hatthacchinno* 'whose hands are cut off', *aṅgulicchinnaṁ* 'whose finger has been cut off', *veṇikatā* 'having braided hair' beside the ordinary *kataveṇiyo*, and *vaṅkaghastā* 'having swallowed the hook' beside *gilitabalisā*. Perniola §139(c), p. 172, has one corner of the same fact (a word for a part of the body which would otherwise stand in the locative goes to the end of a *bahubbīhi*, as in *pattahatthā bhikkhū* 'monks with bowls in their hands') and no general statement. His §140, which opens further down that same page, is the *dvanda* turned into a *bahubbīhi* and is a different subject.

The texts settle which of the two orders a reader will actually meet. *Chinnahattho*, the standing example of every grammar consulted, occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; *hatthacchinno* occurs five times in the root texts, in the Mahāvagga's list of those who may not be ordained, in the company of *pādacchinno* and *hatthapādacchinno*. That is a striking result, and it is a reason to distrust a grammar's illustrations in a way one need not distrust its rules.

### The pieces that never stand alone

Some elements occur only at the end of a compound. They are neither abbreviations nor irregularities but ordinary agent nouns and action nouns that have ceased to be used on their own, and an attempt to look them up will fail. Perniola's list is the most practical page in his chapter.[^cpd-bound]

[^cpd-bound]: Perniola §135(c), pp. 167–168. Collins p. 133 makes the same observation in a sentence ("The final member can be a short word not used outside compounds"), with *-ññū* from √*ñā* and *-dhara* from √*dhṛ*. Duroiselle p. 264 gives the class its traditional name, *upapada*, on the authority of the *Niruttidīpanī*: when the second member of an accusative *tappurisa* is a primary derivative, the compound is called *upapada*.

| Element | From | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *-ga*, *-gū* | √*gam* | going, gone to | *pāragū* 'gone to the far shore', *vedagū* 'gone to knowledge' |
| *-ja* | √*jan* | born of | *vārijo* 'water-born', i.e. a fish; *aṇḍajā* 'egg-born'; *jalābujā* 'womb-born' |
| *-da* | √*dā* | giver of | *annado* 'giver of food', *balado* 'giver of strength' |
| *-ṭṭha* | √*ṭhā* | standing in | *gahaṭṭho* 'householder', *ākāsaṭṭhā* 'sky-dwelling' |
| *-pa* | √*pā* | drinking, protecting | *pādapo* 'foot-drinker', i.e. a tree; *gopo* 'cowherd' |
| *-ññū* | √*ñā* | knowing | *sabbaññū* 'all-knowing', *mattaññū* 'knowing moderation', *kataññū* 'grateful' |
| *-vidū* | √*vid* | knowing | *lokavidū* 'knower of the world' |
| *-bhū* | √*bhū* | being, overcoming | *sabbābhibhū* 'all-conquering' |
| *-chida* | √*chid* | cutting | *taṇhacchido* 'cutter of craving' |
| *-nuda* | √*nud* | dispelling | *tamonudo* 'dispeller of darkness' |
| *-dhara*, *-dhārī* | √*dhar* | bearing, carrying | *dhammadharo* 'one who bears the teaching in mind' |
| *-vādī* | √*vad* | speaking | *bhūtavādī* 'truth-speaker', *nivissavādī* 'dogmatist' |
| *-cārī*, *-vihārī* | √*car*, √*har* | faring, dwelling | *mettāvihārī* 'dwelling in loving-kindness' |
| *-kāra* | √*kar* | making, doing | *kammakāro* 'workman', *manasikāro* 'attention' |

Hold on to *kataññū*, because a dictionary gives another sense before its literal one: 'knowing what has been done', which in Pāli is what gratitude is.

The *-gū*, *-vidū* and *-bhū* elements leave the whole compound declining as a masculine noun in *-ū*, which is Appendix A.1's *ū*-stem masculine column, a paradigm this book otherwise has no occasion to use, because an ordinary Pāli noun ending in *-ū* is feminine. Perniola gives the paradigm on *abhibhū* 'conqueror', which is no compound at all, and three of the words he declines beside it are not compounds either. So the class is not confined to them. But compounds are where a reader will meet it.[^cpd-u-stem]

[^cpd-u-stem]: Appendix A.1 tables the class from Ñāṇatusita. Perniola §27, p. 34, is the paradigm; §27(b) declines *vidū* 'wise man', *abhijjhālū* 'covetous' and *aviddasū* 'ignorant' the same way. *Abhibhū* has 41 root-text occurrences, *abhijjhālū* 8 root-text occurrences and *aviddasū* 7 root-text occurrences, so none of the three is a grammarian's construction. The compounded members are simply the common ones: *lokavidū* has 146 root-text occurrences, *pāragū* 102 root-text occurrences and *vedagū* 80 root-text occurrences, every one masculine and every one a compound, while the *-ū* feminines are scarce enough that *vadhū* reaches the root texts only inside *kulavadhū*. See Chapter 3, where that scarcity is set out.

### Taking a long compound apart

The classes are the vocabulary; this is the method. Duroiselle sets it out with a five-layer example, and the procedure is the same at any length.[^cpd-resolution] **Work from the right.** Find the last member, which carries the inflection and the gender. Then ask what the whole of what precedes it does to it. Then treat that whole as a compound in its own right and repeat.

[^cpd-resolution]: Duroiselle p. 264, resolving *aparimitakālasañcitapuññabalanibbattāya* into five nested layers; Thānuttamo pp. 133–134 reproduces the resolution in full. Their example is a commentarial sentence, so the ones worked below were found for this chapter.

Take *pubbenivāsānussatiñāṇāya*, from the standard account of the three knowledges:

- pubbenivāsānussatiñāṇāya cittaṁ abhinīharati abhininnāmeti. (DN 2 — D i 81)
- *pubbenivāsānussatiñāṇāya* < *pubbe nivāsa* former abode + *anussati* recollection + *ñāṇa* knowledge, dative of purpose · *cittaṁ* the mind · *abhinīharati* he directs · *abhininnāmeti* he inclines
- He inclines his mind towards the knowledge of the recollection of past lives.

Resolved from the right:

| Layer | Members | Relation |
|---|---|---|
| *…ñāṇāya* | *pubbenivāsānussati* + *ñāṇa* | genitive *tappurisa*: knowledge **of** recollection |
| *pubbenivāsānussati* | *pubbenivāsa* + *anussati* | genitive *tappurisa*: recollection **of** the former abode |
| *pubbenivāsa* | *pubbe* + *nivāsa* | *alutta* locative: dwelling **in** the past |

Three layers, all determinative, and the innermost one keeps its locative ending: the *alutta* compound met above, inside a longer word where anyone not knowing the class would take *pubbe-* for a mistake.

A second, of a different shape:

- Evameva kho, brāhmaṇa, idha tathāgato loke uppajjati arahaṁ sammāsambuddho vijjācaraṇasampanno sugato lokavidū anuttaro purisadammasārathi satthā devamanussānaṁ buddho bhagavā. (MN 27 — M i 179)
- *tathāgato* the Realised One · *loke* in the world · *uppajjati* arises · *arahaṁ* worthy · *sammāsambuddho* < *sammā* rightly + *sambuddha* fully awakened, a *kammadhāraya* · *vijjācaraṇasampanno* < *vijjā* knowledge + *caraṇa* conduct — a *dvanda* — + *sampanna* endowed with, an instrumental *tappurisa*, the whole a *bahubbīhi* · *lokavidū* knower of the world · *purisadammasārathi* < *purisa* person + *damma* to be tamed — a *kammadhāraya* — + *sārathi* charioteer, the whole a genitive *tappurisa* · *satthā* teacher · *devamanussānaṁ* < *deva* god + *manussa* human, a *dvanda* in the genitive plural
- So too, brahmin, a Realised One arises in the world, worthy, fully awakened, endowed with knowledge and conduct, holy, knower of the world, unsurpassed trainer of those who can be trained, teacher of gods and humans, awakened, blessed.

One of the five deserves the same layer-by-layer treatment as the first, because its class changes at each step:

| Layer | Members | Relation |
|---|---|---|
| *vijjācaraṇasampanno* | *vijjācaraṇa-* + *sampanna* | instrumental *tappurisa*: endowed **with** knowledge and conduct |
| *vijjācaraṇa-* | *vijjā* + *caraṇa* | *dvanda*: knowledge **and** conduct |

Two layers, two classes, and then a third fact that is not a layer at all: the whole word stands beside *tathāgato* as a description of him. That is the *bahubbīhi*, which is a use and not a seventh class, and a table of members will never show it, because nothing inside the word changes.

Five compounds in one formula, and among them a *kammadhāraya*, a *dvanda* inside a *tappurisa*, a *tappurisa* used as a *bahubbīhi*, a bound final member, and a *dvanda* carrying the whole sentence's genitive. Take that formula apart and you can take apart most of what the canon presents, and the formula recurs often enough to reward the work many times over.

### What a compound will not tell you

Three things, and it is better to say so than to imply that a good enough analysis always settles them.

It will not tell you the number of its members, as the *dvanda* section has already shown.

It will not tell you which case relation is meant, and sometimes more than one is defensible. *Ariyasāvako*, ordinarily 'noble disciple', could be read as 'a disciple who is a noble one', 'a disciple of the noble one', or 'a disciple of the noble ones': a *kammadhāraya* or either of two genitive *tappurisas*. *Ariyasacca* is the same problem in a more consequential place: 'noble truth', 'truth of the noble ones', or 'truth for the noble ones'. Sometimes several senses are meant at once, and a translator who forces one is subtracting.

It will not always keep its first member to itself. This is the subtlest of the three, and it is best known about in advance. Hendriksen puts it well:

> The Indian compounds are not so much felt as a unit as for instance the compounds in the western European languages; the first member of the compound has often in some degree preserved its syntactic independence. This appears plainly from the cases, by no means rare, in which a word outside the compound is syntactically connected with the first member only.[^cpd-hendriksen]

[^cpd-hendriksen]: Hendriksen, *Syntax of the Infinite Verb-forms of Pali* (1944), p. 145, quoted by Thānuttamo p. 125. The observation is at second hand here and is flagged as such.

That is to say: a word elsewhere in the sentence may construe with something buried inside a compound, which no amount of care about the compound's internal structure will reveal. It is a reason to read the sentence and not merely the word.

### Compounds that break the rules

Pāli has several types of compound that fit none of the six classes, and one source in this collection names them.[^cpd-oberlies-odd] They are uncommon, but each can make a reader distrust their own analysis on meeting it.

[^cpd-oberlies-odd]: Oberlies §24 *rem.*, pp. 122–123, drawing the categories from the *Critical Pāli Dictionary*'s *Epilegomena*: loose compound, echo compound, quasi-compound, split compound, tautological compound, and *tmesis*. Every one of his instances carries a canonical reference.

**The echo compound**, in which a word is repeated with its initial altered, expressing agitation or confusion: *akkulapakkulikaṁ akāsi* 'made a commotion' (Ud 1.7 — Ud p. 5). The pattern is familiar from spoken languages everywhere and is rare in the canon.

**The tautological compound**, whose two members mean the same thing: *atītagatasatthuno* 'of the teacher who has gone by' (Th 1035), where *atīta* and *gata* both mean 'gone'.

**The split compound**, in which the two members of what should be one word stand apart in the sentence, usually for the metre. And ***tmesis***, its extreme form, in which another word gets in between: *Haṁsassa anabhiññāya, mahā me bhayamāgataṁ*, 'through not recognising the goose, great fear has come upon me', where *mahā* and *bhayaṁ* are the two halves of *mahābhayaṁ* with the enclitic *me* wedged between them.[^cpd-tmesis]

[^cpd-tmesis]: Oberlies §24 *rem.*, p. 123, citing Ja III 210,4\*, where he prints the line's second half as *mahā me bhayaṁ āgataṁ*. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *bhayamāgataṁ* joined, in the Mittavindaka Jātaka — a difference of word division, not of reading, and an instance of what the box above says about editors.

**The *phalāphala* type**, which deserves more attention than its rarity suggests. A word is compounded with its own negation or opposite, and the sense is 'every sort of A', not 'A and not-A':

- Gambhīrapaññaṁ medhāviṁ, maggāmaggassa kovidaṁ; (Snp 3.9 — Sn p. 120)
- *gambhīrapaññaṁ* of deep wisdom · *medhāviṁ* intelligent · *maggāmaggassa* < *magga* path + *amagga* non-path · *kovidaṁ* skilled in
- One of deep wisdom, intelligent, skilled in what is the path and what is not;

The line is Sn's and recurs at Dhp 403. Read as an ordinary *dvanda* it says 'skilled in path and non-path', which is nearly meaningless; read as this type it says 'who knows the whole question of the path'. The canon has others of the same form (*kusalākusalaṁ* 'the wholesome and the unwholesome', *piyāppiyaṁ* 'the liked and the disliked'), and each names a field, not two items.

### Compounds built out of sentences

One last group, which Collins gathers under a heading borrowed from Western scholarship and which belongs here because the members are not nouns at all.[^cpd-syntactic]

[^cpd-syntactic]: Collins pp. 135–136, four types: those whose first member is a verb in a form other than an adjectival participle; those whose first member is a past participle governing something outside the compound; those whose members would, separated, have required a *ti* clause; and the *ehi-* compounds. Perniola §142, pp. 173–174, covers the same ground in three groups, and the *ehi-* compounds are inside his third, not named as a type, so the fourth type is Collins's alone, and it explains the most. Perniola does, in passing, corroborate the derivation this chapter had to settle from DPD: against *ehipassiko* he notes in brackets that *ehi* and *passa* "are imperatives".

The first member may be an **absolutive**: *paṭiccasamuppādo* 'dependent origination' is *paṭicca* 'having depended' plus *samuppāda* 'arising'; *aveccappasādo* 'confirmed confidence' is *avecca* 'having penetrated' plus *pasāda*; *uddissakataṁ* 'made on purpose' is *uddissa* 'having designated' plus *kata*.

Or the members may be words that, separated, would have needed a quotative *ti* between them: a whole small sentence packed into a compound. *Sādhusammato* is 'reckoned to be good', from *sādhū ti sammato*. And the finest instance is a word every Buddhist knows:

- svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko paccattaṁ veditabbo viññūhī'ti. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)
- *svākkhāto* well proclaimed · *bhagavatā* by the Blessed One · *dhammo* the teaching · *sandiṭṭhiko* visible here and now · *akāliko* timeless · *ehipassiko* < *ehi* come! + *passa* see! + *-ika* · *opaneyyiko* leading onwards · *paccattaṁ* individually · *veditabbo* to be known · *viññūhi* by the wise
- The teaching is well proclaimed by the Blessed One — visible here and now, timeless, inviting one to come and see, leading onwards, to be known individually by the wise.

***Ehipassiko*** is two second-person imperatives with an adjectival suffix on the end.[^cpd-ehipassika] The Dhamma is a 'come-and-see!' thing. No class of compound in any grammar's list quite accommodates it, and it is one of the most familiar words in the canon.

[^cpd-ehipassika]: DPD, s.v. *ehipassika*: construction *ehi + passa + ika*, literally "come! see!". Collins p. 136 gives the whole family — *ehibhaddantika*, 'one invited with the words "come, sir"', and *ehibhikkhupabbajjā*, the ordination performed with the words "come, monk".

### A suffix that turns a compound into an adjective

Finally, a small device that belongs here and not with the suffixes of Chapter 14, because it operates on a finished compound, not on a stem. Adding *-ika*, or its variant *-iya*, to a compound makes it an adjective, as English *-ed* makes *white coat* into *white-coated*.[^cpd-ika]

[^cpd-ika]: Collins p. 135, with the English analogy and a table of five worked pairs; Perniola §137(b), p. 170; Duroiselle p. 251. The initial vowel is sometimes strengthened in the process, which turns *punabbhava* into *ponobhavika*: the same front *vuddhi* Chapter 14 shows when *upa-* becomes *opa-* in *opaneyyiko*.

| Compound | Adjective | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| *anuloma* 'with the hair' | *anulomika* | in accordance with, proper |
| *punabbhava* 'renewed existence' | *ponobhavika* | leading to rebirth |
| *aṭṭhakavagga* 'the Chapter of Eights' | *aṭṭhakavaggikāni* | belonging to the Chapter of Eights |
| *tiracchānakathā* 'animal talk' | *tiracchānakathikā* | those who talk frivolously |
| *bhīrukajāti* 'fearful nature' | *bhīrukajātika* | of a fearful nature |
| *dhammakathā* 'talk on the teaching' | *dhammakathika* | a preacher |

The last has become entirely ordinary: *dhammakathiko* occurs seventy-seven times in the root texts and is simply the word for a preacher, its compound origin invisible.

### Where the compounds chapter stops

Prefixes joined to verbal roots (*ā-gacchati*, *upa-saṅkamati*, *sam-uppajjati*) are treated by two of the sources read here as compounds, and are not treated as compounds in this book. Warder gives the reason in a footnote and it is the right one: "the combination of prefixes with roots is a different matter".[^cpd-boundary] A prefixed verb is one word built, not two words joined, and Chapter 14 has the twenty prefixes with their senses.

[^cpd-boundary]: Warder p. 77 n. 1, which also states the other limit: "Finite verbs are not compounded with nouns, but participles and other nouns derived from verbs may be." Perniola's chapter X, "Verbal compounds", is the material excluded here.

Two other boundaries, both already drawn elsewhere. What each case *does* is Chapter 15's; this chapter names the case relation inside a compound and leaves its syntax there. And the adverbial accusative (*sukhaṁ*, *dukkhaṁ*, *khippaṁ*) belongs with the adjectives in Chapter 4 and the indeclinables in Chapter 12; what this chapter adds is that an *avyayībhāva* is one way such an adverb gets made.

### Splitting before reading

Faced with a long compound, work in a fixed order. Find the joins first, restoring any first member the join has shortened: *mahā-* to *mahant*, *rāja-* to *rājan*. Then start from the right: the last member carries the case and the gender, and decides the word's function in its sentence. Then ask what everything in front of it does to it, and repeat, a layer at a time. Only when the layers are named is it time to translate, and it is the sentence around the word, not the word, that decides whether the whole is describing something else.
