## Gender and Adjectives

The previous chapter set out the endings. This one is about the two things that decide which endings a word takes: its **gender**, which for a noun is fixed and must simply be known, and **agreement**, which is how an adjective borrows the gender, number and case of whatever it describes.

### Gender is grammatical

Every Pāli noun is masculine, neuter or feminine, and that assignment is a fact about the word, not about the thing it names. The clearest demonstration is a set of words meaning the same thing in three genders:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| *maggo* | masculine | road |
| *vīthi* | feminine | road, street |
| *vaṭumaṁ* | neuter | road |

Nothing about a road is masculine, feminine or neuter. The gender belongs to the word.[^road-triple]

Where a noun does name something with a sex, the grammatical gender usually agrees (*kassako* 'farmer' is masculine, *bhikkhunī* 'nun' feminine), and it is safest to treat that as a convenience, not a rule, because the exceptions are common. *Dāra* 'wife' is masculine, and is met in the plural: *sehi dārehi asantuṭṭho*, 'not content with his own wives'. *Itthiratanaṁ*, 'the woman-treasure' in the list of a wheel-turning monarch's seven treasures, is neuter.[^dara]

[^dara]: DPD, s.v. *dāra*, masculine, 'wife', citing the Parābhava Sutta; it also records a feminine *dārā* built from the same stem. In the root texts the word is found in oblique forms (*dāraṁ* 17 occurrences, and the *dārehi* of the verse quoted), not in the nominative singular *dāro*, which occurs only in the grammarians' texts. *Itthiratanaṁ* has 25 occurrences in the root texts.

[^road-triple]: Perniola §24, p. 29, makes the same point with *maggo*, *vattani*, *vaṭumaṁ*. His feminine has been replaced here: *vattani* occurs only in the grammarians' texts and *vaṭṭanī* not at all, whereas *vīthi* 'street, road' is ordinary canonical Pāli: *vīthiṁ* has 35 occurrences in the root texts, among them MN 33 — M i 220, where the incompetent cowherd "does not know the road". DPD, s.v. *vīthi*, gives 'street; road' and 'path; track'; s.v. *vaṭuma*, 'road, path', neuter.

#### How to tell a noun's gender

Most of the time a reader does not need to. The case is what carries the meaning, and the case can usually be read off the ending without settling the gender at all. When it does matter, there are three ways to find out.

**The dictionary says so.** Most mark m., n. and f. The PTS *Pali-English Dictionary* misleads here, because it omits 'm.': an entry with no gender given is masculine.

**The nominative singular usually shows it.** This is the practical method, and it works because the previous chapter found the three genders differing in the nominative. In the *a*-declension, *-o* is masculine and *-aṁ* neuter; *-ā* is a feminine *ā*-stem.

- … pubbe ananussutesu dhammesu **cakkhuṁ** udapādi, **ñāṇaṁ** udapādi, **paññā** udapādi, **vijjā** udapādi, **āloko** udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)
- … regarding teachings not heard before, **vision** arose, **knowledge** arose, **wisdom** arose, **realisation** arose, **light** arose.

Five nouns, all nominative singular, all subjects of the same verb. Their endings sort them without any need for a dictionary: *cakkhuṁ* and *ñāṇaṁ* are neuter, *paññā* and *vijjā* feminine, *āloko* masculine.

An adjective agreeing with it shows it. If the noun's own ending is ambiguous, a word describing it may not be, and that is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

> **When the manuscripts disagree**
>
> A fair number of nouns are found in more than one gender, and the commonest reason is that the manuscript traditions were undecided, not Pāli. These are variants of the same order as the *ṭ*/*t* and *ḷ*/*l* spellings met in the chapter on the alphabet: real, recorded and of no consequence for reading.
>
> A second and more interesting reason was met in the previous chapter. The *-as* stems (*mano*, *cetas*, *tapas*) are in the middle of moving into the *a*-declension, and a word whose nominative is *mano* invites masculine agreement whatever its history. The canon supplies both. In the list of the six senses at SN 35.2, five sense-bases take the agreement their own gender requires and the sixth takes *mano dukkho*, masculine.

#### A noun keeps its gender when it describes another noun

Nouns are fixed in gender, and they do not surrender it when they are set beside another noun to characterise it. So a perfectly ordinary Pāli sentence can equate two nouns of different genders, with different endings, without error:

- **Moho** **nidānaṁ** kammānaṁ samudayāya. (AN 3.34 — A i 134)
- **Delusion** is a **source** for the origination of deeds.

- **Puññāni** paralokasmiṁ, **patiṭṭhā** honti pāṇinaṁ. (AN 5.36 — A iii 41)
- **Good deeds** are a **support** for beings in the world beyond.

*Moho* is masculine and *nidānaṁ* neuter; *puññāni* is neuter plural and *patiṭṭhā* a feminine singular. An adjective could not do this. That is the difference between a noun and an adjective, and the next section states it more formally.

### Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun. The Pāli grammarians called it *guṇanāma*, 'quality-name', against *nāmanāma*, 'name-name', for the noun proper — and they drew the line between the two where this chapter has been heading: a noun has one gender and an adjective has three.[^collins-guna]

[^collins-guna]: Collins §2.1, who sets out the indigenous distinction and its two criteria: that adjectives take the gender of the noun they qualify, and that they can be compared. He adds that a noun standing as the final member of a compound acquires all three genders too, so the line is thinner in practice than in principle, a point the chapter on compounds takes up.

An adjective **agrees** with its noun: it takes that noun's gender, its number and its case. The stem stays what it is; only the ending moves.

- **Cakkhuṁ**, bhikkhave, **dukkhaṁ**. … **jivhā dukkhā** … **kāyo dukkho** … **mano dukkho**. (SN 35.2 — S iv 2)
- **The eye**, mendicants, is **suffering**. … **the tongue** is **suffering** … **the body** is **suffering** … **the mind** is **suffering**.

One adjective, *dukkha*, four times, in three genders. It is neuter with *cakkhuṁ*, feminine with *jivhā*, masculine with *kāyo*, and masculine again with *mano*, the last for the reason given in the box above.

A single sentence in the Vinaya's ordination procedure shows the same, and adds number to it. Straight after an ordination five things are to be settled, and each of them is put in a gerundive:

- Tāvadeva chāyā metabbā, utuppamāṇaṁ **ācikkhitabbaṁ**, divasabhāgo **ācikkhitabbo**, saṅgīti **ācikkhitabbā**, cattāro nissayā **ācikkhitabbā**. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)
- *tāvadeva* straightaway · *chāyā*<sub>a</sub> the shadow (f. nom. sg.) · *metabbā*<sub>a</sub> is to be measured (f. nom. sg.) · *utuppamāṇaṁ*<sub>b</sub> the date (n. nom. sg.) · *ācikkhitabbaṁ*<sub>b</sub> is to be told (n. nom. sg.) · *divasabhāgo*<sub>c</sub> the part of the day (m. nom. sg.) · *ācikkhitabbo*<sub>c</sub> (m. nom. sg.) · *saṅgīti*<sub>d</sub> the recitation (f. nom. sg.) · *ācikkhitabbā*<sub>d</sub> (f. nom. sg.) · *cattāro nissayā*<sub>e</sub> the four supports (m. nom. pl.) · *ācikkhitabbā*<sub>e</sub> (m. nom. pl.)
- "Straightaway the shadow is to be measured, the date **told**, the part of the day **told**, the recitation **told**, and the four supports **told**."

*Ācikkhitabba* 'to be told' stands four times in one sentence and in four shapes: neuter singular beside *utuppamāṇaṁ* 'the date', masculine singular beside *divasabhāgo* 'the part of the day', feminine singular beside *saṅgīti* 'the recitation', masculine plural beside *cattāro nissayā* 'the four supports'.[^acikkhitabba-variant] The stem never moves.

Laid out pair by pair, the sentence shows something the running text hides:

| Noun | Its predicate | What settles the predicate's form |
|---|---|---|
| *chāyā* (f. nom. sg.) | *metabbā* | the noun |
| *utuppamāṇaṁ* (n. nom. sg.) | *ācikkhitabbaṁ* | its own ending |
| *divasabhāgo* (m. nom. sg.) | *ācikkhitabbo* | its own ending |
| *saṅgīti* (f. nom. sg.) | *ācikkhitabbā* | the noun |
| *cattāro nissayā* (m. nom. pl.) | *ācikkhitabbā* | the noun |

The last two are spelled alike, and the word itself cannot decide between them. *Ācikkhitabbā* is the feminine nominative singular, and it is also the masculine nominative plural. *Saṅgīti* on the one side and *cattāro nissayā* on the other decide it, so an adjective can settle a noun's gender (the third of the three methods given above), and a noun can equally settle an adjective's. Agreement runs in both directions.

[^acikkhitabba-variant]: The apparatus disagrees about what follows, and no variant touches the running text quoted here. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's own note gives *cattāri akaraṇīyāni ācikkhitabbāni* as an addition after *saṅgīti ācikkhitabbā, cattāro nissayā ācikkhitabbā*, and sources it to *ka.*, 'in some manuscripts'. DPD's entry for *utuppamāṇa* cites this sentence as its illustration, and prints the sandhi divided, *tāva'd'eva*.

Number and case work the same way:

- Idha, bhikkhave, **pāpaṇiko kusalo** hoti paṇiyaṁ ketuñca vikketuñca. (AN 3.20 — A i 116)
- Here, mendicants, **a shopkeeper** is **skilful** at buying and selling goods.

- … riñcati **kusalaṁ ṭhānaṁ**. (MN 60 — M i 403)
- … he neglects **the skilful course**.

- Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, **paṇḍito byatto kusalo sūdo** rājānaṁ vā rājamahāmattaṁ vā nānaccayehi sūpehi paccupaṭṭhito assa … (SN 47.8 — S v 151)
- Suppose, mendicants, **a wise, capable, skilful cook** were to serve a king or a king's minister with various sauces …

*Kusalo* masculine nominative singular in the first, *kusalaṁ* neuter accusative singular in the second, and in the third one noun with three adjectives, all four words in step.

#### An adjective's stem does not change

Agreement moves the ending, not the stem. An adjective in *-a* describing a masculine *u*-stem noun becomes masculine, but it stays an *a*-stem: *kusalo bhikkhu*, not *kusalu bhikkhu*. So the previous chapter's paradigms are the whole apparatus needed for adjectives too: an adjective in *-a* declines like *purisa*, *rūpa* and *kaññā* according to the gender it has taken on, one in *-i* like *aggi* or *jāti*, one in *-(n)t* like *bhagavant*.

Perniola puts it in one line: the adjective *kusala* "is declined like *puriso* in the masculine, like *vanitā* in the feminine, and like *rūpaṁ* in the neuter".[^perniola-adj]

[^perniola-adj]: Perniola §31, p. 37, whose opening sentence he has already made once, at the end of §24 on p. 30. His §31 sets out *dīgha* 'long' in all three genders and both numbers, and notes that "when the adjective ends in *-i*, the adjective is declined like *nadī*" in the feminine.

### Forming the feminine

An adjective needs a feminine form, and so does any noun naming a role that women fill. Pāli builds both from the masculine stem, and which suffix it uses depends on what the stem ends in. Perniola gathers the rules; the texts decide between the versions where sources differ.[^fem-perniola]

| Masculine stem | Feminine | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun in *-a* | *-ī* | *dāsa* → *dāsī* 'servant'; *cora* → *corī* 'thief'; *deva* → *devī* 'goddess, queen'; *kumāra* → *kumārī* 'girl'; *brāhmaṇa* → *brāhmaṇī* |
| **adjective** in *-a* | *-ā* | *dīgha* → *dīghā* 'long'; *manāpa* → *manāpā* 'agreeable'; *gata* → *gatā* 'gone' |
| *-aka* | *-ikā* | *janaka* → *janikā* 'mother'; *kumāraka* → *kumārikā*; *upāsaka* → *upāsikā* 'laywoman'; *sāvaka* → *sāvikā* 'female disciple' |
| *-in* | *-inī* | *hatthin* → *hatthinī* 'she-elephant'; *musāvādin* → *musāvādinī* 'liar' |
| *-u*, *-ū* | *-nī* | *bhikkhu* → *bhikkhunī* 'nun'; *abhijjhālū* → *abhijjhālunī* 'covetous' |
| *-(n)t*, adjective | *-tī* (weak stem) | *mahant* → *mahatī* 'great'; *sīlavant* → *sīlavatī* 'virtuous'; *guṇavant* → *guṇavatī* |
| *-(n)t*, present participle | *-ntī* (strong stem) | *gacchant* → *gacchantī* 'going'; *vasant* → *vasantī* 'dwelling' |

The second row is the most useful, and the most easily missed. A noun in *-a* makes its feminine in *-ī* and an adjective in *-a* makes its feminine in *-ā*. So *dāsa* gives *dāsī* but *dīgha* gives *dīghā*, and the difference is grammatical, not phonetic. Perniola notes that a few words do both, according to which they are being used as: *kalyāṇa* 'beautiful' gives the adjective *kalyāṇā* but the noun *kalyāṇī* 'a beautiful woman', and both are canonical.[^dahara]

[^dahara]: Perniola §38, N.B., p. 46. His other instance of the pair, *dahara* 'young' → adjectival *daharā* but nominal *daharī* 'young woman', is only half supported: *daharā* has 54 occurrences in the root texts and *daharī* none anywhere but in the grammarians' texts. The rule is sound; that particular illustration of it is not, and it has been left out.

- **Sukhiniyo** hontu tā, mahārāja, somā ca bhaginī sakulā ca bhaginī. (MN 90 — M ii 126)[^sukhiniyo]
- May they be **happy**, great king — sister Somā and sister Sakulā.

- Tena kho pana samayena sukkā bhikkhunī **mahatiyā** parisāya parivutā dhammaṁ deseti. (SN 10.9 — S i 212)
- Now at that time the nun Sukkā was teaching the Dhamma surrounded by **a large** assembly.

[^sukhiniyo]: *Sukhinī* is the feminine of *sukhin* 'happy', an *-in* stem, and *sukhiniyo* its nominative plural: the wish is addressed to two nuns. The form is rare: one occurrence in the root texts, this one.

The Vinaya is two codes, one for monks and one for nuns, and although they differ in what they forbid they are built on one frame. That puts *bhikkhu* and *bhikkhunī* (the *-u* row of the table) into sentences which hold everything else constant:

- Yo pana **bhikkhu** atirekacīvaraṁ dhāreyya, nissaggiyaṁ pācittiyan'ti. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 195)
- *yo* whoever (m. nom. sg.) · *pana* moreover · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *atirekacīvaraṁ* a surplus robe (n. acc. sg.) · *dhāreyya* should keep (3 sg. opt.) · *nissaggiyaṁ* entailing relinquishment (n. nom. sg.) · *pācittiyaṁ* an offence entailing confession (n. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation)
- "Whatever **monk** should keep a surplus robe: an offence entailing relinquishment and confession."

- Yā pana **bhikkhunī** pattasannicayaṁ kareyya, nissaggiyaṁ pācittiyan'ti. (Bi Np 1 — Vin iv 243)
- *yā* whoever (f. nom. sg.) · *pana* moreover · *bhikkhunī* a nun (f. nom. sg.) · *pattasannicayaṁ* a stash of bowls (m. acc. sg.) · *kareyya* should make (3 sg. opt.) · *nissaggiyaṁ pācittiyaṁ* as above · *ti* (quotation)
- "Whatever **nun** should make a stash of bowls: an offence entailing relinquishment and confession."

Two different offences and one frame. *Yo* becomes *yā* and *bhikkhu* becomes *bhikkhunī*; the optative, and the verbless clause that states the penalty, do not move at all. Even that clause is an agreement: *nissaggiyaṁ* is itself a gerundive, 'to be relinquished', taking the neuter of *pācittiyaṁ*.

The parallel is not confined to the rules. The formal act that opens a meeting of the Sangha begins *Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho* among monks and *Suṇātu me, ayye, saṅgho* among nuns (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196, Bi Np 1 — Vin iv 244): one word apart, and the word is the term of address.

A handful of nouns form the feminine irregularly, and three are common: *nara* 'man' → *nārī*, *assa* 'horse' → *vaḷavā* 'mare', *go* 'ox' → *gāvī* 'cow'.[^fem-irregular]

[^fem-perniola]: Perniola §38, pp. 45–47, whose nine rules are the basis of the table. Two of his examples have been dropped because the texts do not support them. Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, *tāpasī* 'female ascetic' is (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 10), the grammarians' texts alone, and *sāminī* 'mistress' (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 15, ṭīkā 0, later works 1), the commentaries alone. Every form printed in the table above is canonical.

[^fem-irregular]: Perniola §38(h), p. 47, gives a list of twelve. Of them a reader will meet *nārī*, *vaḷavā* and *gāvī*: 71, 1 and 39 occurrences respectively in the root texts. Two others he prints are not canonical and are left out; *rājinī* 'queen' occurs only in the grammarians' texts, though *devī* does the same job and is common.

### Comparative and superlative

An adjective can be compared, and this is the second of the two criteria by which the tradition separated adjectives from nouns. Pāli forms the comparative and the superlative in three ways, and keeps a small stock of irregulars besides.[^comparative]

**The ordinary suffixes are *-tara* and *-tama***, added to the stem: *piya* 'dear' → *piyataro*, *paṇḍita* 'wise' → *paṇḍitataro*, *bahu* 'much' → *bahutaro*. Of the two the comparative is much the commoner; several stems form a *-tara* freely and a *-tama* barely at all.[^tara-tama] These suffixes are productive, and they attach to a good deal more than plain adjectives: to past participles (*abhikkantataro* 'more surpassing'), to compounds (*sīlasampannataraṁ* 'endowed with greater virtue'), and even to nouns (*sappurisataro* 'a better man').

**An older pair, *-iya* and *-iṭṭha***, survives in a few words: *pāpa* 'bad' → *pāpiyo*, *pāpiṭṭha*.[^papittha]

[^tara-tama]: *Piyataro* is canonical; *piyatamo* occurs nowhere, and the superlative shows up once in the whole of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, as the instrumental *piyatamena*. A grammar's neat pairs invite the conclusion that both members are equally available, and they are not.

[^papittha]: The superlative *pāpiṭṭha* is a curious case. In the nominative singular *pāpiṭṭho* it occurs only in the grammarians' texts, but its own doubled comparative *pāpiṭṭhatara* is thoroughly canonical, with *pāpiṭṭhataraṁ* at 17 occurrences in the root texts and four further inflected shapes beside it. The canon uses the comparative of the superlative more readily than the superlative.

**A third suffix, *-ma***, makes superlatives from a small set, often with the stem vowel changed to *-i*: *majjha* 'middle' → *majjhima*, *anta* 'end' → *antima* 'last', *pacchā* 'after' → *pacchima* 'last', *para* → *parama* 'highest', *pura* → *purima* 'former'.

The irregulars have to be learnt as vocabulary, because they are frequent and because they do not look like the words they belong to:

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| *sant* 'good' | *seyya* 'better' | *seṭṭha* 'best' |
| *yuva* 'young' | *kaniya* | *kaniṭṭha* 'youngest' |
| *vuḍḍha* 'old' | *jeyya* | *jeṭṭha* 'eldest' |
| *bahu* 'much' | *bhiyyo* 'more' | — |

*Seṭṭha* and *jeṭṭha* and *kaniṭṭha* are everywhere in the canon, the first in the Buddha's own titles and the other two in every account of a family. *Bhiyyo* is likewise common, and it does not decline: it is an adverb, 'more, further'.

A curiosity, and evidence of how far these had drifted from the words they came from: several have themselves acquired a fresh comparative. The canon has *pāpiṭṭhataraṁ*, a comparative built on a superlative meaning 'worst'; *seyyataraṁ*, one built on 'better'; and *purimatarāni*, one built on 'former'. The suffix had stopped being felt as a suffix.[^double-comparative]

The comparative and superlative endings also attach to prefixes and adverbs, making adjectives out of them: *ud-* 'up' gives *uttara* 'higher' and *uttama* 'highest'; *adha-* 'down' gives *adhara* and *adhama* 'lowest'; *upari* 'above' gives *uparima* 'uppermost'.[^prefix-comparative]

[^comparative]: Perniola §39, pp. 48–52, twelve subsections, which is the fullest treatment in any source consulted. Oberlies handles the comparatives under §38.2, *transfer of °as- into a-stems*, because the *-iya* comparative is historically an *-yas*-stem, and so *seyya* and *bhiyyo* look as they do. The old instrumental of *bhiyyo* survives in one place, the fixed phrase *yebhuyyena* 'for the most part', common enough at 129 occurrences in the root texts, but *bhuyyena* is never found standing on its own.

[^double-comparative]: Perniola §39(g), p. 49, gives four: *seyyatara*, *seṭṭhatara*, *pāpiṭṭhatara*, *purimatara*. Three of the four are found in the texts; only *seṭṭhatara* is not, occurring in the grammarians' texts alone. All three of the survivors are canonical only in inflected forms (*pāpiṭṭhataraṁ* 17 occurrences in the root texts, *purimatarāni* 10, *seyyataraṁ* 1), so the chapter prints them as the canon has them, not as bare stems. Each of the three is missing in its nominative singular and is found only through the stem. A word the canon uses only in oblique cases will always read as absent if it is looked up in a nominative the canon never uses.

[^prefix-comparative]: Perniola §39(l), p. 51, for the forms built on prepositions and adverbs; *majjhima* and *antima* come from his §39(d), p. 49, which is the *-ma* superlative with the stem vowel raised to *-i*. Of the forms he prints, *uttaraṁ*, *uttamo*, *pacchimo*, *paramo*, *purimo* and *majjhimo* are all canonical; *adhamo* and *antimo* occur in the commentaries but not the root texts, and are given above as glosses, not as canonical forms.

How a comparison is expressed in a sentence (what case the thing compared against goes into) is a question about the cases and belongs with them; the chapter on case syntax takes it up under the ablative.

### Nouns made from adjectives

The line between adjective and noun is thin in English and thinner in Pāli. English can say 'the grass is green' and 'green is a beautiful colour', using one word two ways, and Pāli does this with almost every adjective it has: a word listed in the dictionary as an adjective can nearly always stand on its own as a noun.

When such a noun names a thing or a quality it is generally **neuter**: *dukkhaṁ* 'suffering', *kusalaṁ* 'the wholesome'. When it names a person it takes the gender of the person, masculine or feminine.

- Loke samaññaṁ **kusalo** viditvā … (SN 1.25 — S i 15)
- Having understood **the skilled one's** designation in the world …

Context is the only guide. In *riñcati kusalaṁ ṭhānaṁ* above, *kusalaṁ* is an adjective describing *ṭhānaṁ*; standing alone it would be the noun 'the wholesome'.

### Adverbs made from adjectives

English turns an adjective into an adverb with *-ly*: *kind* becomes *kindly*. Pāli uses the accusative singular of the adjective. *Visama* 'uneven, unjust' gives *visamaṁ* 'unjustly'; *dīgha* 'long' gives *dīghaṁ* 'at length'; *sādhuka* 'good' gives *sādhukaṁ* 'well'; *nicca* 'constant' gives *niccaṁ* 'constantly'; *cira* 'long' gives *ciraṁ* 'for a long time'.

Only context distinguishes such an adverb from the accusative it is made of, and the test is simple: ask what the word is describing. If it describes a noun it is an adjective in the accusative; if it describes a verb it is an adverb. The pairs below make the test concrete.

- Gottena mattā **visamaṁ** caranti. (SN 35.132 — S iv 117)
- Drunk on their lineage, they behave **unjustly**.

- **Visamaṁ** maggamāruyha … (SN 2.22 — S i 57)
- Having taken **the uneven** road …

- Dīghaṁ vā assasanto '**dīghaṁ** assasāmī'ti pajānāti. (MN 118 — M iii 82)
- Breathing in long, he knows 'I breathe in **long**'.

- **Dīghaṁ** santassa yojanaṁ. (Dhp 60 — Dhp p. 9)
- **Long** is a league to the weary.

In the first of each pair the word answers "how?" and modifies the verb; in the second it answers "which?" and modifies a noun. *Visamaṁ caranti* is 'they walk unevenly'; *visamaṁ maggaṁ* is 'an uneven road'.

Strictly, such adverbs are no longer in a case at all. They are **indeclinables** that happen to have the form of an accusative, frozen in it; the chapter on indeclinables returns to them.

What the cases mean and how comparison is constructed are in the chapter on case syntax. How a noun keeps or changes its gender inside a compound is in the chapter on compounds, where the *bahubbīhi* type turns whole phrases into adjectives. And the **complete declensions**, for adjectives as for nouns, are in Appendix A.1, since an adjective has no endings of its own.

**Exercises for this chapter are in the workbook**, under "Exercises: Gender and Adjectives", with worked answers at the back of that volume.

**Principal sources.** Perniola §§24, 31, 38 and 39 for gender, agreement, the formation of the feminine, and the comparative; Oberlies §§28.2 and 38.2 for where the comparatives came from; the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana itself for which of the forms the grammars print are actually used.
