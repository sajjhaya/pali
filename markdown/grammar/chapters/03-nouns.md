## Nouns: Stems and Declension

A Pāli noun is a **stem** plus an **ending**. The stem carries the meaning and does not change; the ending carries the grammar and changes constantly. *Buddha* is a stem, and it is not a word: what the texts contain is *buddho*, *buddhaṁ*, *buddhena*, *buddhassa*, *buddhe*, *buddhānaṁ*. The consequence is one a reader meets on the first page of any sutta. No Pāli word ends in a bare consonant, and no Pāli word is a bare stem; every word in the canon ends in a vowel or in the niggahīta *ṁ*. When a grammar prints *rājan* or *bhagavant* or *satthar*, it is printing something the texts never say.

The set of endings a stem takes is its **declension** (*vibhatti*, 'the division'), and stems that take the same endings form a **declension group**. There are around twenty of these, which sounds worse than it is: three of them account for most of the canon, and the rest are variations legible from those three.

### Cases, numbers and genders

Every inflected noun in the canon simultaneously answers three questions: what job is it doing in its sentence, how many of it are there, and what gender is it.

**Seven cases.** Nominative, vocative, accusative, instrumental, ablative, genitive/dative, locative. This book's tables print seven rows and not eight, because the genitive and the dative have fallen together: the same ending serves both almost everywhere.[^gen-dat] What each case *does* is the business of the chapter on case syntax; here it is only their forms.

**Two numbers**, singular and plural. Old Indic had a third, the dual, and Pāli has lost it entirely. Only a single word survives, *ubho* 'both', which still inflects as one: the same fact met from the other side in the chapter on numerals, where it explains why 'two' has no paradigm of its own.[^dual]

**Three genders**, masculine, neuter and feminine, abbreviated m., n. and f. throughout. Gender here is a grammatical category and not a statement about the world: the chapter on gender and adjectives takes that up, and shows three words for 'road' in three different genders.

[^gen-dat]: Oberlies §28.6, p. 137, treats this under *case syncretism*, the general term for two cases collapsing into one form. A separate dative survives in strength only in the *a*-stem singular, where `-āya` stands beside genitive `-assa`: *hitāya* 'for the welfare', *sukhāya* 'for the happiness', *dassanāya* 'in order to see' (Perniola §25e, p. 31). Ñāṇatusita marks the same form `āya (= Dat.)` in his own table, reproduced at Appendix A.1.

[^dual]: Oberlies §28.1, pp. 130–131. Perniola §24, p. 29, states the two numbers without comment on the loss.

### The one fact that organises the rest

Before any paradigm, the fact that makes them learnable instead of memorisable. Perniola states it in a sentence and then moves on, and it deserves more weight than he gives it:

> the masculine and the neuter nouns are declined in the same way with the exception of the nominative, vocative and accusative singular and plural. The feminine nouns have different case endings.[^perniola-24]

So the masculine and neuter are one declension with a small exception, not two declensions. Everywhere except the three cases Perniola names, *buddhena* and *cittena*, *buddhassa* and *cittassa*, *buddhesu* and *cittesu* (a masculine and a neuter) are simply the same. The gender difference lives in the nominative, vocative and accusative, and nowhere else. That is a great deal less to learn than a table implies, and it holds across every stem class in the language, not merely the *a*-stems where it is easiest to see.

[^perniola-24]: Perniola §24, p. 29. The same division underlies Ñāṇatusita's layout at Appendix A.1, where masculine and neuter share a table and the feminine is given its own.

### The *a*-stems

By far the largest class, and the one every other stem is read against. The masculine goes like *purisa* 'man', the neuter like *rūpa* 'form'.

| Nom. sg. | *puriso* | *rūpaṁ* |
| Voc. sg. | *purisa* | *rūpaṁ* |
| Acc. sg. | *purisaṁ* | *rūpaṁ* |
| Instr. sg. | *purisena* | *rūpena* |
| Abl. sg. | *purisā*, *-asmā*, *-amhā*, *-ato* | *rūpā*, *-asmā*, *-amhā*, *-ato* |
| Gen./Dat. sg. | *purisassa*, *-āya* (dat.) | *rūpassa*, *-āya* (dat.) |
| Loc. sg. | *purise*, *-asmiṁ*, *-amhi* | *rūpe*, *-asmiṁ*, *-amhi* |
| Nom./Voc. pl. | *purisā* | *rūpāni*, *rūpā* |
| Acc. pl. | *purise* | *rūpāni*, *rūpe* |
| Instr./Abl. pl. | *purisehi* | *rūpehi* |
| Gen./Dat. pl. | *purisānaṁ* | *rūpānaṁ* |
| Loc. pl. | *purisesu* | *rūpesu* |

Read the two columns against each other and Perniola's rule is visible: they differ in four rows and are identical in the other seven.

A caution about how to read that table, and about every paradigm table in this book and in every other grammar. A paradigm teaches endings, and its forms are only specimens of them. *Purisasmā* and *purisamhi* occur nowhere in the canon or its commentaries; they are found only in the grammarians' own texts, where somebody has done what the table above does and completed the paradigm by rule. The *endings* are real and common (*kāyasmā*, *kāyamhi* and *lokasmiṁ* are ordinary canonical Pāli, and *lokasmiṁ* alone occurs over four hundred times in the root texts), but nobody in the canon happens to have said *purisasmā*.[^specimen-forms]

This holds for every table below, and it is not a defect in them. A paradigm has to be complete to be a paradigm. It only misleads if it is read as a list of words the texts contain, and the rule for this book is accordingly narrow: where a specific form is asserted in the prose and not merely displayed in a table, it has been checked against the texts first. Appendix A.1 sidesteps the question altogether by printing bare endings and never a whole word.

[^specimen-forms]: Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, in four groups: *purisasmā* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 13) and *purisamhi* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 6), where 'later works' are the grammatical and chronicle literature outside the commentaries. Against these, *lokasmiṁ* (root texts 466, aṭṭhakathā 134, ṭīkā 10, later works 107). Perniola §25, pp. 30–31, prints the full *purisa* paradigm without distinguishing them, which is ordinary practice in a grammar and is not an error; the distinction only matters when a paradigm is mistaken for a word list.

- Nāhaṁ, bhikkhave, aññaṁ ekarūpampi samanupassāmi yaṁ evaṁ **purisassa** cittaṁ pariyādāya tiṭṭhati … (AN 1.1 — A i 1)
- Mendicants, I do not see a single sight that so occupies **a man's** mind …

- Idh'evam eso **lokasmiṁ**, mūlaṁ khaṇati attano. (Dhp 247 — Dhp p. 36)
- Right here **in this world** this man digs up his own root.[^idhevameso]

- Nibbindaṁ virajjati; **virāgā** vimuccati. **Vimuttasmiṁ** vimuttamiti ñāṇaṁ hoti. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)
- Being disillusioned, desire fades; **through the fading of desire** he is freed. **When he is freed**, the knowledge comes that he is freed.

[^idhevameso]: The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints the second word solid, *Idhevameso*, and it divides as *idha* + *evaṁ* + *eso*, the niggahīta of *evaṁ* becoming *m* before the vowel. This book divided it *idheva meso* until the Buddha Jayanti edition was consulted, which prints the line word-divided as *Idh'evam eso lokasmiṁ* in one of its two Dhammapada texts and reads *Idheva poso lokasmiṁ*, "this man", in the other, either way with a subject for *khaṇati* and no *me* in the line. DPD's *meso*, *me* + *eso* 'this is my', is a real sandhi form and belongs to the *na meso attā* passages, not here.

The last of those has two *a*-stem obliques with different functions in consecutive clauses: *virāgā* an ablative of cause, *vimuttasmiṁ* a locative, and both of them ordinary canonical forms, not paradigm fillers.

#### Why the endings look as they do

Four rules account for most of the pattern of that table, and Perniola sets them out where a reader can use them.[^perniola-25g] The stem is *purisa-* throughout, and:

- Before the nominative singular marker *-s*, the stem vowel and the marker together give *-o*: *purisa-s* > *puriso*.
- Before the nominative plural *-as*, they give *-ā*: *purisa-as* > *purisā*.
- The stem vowel **lengthens** before an ending beginning with *n-*: *purisa-naṁ* > *purisānaṁ*, *rūpa-ni* > *rūpāni*.
- The stem vowel **drops** before an ending beginning with *e-*: *purisa-ena* > *purisena*, *purisa-ehi* > *purisehi*, *purisa-esu* > *purisesu*.

None of this has to be applied consciously while reading. It is here because, once you have seen it, you stop experiencing *purisānaṁ* and *purisesu* as two unrelated forms and start hearing one stem under both, and that is the whole difficulty of the declension in miniature.

[^perniola-25g]: Perniola §25(g), p. 32, where the four are given as one list. The rules are stated as synchronic descriptions of the Pāli, not as historical derivations; for the history see Perniola chapter 13, *Evolution of the declensions*.

#### Where the *a*-stems vary

Three variants a reader will meet and should not be thrown by.

A nominative singular in *-e* instead of *-o* turns up occasionally in both masculine and neuter: *purise*, *manusse*, *jīvite*, *sukhe*, *dukkhe*.[^nom-e] A nominative plural in *-āse* appears in verse — *paṇḍitāse*, *gatāse*. And an ablative singular in *-to* is available to every stem in the language, not only this one; it is common enough to meet on its own, below.

[^nom-e]: Perniola §25(a), p. 31, who gives eleven instances. Ñāṇatusita marks the *-e* nominative as rare in both genders (Appendix A.1). Oberlies §30.1, p. 142, treats it as the Māgadhī nominative surviving in the Pāli of the canon, and so it is more frequent in some texts than in others.

### The *ā*-stems

The feminine counterpart, and the third of the three paradigms that carry the canon. It goes like *kaññā* 'girl'.

| Case | Fem. |
|---|---|
| Nom. sg. | *kaññā* |
| Voc. sg. | *kaññe* |
| Acc. sg. | *kaññaṁ* |
| Instr. sg. | *kaññāya* |
| Abl. sg. | *kaññāya*, *-āto* |
| Gen./Dat. sg. | *kaññāya* |
| Loc. sg. | *kaññāya*, *-āyaṁ* |
| Nom./Voc./Acc. pl. | *kaññā*, *kaññāyo* |
| Instr./Abl. pl. | *kaññāhi* |
| Gen./Dat. pl. | *kaññānaṁ* |
| Loc. pl. | *kaññāsu* |

What is striking here is a collapse, not a form: in the singular, *-āya* serves four cases (instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative), and *-āya* or *-āyaṁ* the locative besides. Five of the seven cases are one ending. A feminine *-ā* noun in an oblique case therefore shows almost nothing about which case it is in; only the sentence around it does. This is the sharpest instance in the nominal system of a general truth about reading Pāli, that an ending narrows the possibilities and the context decides among them.

- … pubbe ananussutesu **dhammesu** cakkhuṁ udapādi, ñāṇaṁ udapādi, **paññā** udapādi, **vijjā** udapādi, āloko udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)
- … regarding **teachings** not heard before, vision arose, knowledge arose, **wisdom** arose, **realisation** arose, light arose.

Two feminine *ā*-stems there, *paññā* and *vijjā*, both nominative singular and both recognisable as feminine by their ending alone, and nowhere else in this paradigm is the ending decisive.

### The *i*- and *u*-stems

These two behave so alike that they are best met together: whatever the *i*-stem does with *i*, the *u*-stem does with *u*. Masculine *aggi* 'fire' and *bhikkhu* 'monk'; neuter *akkhi* 'eye' and *cakkhu* 'eye'.

| Case | *i*- m. | *i*- n. | *u*- m. | *u*- n. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom./Voc. sg. | *aggi* | *akkhi*, *akkhiṁ* | *bhikkhu* | *cakkhu*, *cakkhuṁ* |
| Acc. sg. | *aggiṁ* | *akkhi*, *akkhiṁ* | *bhikkhuṁ* | *cakkhu*, *cakkhuṁ* |
| Instr. sg. | *agginā* | *akkhinā* | *bhikkhunā* | *cakkhunā* |
| Abl. sg. | *agginā*, *-ismā*, *-imhā* | *akkhinā*, *-ismā* | *bhikkhunā*, *-usmā*, *-umhā* | *cakkhunā*, *-usmā* |
| Gen./Dat. sg. | *aggino*, *aggissa* | *akkhino*, *akkhissa* | *bhikkhuno*, *bhikkhussa* | *cakkhuno*, *cakkhussa* |
| Loc. sg. | *aggismiṁ*, *-imhi*, *aggini* | *akkhismiṁ*, *akkhini* | *bhikkhusmiṁ*, *-umhi* | *cakkhusmiṁ*, *cakkhumhi* |
| Nom./Voc. pl. | *aggī*, *aggayo* | *akkhīni*, *akkhī* | *bhikkhū*, *bhikkhavo* | *cakkhūni*, *cakkhū* |
| Acc. pl. | *aggī*, *aggayo* | *akkhīni*, *akkhī* | *bhikkhū*, *bhikkhavo* | *cakkhūni*, *cakkhū* |
| Instr./Abl. pl. | *aggīhi* | *akkhīhi* | *bhikkhūhi* | *cakkhūhi* |
| Gen./Dat. pl. | *aggīnaṁ* | *akkhīnaṁ* | *bhikkhūnaṁ* | *cakkhūnaṁ* |
| Loc. pl. | *aggīsu* | *akkhīsu* | *bhikkhūsu* | *cakkhūsu* |

Two observations justify the table. The plural lengthens the stem vowel: *aggi-hi* > *aggīhi*, *bhikkhu-su* > *bhikkhūsu*. The specimen is *aggi* because it declines cleanly, not because the canon has occasion to speak of fires in the instrumental plural; the same ending on commoner stems gives *isīhi* 'by the seers' and *gahapatīhi* 'by the householders'. And in the nominative and accusative plural the vowel is strengthened, not lengthened: *aggi-o* becomes *aggayo* by way of *agge-o*, and *bhikkhu-o* becomes *bhikkhavo* by way of *bhikkho-o*.[^guna-plural] That explains *bhikkhavo*, and the same operation produces the vocative *bhikkhave*, which a reader meets in the first line of most suttas in the canon.

[^guna-plural]: Perniola §26(a), p. 33, who derives *aggi-o* > *agge-o* > *aggayo* and *taru-o* > *taro-o* > *taravo*. On the two vocative plurals, Perniola §26(g), p. 34, notes that *bhikkhu* has both *bhikkhavo* and *bhikkhave*, "the latter form is more common", decisively so: *bhikkhave* occurs 26,716 times in the root texts against *bhikkhavo*'s 180.

- Te taṁ rattiṁ tikkhattuṁ paccuṭṭhāya pañjalikā **aggiṁ** namassanti. (AN 10.119 — A v 234)
- Getting up three times in the night, with palms joined, they worship **the fire**.

- Idha pana, upavāṇa, **bhikkhu cakkhunā** rūpaṁ disvā rūpappaṭisaṁvedī ca hoti. (SN 35.70 — S iv 41)
- Here, Upavāṇa, **a mendicant**, having seen a form **with the eye**, experiences the form.

The second of those shows the two stems side by side doing different work in one clause: *bhikkhu* is a masculine *u*-stem in the nominative, subject of the sentence, and *cakkhunā* a neuter *u*-stem in the instrumental, the means by which the seeing is done.

### Feminine *ī*- and *ū*-stems

The feminines in *-ī* and *-ū* (*nadī* 'river', *vadhū* 'daughter-in-law') differ from the feminines in *-i* and *-u* in the nominative singular and almost nowhere else.[^fem-i-ii] The oblique singular is *nadiyā*, *vadhuyā*, and the plural *nadiyo*, *vadhuyo*, *nadīhi*, *vadhūhi*, *nadīnaṁ*, *nadīsu*.

Because the difference is so slight, a good many words are found in both shapes: *hiri* beside *hirī* 'moral shame', *itthi* beside *itthī* 'woman'. Nothing hangs on which a manuscript prints.

[^fem-i-ii]: Perniola §30, p. 36, whose *jāti* / *nadī* / *dhenu* / *vadhū* paradigm shows the four columns diverging only in the nominative and vocative singular. Oberlies §36, pp. 160–164, treats *ī/ū*-inflexion as one section for the same reason, and his *Rem.* to §36.8, p. 164, takes up *itthī-*, *sirī-* and *hirī-* specifically as words that waver.

The really rare stems here are the two with a *u*: the **feminine in *-ū*** and its short-vowel partner in ***-u***. Perniola's paradigm supplies a specimen of each (*vadhū* 'daughter-in-law', *dhenu* 'cow'), and the root texts barely use either.[^fem-i-ii] *Vadhū* reaches them only inside the compound *kulavadhū*; *dhenu* reaches them as *dhenuṁ* and *dhenuyo* and scarcely another case. The clearest instance of a *-ū* stem in the early texts is the river-name *Sarabhū*, not a common noun at all. Both paradigms are more of a curiosity than a tool.

### The odd one: *go*

One stem in the language ends in *-o*: *go* 'cow, ox'. In the singular it declines much like *purisa* apart from the nominative, and in the plural the sequence *-ava-* has contracted, giving *gāvo*, *gohi*, *gunnaṁ*, *gosu*.

It gets a paragraph because Oberlies explains where its stranger forms came from, and the explanation demonstrates a process at work throughout the nominal system. A new stem *gava-* was built out of the instrumental *gavā*, which had come to be understood as an ablative; and another stem, *goṇa-*, was extracted from the newly-created feminine *goṇī-*.[^go-oberlies] Forms are not only inherited. They are also **back-formed**, reverse-engineered by speakers from a form they had in front of them, and a paradigm that looks chaotic is often several such rebuildings laid on top of one another.

[^go-oberlies]: Oberlies §37.2–3, p. 165. He gives *gāvuṁ* at Vin I 150,9 as itself a transformation of *gavaṁ* under the influence of the neighbouring words, citing Saddanīti V 1358. Of the two genitive plurals Perniola §28, p. 35, prints, *gunnaṁ* is canonical, with 11 occurrences in the root texts, and *gonaṁ* is found only in the grammarians' texts.

### Consonant stems

Old Indic had a large and productive consonant declension. Pāli has the wreckage of it, and of the sources consulted here only Oberlies says so outright:

> Only traces of (a) the root nouns and (b) the consonant stems without vowel alternation — mainly neuter nouns in *-as*, *-is* and *-us*, and very few masc./fem. *as*-nouns — have survived.[^ob-38-1]

*Traces*, and the direction of travel is towards the *a*-stems. Several consonant stems have a set of forms built the old way and a second set built as though the word were an ordinary *a*-stem: beside the accusative singular *rājānaṁ* stands *rājaṁ*, and beside *attānaṁ* stands *attaṁ*.[^transfer] Expect the consonant stems to be tidy and you will be puzzled; expect them to be halfway through a migration and you will not.

How far the migration has gone can be measured instead of assumed: less far than the grammars imply. Perniola gives six transferred forms for these two words, and only the two accusatives above are found in the canon at all; the genitives *rājassa* and *attassa* and the plurals *rājehi* and *attehi* are constructions.[^transfer-measured] The transfer is real, and in the canon it is still mostly confined to the accusative.

[^ob-38-1]: Oberlies §38.1, p. 165. His cross-reference is to §28.2, on the transfer of stems, which is the mechanism.

[^transfer]: Perniola §36(a), p. 42, who gives the doubled forms for *rājan* and *attan* and calls them declension "like the stems in *-a*". Oberlies §28.2, pp. 131–136, gathers the same phenomenon across the whole nominal system under *transfer of stems*.

[^transfer-measured]: Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. Canonical: *rājaṁ* 3 in the root texts, *attaṁ* likewise present. Not canonical: *rājassa* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 56) and *rājasmā* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 2), both grammarians' texts only; *attassa* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 3, ṭīkā 2, later works 14), commentary only; *rājehi* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 12), *attehi* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 6) and *attanehi* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 15), grammarians' texts only. Perniola's list (§36(a), p. 42) describes what the declension permits, a different claim from what the canon contains, and he does not distinguish them.

#### Stems in *-(n)t*

Words in *-(n)t* are adjectives or present participles by origin, though several of the commonest are used as nouns. This class has **two stems, a strong one in *-ant* and a weak one in *-at***, and it uses both: *bhagavant-* gives *bhagavanto* and *bhagavatā*, *sīlavant-* gives *sīlavanto* and *sīlavato*. The nominative singular is shorter than either, ending in a bare *-ā*.

| Case | Sg. | Pl. |
|---|---|---|
| Nom. | *bhagavā* | *bhagavanto* |
| Voc. | *bhagavā*, *bhagava* | *bhagavanto* |
| Acc. | *bhagavantaṁ* | *bhagavante* |
| Instr./Abl. | *bhagavatā*, *bhagavantena* | *bhagavantehi* |
| Gen./Dat. | *bhagavato* | *bhagavataṁ*, *bhagavantānaṁ* |
| Loc. | *bhagavati* | *bhagavantesu* |

- … **bhagavati** pasādo. (SN 55.30 — S v 390)
- … confidence **in the Blessed One**.

- Mahantasmiṁ kho pana lokasannivāse santi samaṇabrāhmaṇā **iddhimanto** dibbacakkhukā paracittaviduno. (AN 3.40 — A i 148)
- In this vast human society there are ascetics and brahmins **with psychic power**, with clairvoyance, who know the minds of others.

The lower right of that table is thinner than it looks, and no case in the chapter more clearly shows a paradigm completed by rule. The *-(n)t* class largely has no oblique plural at all. Perniola says so directly: there are no forms for the instrumental, ablative or locative plural except *sabbhi*, the instrumental and ablative plural of *sant* 'good, true'. The texts agree with him (*bhagavantehi* and *bhagavantesu* occur only in the grammarians' texts, and *bhagavantena* nowhere at all), while *sabbhi* is thoroughly canonical, with 36 occurrences in the root texts.[^sabbhi] A reader will meet *sabbhi* in verse long before meeting the paradigm it belongs to.

[^sabbhi]: Perniola §34, p. 40: "There are no forms for the instrumental, ablative and locative plural, with the exception of *sabbhi* which is the instrumental and ablative plural of *sant* 'good'." Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana: *sabbhi* (root texts 36, aṭṭhakathā 38, ṭīkā 7, later works 42); *bhagavantehi* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 4) and *bhagavantesu* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 1), grammarians' texts only; *bhagavantena* 0 throughout. The oblique plural cells printed above are therefore the *a*-stem pattern applied by rule, and are given because a reader needs somewhere to put an ending, not because the texts supply them.

#### Stems in *-in*

All words in *-in* are adjectives by origin (*ātāpin* 'ardent', *sukhin* 'happy', *saññin* 'perceiving'), though some, like *gihin* 'householder', have settled into nouns. The stem is a **possessive suffix**: *-in* added to a noun makes an adjective meaning 'having that thing', so *ātāpin* is 'having ardour' and *gihin* 'having a house'. Two related suffixes, *-min* and *-vin*, serve the same purpose: *sassatasamin*, *medhāvin* 'having wisdom, wise'.[^in-suffix]

| Case | Sg. | Pl. |
|---|---|---|
| Nom. | *hatthī* | *hatthino*, *hatthī* |
| Voc. | *hatthī* | *hatthino* |
| Acc. | *hatthinaṁ* | *hatthino*, *hatthī* |
| Instr./Abl. | *hatthinā* | *hatthīhi* |
| Gen./Dat. | *hatthino* | *hatthīnaṁ* |
| Loc. | *hatthini* | *hatthīsu* |

The nominative singular *-ī* is the point of difficulty, because it looks like a feminine *ī*-stem and is not one. *Hatthī* 'elephant' is masculine; so is *bhikkhu*'s near-neighbour *dhammacārī* 'one who lives by the Dhamma'.

- **Ātāpino** saṁvegino bhavātha. (Dhp 144 — Dhp p. 21)
- Be **ardent** and full of urgency.

[^in-suffix]: Oberlies treats all three together as *i(n)-/mi(n)-/vi(n)-*inflexion at §34, so they are given together here, not as three unrelated stem types. Perniola §35, p. 42, has only *-in*.

#### Stems in *-an*

A small class, and two of its members are among the most frequent words in the canon: *rājan* 'king' and *attan* 'self'.

| Case | *rājan* sg. | *rājan* pl. | *attan* sg. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nom. | *rājā* | *rājāno* | *attā* |
| Voc. | *rāja* | *rājāno* | *atta* |
| Acc. | *rājānaṁ* | *rājāno* | *attānaṁ* |
| Instr./Abl. | *raññā*, *rājinā* | *rājūhi* | *attanā* |
| Gen./Dat. | *rañño*, *rājino* | *raññaṁ*, *rājūnaṁ* | *attano* |
| Loc. | *raññe*[^rajini] | *rājūsu* | *attani* |

The *raññā* / *rañño* forms are the ones to fix in memory, because they do not look as though they belong to *rājā* at all and they are everywhere.

[^rajini]: The table gives *raññe*, not *rājini*, for the locative singular. *Rājini* is not canonical (none in the root texts, 7 in the commentaries and 33 in the grammarians' texts), whereas *raññe* has 16 in the root texts and is the locative a reader will meet. Both are Ñāṇatusita's, whose *an*-stem locative singular gives four endings, *-ni*, *-ismiṁ*, *-imhi* and *-ne*; on this word only the last of them is Pāli anyone wrote. The *-in* forms of *rājan* are real elsewhere in the paradigm (*rājino* has 33 root-text occurrences and *rājinā* one), so the stem transfer is real and it is the locative specifically that did not take.

- **Attā** hi **attano** nātho. (Dhp 160 — Dhp p. 24)
- **Oneself** is indeed **one's own** protector.

Others of this class are *brahman* 'Brahmā', *yuvan* 'youth', *addhan* 'road', *puman* 'man', and the neuters *kamman* 'action' and *muddhan* 'head'.[^an-others] Most of these have gone over to the *a*-declension almost completely (*kamma*, *kammaṁ*, *kammena*), and their *-an* forms survive chiefly in verse and in compounds.

[^an-others]: Perniola §36(e), p. 43, who lists them and prints the few cases each is actually found in. His list is the fullest of any source read for this chapter; Oberlies §39, pp. 168–171, covers the same ground historically.

#### Stems in *-ar*

The *-ar* stems are two groups wearing one paradigm, and Perniola makes a distinction here that no other source consulted makes.

**Agent nouns** (*satthar* 'teacher', *dātar* 'giver', *netar* 'leader', *sotar* 'hearer') are formed from verbs and mean 'the one who does this'. **Relationship nouns** (*pitar* 'father', *mātar* 'mother', *bhātar* 'brother', *dhītar* 'daughter') are inherited kinship terms. They decline alike except in the accusative singular and the nominative, vocative and accusative plural, where the agent nouns have a long stem vowel and the relationship nouns a short one: *satthāraṁ*, *satthāro* against *pitaraṁ*, *pitaro*, *mātaraṁ*, *mātaro*.[^ar-perniola]

| Case | *satthar* | *pitar* | *mātar* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nom./Voc. sg. | *satthā* | *pitā* | *mātā* |
| Acc. sg. | *satthāraṁ* | *pitaraṁ* | *mātaraṁ* |
| Instr./Abl. sg. | *satthārā* | *pitarā* | *mātarā* |
| Gen./Dat. sg. | *satthu* | *pitu* | *mātu* |
| Loc. sg. | *satthari* | *pitari* | *mātari* |
| Nom./Voc./Acc. pl. | *satthāro* | *pitaro* | *mātaro* |
| Instr./Abl. pl. | *satthūhi* | *pitūhi* | *mātūhi* |
| Gen./Dat. pl. | *satthūnaṁ* | *pitūnaṁ* | *mātūnaṁ* |
| Loc. pl. | *satthūsu* | *pitūsu* | *mātūsu* |

- Punappunaṁ vaccho upeti **mātaraṁ**. (SN 7.12 — S i 174)
- Again and again the calf goes to **its mother**.

- … anuttaro purisadammasārathi **satthā** devamanussānaṁ. (DN 2 — D i 49)
- … the unsurpassed trainer of those who can be trained, **teacher** of gods and humans.

[^ar-perniola]: Perniola §37(b), p. 44. His §37(c), p. 45, adds that the masculine stems form the instrumental and genitive singular on the *u*-stem pattern (*satthunā*, *satthuno*, *pitunā*, *pituno*), and that the feminine *mātar* forms the locative singular that way too, *mātuyā*, *mātuyaṁ*. *Dhītar* 'daughter' is often declined instead as an *ā*-stem: *dhītā*, *dhītaṁ*, *dhītāya*.

#### Stems in *-as*, *-is* and *-us*

A dozen or so nouns end in *-as*: *manas* 'mind', *cetas* 'mind, heart', *tapas* 'ardour', *tejas* 'fire, majesty', *yasas* 'fame', *vacas* 'speech', *siras* 'head', *ayas* 'iron', *vayas* 'age', *uras* 'breast', *jaras* 'old age'. Beside the *-as* stems stand a very few in *-is* and *-us*.

Their distinguishing forms are a nominative and accusative in *-o*, and an oblique singular in *-asā*, *-aso*, *-asi*:

| Case | Sg. |
|---|---|
| Nom./Acc. | *mano* |
| Instr./Abl. | *manasā* |
| Gen./Dat. | *manaso* |
| Loc. | *manasi* |

Every one of those is common. What is not stable is anything else about the class.

These nouns are in the middle of changing declension, and their gender is disputed because of it. Alongside the forms above, the same words are declined throughout as ordinary neuter *a*-stems: *manaṁ*, *manena*, *manasmā*, *manassa*, *manasmiṁ*, *manāni*, *manehi*, *manānaṁ*, *manesu*. Oberlies gives the phenomenon its own section, *transfer of °as- into a-stems*, and it underlies the sources' disagreement about gender. Perniola and Ñāṇatusita call the class neuter, which is what it was; the Digital Pāli Dictionary calls *manas*, *cetas* and *tapas* masculine, as a word whose nominative is *mano* looks once it has arrived in the *a*-declension. Oberlies's formula ("mainly neuter nouns in *-as*, *-is* and *-us*, and very few masc./fem. *as*-nouns") is the accurate one, and it is accurate partly because it declines to be tidy.[^as-gender]

For a reader, the practical consequence is small: treat these words as neuter, expect masculine agreement occasionally, and do not be surprised by either.

- **Manasā** ce paduṭṭhena, bhāsati vā karoti vā. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)
- If **with a corrupted mind** one speaks or acts.

- Mātā yathā niyaṁ puttam **āyusā** ekaputtamanurakkhe. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)
- As a mother would guard her own son, her only son, **with her life**.

[^as-gender]: Oberlies §38.1, pp. 165–166, for the formula; his §38.2, pp. 166–167, for the mechanism. Perniola §33, pp. 39–40: "There are a few stems in *-s* which are neuter in gender and are declined only in the singular", with §33(a) noting that "these stems are often declined like neuter stems in *-a*" and §33(c) allowing one masculine, *candimas* 'moon', nominative *candimā*. DPD, s.v. *manas*, *cetas*, *tapas*, classes all three as masculine, "mano group". Ñāṇatusita marks his *as*- and *us*-columns neuter (Appendix A.1).

The second example above is a small lesson in reading a digital edition. Searched for as a word, *āyusā* finds nothing in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, which would ordinarily suggest a form built by grammarians, not used by anyone. It is nothing of the kind: the edition prints the line with the words run together as *puttamāyusā*, so a search for the word cannot see it, and the form sits in one of the best-known passages in the canon.[^ayusa-search]

The word *āyu* 'life, lifespan' is itself the clearest case of the drift this section describes. It is historically a *-us* stem, and Oberlies flags its gender as an open question in the scholarship; in the canon it behaves as a neuter *u*-stem (*āyunā*, *āyuno*), with the older *āyusā* surviving in verse.[^ayu]

[^ayusa-search]: *Āyusā* has 0 root-text occurrences as a word. The line is found only by looking for the string inside a longer one: Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26 prints *Mātā yathā niyaṁ puttamāyusā ekaputtamanurakkhe*. A word that cannot be found has to be looked for again as part of a longer word before anyone concludes it does not exist, and this shows why.

[^ayu]: Oberlies §38.1 n. 5, p. 166, referring the gender question to Caillat, *IF* 74 (1969) 224. DPD makes *āyu* neuter, from *ā* + √*i* > *ay* + *u*. In the root texts *āyunā* occurs 43 times and *āyuno* 23, against one occurrence of *āyusā*.

#### Root stems

Finally, and mostly for recognition, not use: a handful of words in the canon are **root nouns**, in which the case ending was added directly to a verbal root with no stem-forming vowel between. Pāli has fragments of these and no complete paradigm.

What survives is a scattering of individual forms, chiefly in verse: instrumental *vācā* 'by speech' and *padā* 'with the foot', locative *parisati* 'in the assembly', nominative plural *āpo* 'waters', genitive plural *dvipadaṁ … catuppadaṁ* 'of two-footed and four-footed beings'.[^root-nouns] Met in isolation these look like errors, or like forms of some other word. They are neither, and knowing the class exists is enough.

[^root-nouns]: Oberlies §38.1, pp. 165–166, who gives each with its reference: *diso-disaṁ* (Ja III 459,22\*), *padā*, *vācā* (Sn 130, 232), *parisati* (Ja V 61,24\*), *saṁsati* (M I 68,8), *āpo* (Sn 307), *dvipadaṁ … catuppadaṁ* (S I 6,22\*/24\*). Perniola §32, p. 38, calls them "a few remnants of the Sanskrit root stems declensions" and gives an overlapping list. Oberlies also gives the locative *divi* 'in heaven', citing D II 206,8, though under the diphthong stems (§37.1, p. 165), not among the root nouns; it is left out of the list above because it does not occur as a word in the root texts of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition, and the longer words that contain the string (*ādivisodhanā*, *nandivisālo*) are unrelated. An edition difference is the likeliest explanation and the form is not in doubt, but this book cites what it can check.

### The ablative in *-to*

One ending cuts across every class in the chapter, and it is easier to meet once than five times. **Any noun can form an ablative singular in *-to***, whatever its stem: *dūrato* 'from afar', *gharato* 'from the house', *gāmato* 'from the village', *dukkhato* 'from suffering', *rūpato* 'from form', *jātito* 'by birth', *kammato* 'by action', *aggito* 'from fire', *cakkhuto* 'from the eye'. The last two are the point of the suffix: an *i*-stem and a *u*-stem forming an ablative with the same ending an *a*-stem uses, so it is met here as one fact, not as one fact per declension.[^to-suffix]

There is a companion to it in *-so*, no longer really a case ending at all but a maker of adverbs: *yoniso* 'thoroughly, from the root', *dhātuso* 'according to nature', *hetuso* 'by way of cause', *mattaso* 'in moderation'. Anyone who has met *yoniso manasikāra* 'proper attention' before meeting any grammar has already met this suffix.

[^to-suffix]: Oberlies gathers it at §28.4 as a general feature of the case system, not a fact about any one declension, and so it is given once here. Perniola states it separately at §25(f), §26(d), §29(b) and §30(c) (pp. 31, 33, 36 and 37), once per stem class. Two of the forms he gives do not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and have been left out above: *nāvato* 'from the boat' occurs nowhere in it, and neither does *ādiso* 'from the beginning'; the longer words that contain the latter, *yādiso* and *tādiso*, are unrelated. *Bārāṇasito* 'from Benares' is real but commentarial, with 48 occurrences in the aṭṭhakathā and none in the root texts.
