## Pronouns

### What a pronoun is

A **pronoun** is a word that stands in the place of a noun: *he*, *this*, *who*, *anyone*. Pāli has them in quantity, and one fact about the language shapes everything in this chapter: a Pāli verb already carries its own person, so a pronoun subject is never structurally required. *Gacchāmi* is a complete sentence, "I go". When *ahaṁ* 'I' turns up in front of it, then, it does more than fill a gap. It emphasises, or contrasts, or picks one referent out of several, and reading Pāli well means asking which.

The indigenous grammarians call the whole class *sabbanāma*, 'all-name', and their inventory is a closed list of twenty-seven words.[^sabbanama-list] It is not the list a European grammar would draw up. It counts the numerals *eka* 'one', *ubha* 'both', *dvi* 'two', *ti* 'three' and *catu* 'four' among the pronouns, and the directional adjectives *dakkhiṇa* 'southern, right', *uttara* 'northern, upper' and *adhara* 'lower' as well. That matters for the section on **pronominal adjectives** below, because the awkward category it has to describe is the residue: the words the tradition counts as pronouns and a European description does not.

[^sabbanama-list]: *Sabba, katara, katama, ubhaya, itara, añña, aññatara, aññatama, pubba, para, apara, dakkhiṇa, uttara, adhara, ya, ta, eta, ima, amu, kiṁ, eka, ubha, dvi, ti, catu, tumha, amha*: Saddanīti I 150, reported by Thānuttamo, *Māgadhabhāsā*, p. 55, with Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students* §2.2.

Two general facts hold across the whole class. Pronouns decline for case and number like nouns, and the demonstratives and interrogatives decline for gender as well; but no pronoun has a vocative, so the tables below run six cases and not seven. And a great many of them share one set of endings, the *pronominal declension*, so the *ta-*, *eta-*, *ya-* and *ka-* paradigms will soon start to look repetitive. That is a mercy, not a coincidence: learn one and you have most of four.

### Personal pronouns

Strictly, Pāli has personal pronouns for two persons only. The first person is built on the stem *amha-* and the second on *tumha-*; for the third it simply uses the demonstrative *ta(d)* 'that', so that "he", "she", "it" and "that one" are one and the same word. **Demonstrative pronouns** below is where that word is set out, and so the two tables here are complete as they stand, not missing a third.

Neither the first nor the second person inflects for gender. In Pāli as in English, a man and a woman alike say *ahaṁ*.

Both tables give the forms a reader will meet. Each pronoun has a further tail of rare and metrical variants (*vayaṁ* and *amhā* for *mayaṁ*, *asmākaṁ* for *amhākaṁ*, *tyaṁ* for *taṁ*), and those are set out in Appendix A.2, which declines both pronouns in full.

Two examples, with the pronoun in bold and its English counterpart likewise. Layer two gives each word its gloss, and parses the pronouns.

- **So maṁ** sacce nivesayi. (SN 5.6 — S i 132)
- *so* he (nom. sg. m.) · *maṁ* me (1st acc. sg.) · *sacce* in the truth · *nivesayi* settled
- **He** settled **me** in the truth.

- **Tvaṁ**, samma medakathālike, **mamaṁ** rakkha, **ahaṁ** **taṁ** rakkhissāmi. (SN 47.19 — S v 168)
- *tvaṁ* you (2nd nom. sg.) · *samma* dear · *medakathālike* Medakathālikā (voc.) · *mamaṁ* me (1st acc. sg.) · *rakkha* protect! · *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *taṁ* you (2nd acc. sg.) · *rakkhissāmi* I shall protect
- "**You** protect **me**, dear Medakathālikā, and **I** shall protect **you**."

The second of those holds the chapter's first real trap. *Taṁ* is the second person accusative singular, "you", and it is also the third person accusative singular, "him, her, it".[^tam-homonym] Both are common. The form itself does not distinguish them, and here the paired *ahaṁ* settles it: the acrobat is speaking to his assistant, so the person he will protect is the one he is talking to. This is the first instance of a habit this chapter will insist on repeatedly. A Pāli pronoun is identified, not recognised.

[^tam-homonym]: DPD keeps the two apart as separate headwords: *taṁ* "him; her; that person", masc./fem./nt. acc. sg. of *ta*; and *taṁ* "you (object)", 2nd acc. sg. of *tvaṁ*, from *tvaṁ* + *aṁ*. Warder's Lesson 5 tables (p. 29) print *ta(d)* as the stem of both, and the collision comes from there.

#### The enclitics *me*, *te*, *no*, *vo*

Four forms in the tables above are **enclitics**: short, unstressed forms that lean on the word before them. *Me* and *te* serve the singular, *no* and *vo* the plural, and they behave differently from the full forms in two ways that matter for reading.

The first is positional and absolute: an enclitic is never the first word of its sentence.[^enclitic-position] So a sentence-initial *no* is not "us" at all but the negative particle *no* 'not', and *te* at the head of a clause is the nominative plural of *ta(d)*, "they". Position alone resolves both.

[^enclitic-position]: Thānuttamo, *Māgadhabhāsā*, p. 56, citing Duroiselle's 1997 re-edition. In the 1921 edition the rule is stated of *me* and *no* in the remarks that close §289, p. 119.

The second is that their case has spread. In Sanskrit *me* and *te* are dative and genitive only, and *no* and *vo* accusative, dative and genitive; Pāli extends all four beyond that range, into the instrumental and elsewhere, so an enclitic is often ambiguous and the context has to decide.[^enclitic-case] The tables above already show this (*me* stands in three cases and *no* in four), and it is a fact about the language, not a defect in the paradigm.

[^enclitic-case]: Wijesekera, *Syntax of the Cases in the Pāli Nikāyas* §15, who gives *te … karissāmi* (M I 377) with *te* accusative and notes a passage at D III 48 where *me* "can be either the acc. or the inst."

- Evaṁ **me** sutaṁ. (DN 1 — D i 1)
- *evaṁ* thus · *me* by me (1st instr. sg.) · *sutaṁ* heard
- Thus have **I** heard.

- Yā sā, mahārāja, sundarī paribbājikā; **sā** **no** na dissati. (Ud 4.8 — Ud p. 44)
- *yā sā* she who · *mahārāja* great king · *sundarī* Sundarī · *paribbājikā* female wanderer · *sā* she (nom. sg. f.) · *no* by us (1st instr. pl.) · *na* not · *dissati* is seen
- "Great king, the wanderer Sundarī — **she** is not to be seen **by us**."

- Ovadatu **no** bhavaṁ gotamo … yaṁ **amhākaṁ** assa dīgharattaṁ hitāya sukhāya. (AN 3.51 — A i 155)
- *ovadatu* let him advise · *no* us (1st acc. pl.) · *bhavaṁ* the worthy · *gotamo* Gotama · *yaṁ* which · *amhākaṁ* for us (1st gen. pl.) · *assa* would be · *dīgharattaṁ* for a long time · *hitāya* for the welfare · *sukhāya* for the happiness
- "Let the worthy Gotama advise **us** … which would be for **our** lasting welfare and happiness."

Three separate things in that last one are the business of this chapter. *No* is accusative here, the object of *ovadatu*, where in the Udāna passage above it was instrumental: the case spread, in two adjacent examples. *Amhākaṁ* is the ordinary way Pāli says "our", which the possessive section below takes up. And *bhavaṁ* 'the worthy one, sir' is a polite substitute for "you" that takes a **third**-person verb: *ovadatu* is third singular, not second.[^bhavam-third-person] Two brahmins are addressing the Buddha with the deference the situation calls for, and Pāli marks that deference grammatically.

[^bhavam-third-person]: Duroiselle §333, who classes *bhavaṁ* among the pronouns: "a very respectful term of address used for the Second Pers Pronoun, the verb is put in Third Person". DPD agrees on the form (*bhavaṁ*, masculine nominative singular of *bhavant*, "master; sir; you"), and its own headword example, *kim'etaṁ bhavaṁ gotamo āha* (Ambaṭṭha Sutta), shows the same third-person agreement. His §§334–335 add *ayya* 'lord', used chiefly in addressing monastics and often paired with *bhante*, and the indeclinable *āvuso* 'friend', used mostly by senior monks to junior ones.

The same courtesy works through number as well as through person. A speaker may use the first person **plural** of himself, and the second person plural of one person addressed, where English would use the singular. The distinction is the one French makes with *tu* and *vous*, or German with *du* and *Sie*, and it tracks social position in the same way.[^honorific-plural] The second singular, correspondingly, is for intimates and juniors.

[^honorific-plural]: Collins, *A Pali Grammar for Students* p. 65: "The first person plural is likely to replace the first person singular, for reasons of politeness, especially but not only when speaking to someone in a superior position. The second singular is used for intimates and inferiors, as with French *tu* and German *du*." He notes that the same social hierarchy governs the choice of imperative forms.

> **The tradition on *evaṁ me sutaṁ*.** The formula that opens every sutta is the commonest sentence in the canon, and its *me* is not settled. Buddhaghosa takes it both ways. Glossing the phrase, he distinguishes three senses in which *me* is met: instrumental (*gāthābhigītaṁ me abhojaneyyaṁ* = *mayā*), dative (*sādhu me bhante Bhagavā … desetu* = *mayhaṁ*), genitive (*dhammadāyādā me bhikkhave bhavatha* = *mama*). Then he declines to choose here: *idha pana "mayā sutan"ti "mama sutan"ti ca atthadvaye yujjati*, "but in this place both 'heard by me' and 'my hearing' fit."[^evam-me-sutam] So "Thus have I heard" renders an instrumental the tradition itself allows to be a genitive of the agent, and to take the instrumental as fixed is to take a side the commentary does not.

[^evam-me-sutam]: Sumaṅgalavilāsinī on DN 1, Sv I 28; the same gloss opens the Papañcasūdanī on MN (Ps I 4) and the Sāratthappakāsinī on SN (Spk I 6). Reported by Wijesekera, *Syntax of the Cases in the Pāli Nikāyas* §15, and verified in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana commentarial text.

### Demonstrative pronouns

A demonstrative points at something instead of naming it. English has four (*this*, *that*, *these*, *those*), used in two positions: standing alone, "did you hear **that**?", and in front of a noun, "I see **this** man". English grammar calls the first a demonstrative pronoun and the second a demonstrative determiner. Pāli makes no formal difference between the two positions, so this book calls both pronouns.

What English does distinguish is distance: *that* and *those* for what is far from the speaker, **distal**; *this* and *these* for what is near, **proximal**. Pāli draws the line more finely. It has three demonstrative stems where English has two (*ta(d)* 'that', *eta(d)* and *ayaṁ/ima-* 'this', and *asu/amu-* 'that one over there'), and they are separated by distance and also by whether the thing spoken of is present to the hearer at all.

#### Distal pronouns

The distal ("that/those") pronouns are built on the stem *ta(d)*, and they are the same words as the third person personal pronouns. *So* is both "he" and "that man"; *taṁ* is both "it" and "that thing". There is accordingly no real boundary between personal and demonstrative in Pāli beyond the first two persons, and it does no harm to think of the third person as a demonstrative that has been put to use, much as English *it* is classed as a personal pronoun and yet points at things.

This is the commonest paradigm in the language, and the one to know cold.

|            | sg. m.                  | sg. n.                  | sg. f.                    | pl. m.        | pl. n.        | pl. f.            |
|------------|-------------------------|-------------------------|---------------------------|---------------|---------------|-------------------|
| nom.       | *so*, *sa*              | *taṁ*, *tad-*           | *sā*                      | *te*          | *tāni*        | *tā*, *tāyo*      |
| acc.       | *taṁ*                   | *taṁ*, *tad-*           | *taṁ*                     | *te*          | *tāni*        | *tā*, *tāyo*      |
| inst.      | *tena*                  | *tena*                  | *tāya*                    | *tehi*        | *tehi*        | *tāhi*            |
| abl.       | *tasmā*, *tamhā*, *tato*| *tasmā*, *tamhā*, *tato*| *tāya*                    | *tehi*        | *tehi*        | *tāhi*            |
| gen./dat.  | *tassa*                 | *tassa*                 | *tassā*, *tissā*          | *tesaṁ*       | *tesaṁ*       | *tāsaṁ*           |
| loc.       | *tasmiṁ*, *tamhi*       | *tasmiṁ*, *tamhi*       | *tassaṁ*, *tissaṁ*        | *tesu*        | *tesu*        | *tāsu*            |

Four points in that table deserve a second look, and a reader will meet the last of them first.

*Sa* stands beside *so* in the masculine nominative singular, and the two are not evenly matched. Geiger counted them: in the Sutta Nipāta *sa* occurs forty times against *so*'s hundred and twenty-four, and in the first five hundred verses of the Theragāthā four times against thirty-seven, twice of those in the set phrase *sa ve*.[^sa-so-frequency] *Sa* then becomes rare. It is a verse form, in short, and prose has settled on *so*.

[^sa-so-frequency]: Geiger §105, note 1 (p. 100): the notes to the *so/sā/taṁ* paradigm, not §106, which begins at the foot of the same page. In Sanskrit *sa* was admitted only before consonants; Pāli lost that restriction, and *so* became the dominant form (Oberlies §42(2), p. 185).

*Te* is both nominative and accusative plural, which looks like carelessness and is not. The masculine nominative *te* was extended to the accusative on the model of the feminine, where *tā* had always done both jobs.[^te-nom-acc] In practice *te* alone never shows whether it is subject or object; the verb and the rest of the clause do.

[^te-nom-acc]: Oberlies §42(2), p. 185: "After the model of the fem. (*tā*) the nom. masc. *te* was used *also* for the acc."

Beside these there is *se*, an eastern form standing for both *so* and *taṁ*, and it would be a curiosity except that it survives inside two words every reader of the canon meets constantly. *Seyyathā* 'just as' is *se* + *yathā*; and *seyyathidaṁ* 'namely, as follows' is *se* + *yathā* + *idaṁ*, literally "that is like this".[^seyyatha] So the formula that introduces every list in the suttas has a demonstrative pronoun buried in its first syllable.

[^seyyatha]: Oberlies §42(2), p. 185, citing Th 412 for the variant *sayathā* and deriving both from AV (Śaunaka) 17.1.20–21 *sá yáthā*; Perniola §41(d), p. 55; Geiger §105, note 2 with its footnote 3 (p. 100), who rejects Pischel's rival derivation of *se* from Vedic *sed* as unconvincing. DPD analyses *seyyathidaṁ* as the sandhi *se* + *yathā* + *idaṁ*.

Last, and most usefully: several oblique forms of *ta(d)* have hardened into adverbs, and are read as adverbs, not parsed as pronouns.[^tad-adverbs] *Tasmā* 'therefore' is the ablative, "from that". *Tato* 'thence, then' is the other ablative. *Tena* is the instrumental, and in the standing pair *yena … tena* 'where … there' Pāli uses it to say that someone went somewhere. A reader meets *tasmā* and *yena … tena* in almost every sutta. They are this paradigm in adverbial use, not a separate vocabulary to be learnt.

[^tad-adverbs]: Thānuttamo, *Māgadhabhāsā*, p. 56, citing Oberlies (2019) pp. 260 and 270 n. 1; he lists *taṁ*, *tamenaṁ*, *tassa*, *tasmā*, *tato* and *tahiṁ/tahaṁ* together. DPD gives *tasmā* two entries for this reason: "pron, masc & nt abl sg of *ta*", and "ind, adv, abl sg of *ta*, therefore; that is why".

Three examples. The pronoun under discussion is in bold.

- **Tāni** me bhagavā paṭiggaṇhātu anukampaṁ upādāya. (AN 5.44 — A iii 50)
- *tāni* those (nom./acc. pl. nt.) · *me* from me (1st gen. sg.) · *bhagavā* the Blessed One · *paṭiggaṇhātu* let him accept · *anukampaṁ* compassion · *upādāya* out of
- May the Blessed One accept **those** from me out of compassion.

- … so puriso **tassā** bhikkhuniyā paṭissutvā **yen**āyasmā ānando **ten**upasaṅkami. (AN 4.159 — A ii 144)
- *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *puriso* man · *tassā* to that (gen./dat. sg. f.) · *bhikkhuniyā* nun · *paṭissutvā* having assented · *yena … tena* where … there (instr. sg. m.) · *āyasmā* the venerable · *ānando* Ānanda · *upasaṅkami* he approached
- … **that** man, having assented **to that** nun, went **to where** the venerable Ānanda was.

- Evaṁ vutte, **te** paribbājakā tuṇhī ahesuṁ. (DN 9 — D i 179)
- *evaṁ* thus · *vutte* when it had been said (loc. abs.) · *te* those (nom. pl. m.) · *paribbājakā* wanderers · *tuṇhī* silent · *ahesuṁ* they were
- When this had been said, **those** wanderers fell silent.

The middle example carries both uses in one line. *Tassā* is an ordinary genitive, "that nun's", and *yena … tena* is the adverbial pair: the same stem, with two quite different functions eight words apart.

#### Proximal pronouns

There are two sets of proximal ("this/these") pronouns, and they are not interchangeable.

The first is simply the distal pronouns with *e-* prefixed: *eso*, *etaṁ*, *esā*, and so on through the whole paradigm, so it needs no table of its own: *eta(d)* declines as *ta(d)* does.[^eta-declines] Presence distinguishes it, not distance. *Ta(d)* is the pronoun of absence, used of someone or something spoken about and not before the hearers; *eta(d)* is **deictic**, pointing at what is there in front of them as the words are spoken.[^warder-deixis] So *eta(d)* is common in direct speech and in similes, and often carries a faint gesture with it.

[^eta-declines]: Oberlies §42(3), p. 186, who gives *eta(ṁ)-* and *ya(ṁ)-* as inflecting identically with *ta(ṁ)-*. The full paradigm is at Appendix A.2 under the third person.

[^warder-deixis]: Warder, *Introduction to Pali*, Lesson 5 (p. 29): *ta(d)* serves "to speak of someone or something in a story and therefore not present to the listeners", *eta(d)* points "to someone or something present to the hearers in direct speech".

- **Esevanto** dukkhassā. (SN 12.51 — S ii 84)
- *esevanto* < *eso* this (nom. sg. m.) + *eva* just + *anto* end · *dukkhassā* of suffering
- Just **this** is the end of suffering.

- **Etaṁ** yajetha medhāvī. (SN 3.9 — S i 76, verse)
- *etaṁ* this (acc. sg. nt.) · *yajetha* he should offer · *medhāvī* the wise one
- The wise one should offer **this**.

- **Ete** manussā gehaṁ pavisanti, **ete** nikkhamanti. (DN 2 — D i 83)
- *ete* these (nom. pl. m.) · *manussā* people · *gehaṁ* house · *pavisanti* they enter · *nikkhamanti* they go out
- **These** people are entering the house, **these** are leaving.

- **Esā** ñatti. (Kd 8)
- *esā* this (nom. sg. f.) · *ñatti* motion
- **This** is the motion.

The first of those is why this book gives every example in three layers. The text has *esevanto*, one word; the pronoun *eso* is inside it, and sandhi has closed over it so completely that only a reader who already knows it is there will find it. The second layer exists to resolve the sandhi, and the resolved form must stay there, because *eso eva anto* is a sequence no manuscript contains.

The second set of proximal pronouns is built on two stems, *ima-* and the *a-/ay-* that supplies the nominative.

|------------|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|------------|------------|------------|

| inst.      | *iminā*                             | *iminā*                             | *imāya*                           | *imehi*    | *imehi*    | *imāhi*    |
| abl.       | *imasmā*, *imamhā*                  | *imasmā*, *imamhā*                  | *imāya*                           | *imehi*    | *imehi*    | *imāhi*    |
| gen./dat.  | *imassa*, *assa*[^assa-optative]    | *imassa*, *assa*[^assa-optative]    | *imissā*                          | *imesaṁ*   | *imesaṁ*   | *imāsaṁ*   |

[^assa-optative]: Careful: *assa* is identical in form to the optative of *atthi* 'is', where it means "would be" — as at AN 3.51 — A i 155 above, *yaṁ amhākaṁ **assa** … hitāya*, "which **would be** for our welfare". DPD keeps four *assa* headwords apart, of which those two are the ones a reader will actually collide with. Only the context tells them apart.

The rare and metrical forms are again left to Appendix A.2, which declines this pronoun in full. One difference between the two tables is explained here, not hidden: this chapter gives *imissā* as the feminine genitive singular, while the appendix, following Ñāṇatusita, gives *imāya* there and puts *imissā* in the ablative. Neither is wrong. In the feminine the oblique cases overlap, and *imāya*, *imissā*, *imissāya*, *assā* and *assāya* are alternative forms shared out across the genitive, dative, instrumental and ablative differently by different grammarians.[^fem-obliques] Meeting *imissā*, expect a genitive and do not be surprised by an ablative.

[^fem-obliques]: Perniola §41, p. 54, prints *imāya* in the paradigm and then lists "Sg. G *imissā*, *imissāya*, *assā*, *assāya*" as alternative forms of the same case; Oberlies §42(5), p. 188, lists *imāya*, *imissā* and *imāsaṁ* together without splitting them by case at all; DPD gives *imissā* as feminine genitive singular of *ima* (*ima* + *issā*, Sanskrit *asyāḥ*), attested *imissā nemiyā* 'of this rim' in the Anaṅgaṇa Sutta.

- **Ayaṁ** te patto. (Kd 1 — Vin i 94)
- *ayaṁ* this (nom. sg. m.) · *te* your (2nd gen. sg.) · *patto* bowl
- **This** is your bowl.

- So bhikkhu mayā **iminā** ovādena ovadito saṁvegamāpādi. (AN 3.128 — A i 280)
- *so* that · *bhikkhu* monk · *mayā* by me (1st instr. sg.) · *iminā* with this (instr. sg. m.) · *ovādena* with the instruction · *ovadito* instructed · *saṁvegamāpādi* < *saṁvegaṁ* urgency + *āpādi* he came to
- That monk, instructed by me **with this** instruction, was stirred to urgency.

Because there is no firm line between personal and demonstrative in Pāli, the proximal pronouns also serve as personal ones ("he", "she", "it"), though less often than *ta(d)* does.

- 'Atthi me attā'ti vā **assa** saccato thetato diṭṭhi uppajjati. (MN 2 — M i 8)
- *atthi* there is · *me* my (1st gen. sg.) · *attā* self · *ti* — end of quotation · *vā* or · *assa* for him (gen./dat. sg. m.) · *saccato* as true · *thetato* as reliable · *diṭṭhi* view · *uppajjati* arises
- The view arises **in him**, as true and reliable: "My self exists."

- Coro **eso**, na brāhmaṇo. (MN 98 — M ii 196)
- *coro* thief · *eso* he, this one (nom. sg. m.) · *na* not · *brāhmaṇo* brahmin
- **He** is a thief, not a brahmin.

Keep the *attā* sentence. It is doctrinally the most consequential sentence in this chapter (the first of the six wrong views of self in the Sabbāsava Sutta), and grammatically it rests on a pronoun a reader is likely to misparse, since *assa* is also the optative "would be". The dative here is the one Pāli uses for the person a thing happens *to*: the view does not merely exist, it arises **for him**.

For recognition, a rough but serviceable rule covers the two series most often confused: the distal *ta(d)* forms begin with a consonant, *t-* or *s-*, and the proximal *eta(d)* and *ima-* forms with a vowel. The rule does not extend to the whole class (the remote *asu/amu* below begins with a vowel and is not proximal at all, and the anaphoric *na-* begins with a consonant without being distal), but within the *ta-*/*eta-*/*ima-* triangle, where the confusion happens, it holds.

#### Remote pronouns: *asu*/*amu*

Beyond "this" and "that" Pāli has a third demonstrative, *asu* in the nominative and *amu-* everywhere else, meaning "that one over there, yonder". It is not common, and it is not obscure either: it is what a speaker reaches for when pointing at something at a distance but still in view, or at a place named only vaguely — "such-and-such a village".

|            | sg. m.                | sg. n.                | sg. f.            | pl. m.    | pl. n.    | pl. f.    |
|------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|-------------------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| nom.       | *asu*, *amu*          | *aduṁ*                | *asu*             | *amū*     | *amūni*   | *amū*     |
| acc.       | *amuṁ*                | *aduṁ*, *amuṁ*        | *amuṁ*            | *amū*     | *amūni*   | *amū*     |
| inst.      | *amunā*               | *amunā*               | *amuyā*           | *amūhi*   | *amūhi*   | *amūhi*   |
| abl.       | *amusmā*, *amumhā*    | *amusmā*, *amumhā*    | *amuyā*           | *amūhi*   | *amūhi*   | *amūhi*   |
| gen./dat.  | *amussa*, *adussa*    | *amussa*, *adussa*    | *amussā*          | *amūsaṁ*  | *amūsaṁ*  | *amūsaṁ*  |
| loc.       | *amusmiṁ*, *amumhi*   | *amusmiṁ*, *amumhi*   | *amussaṁ*         | *amūsu*   | *amūsu*   | *amūsu*   |

The singular and the plural come from different places, so the table looks lopsided. The singular continues an old Indo-Aryan *adas-*, and its traces are the *ad-* forms, *aduṁ* and *adussa*, sitting beside the *amu-* forms in the same cells. The plural is built on *amu-* throughout, and *amu-* has since pushed into the singular as well, as far as the nominative *amu* standing beside *asu*.[^amu-history]

[^amu-history]: Oberlies §42(6), pp. 189–190, who notes that the plural "is built up exclusively from forms of this stem, which encroached likewise on the nom. sg. masc. (*amu*)", and that the neuter *aduṁ* has acquired *amuṁ* beside it under the same pressure. He also reports that *asu* scans short at Ja V 395,26\* and 396,4\*, i.e. as *aso* < *asau*. Two forms Geiger admits in the plural (§109, p. 103), *amuyo* and *amūsānaṁ*, Oberlies rejects as grammarians' constructions; the disagreement is set out in Appendix A.2, and neither form is taught here.

- Passatha no tumhe, bhikkhave, **amuṁ** mahantaṁ aggikkhandhaṁ ādittaṁ sampajjalitaṁ sajotibhūtan-ti? (AN 7.72 — A iv 128)
- *passatha* do you see · *no* — question particle · *tumhe* you (2nd nom. pl.) · *bhikkhave* monks · *amuṁ* that yonder (acc. sg. m.) · *mahantaṁ* great · *aggikkhandhaṁ* mass of fire · *ādittaṁ* burning · *sampajjalitaṁ* blazing · *sajotibhūtaṁ* glowing
- Monks, do you see **that** great mass of fire over there, burning, blazing and glowing?

- … ahaṁ kho **sakamhā** gāmā **amuṁ** gāmaṁ agacchiṁ. (DN 2 — D i 81)
- *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *kho* indeed · *sakamhā* from my own (abl. sg. m.) · *gāmā* from the village · *amuṁ* to that yonder (acc. sg. m.) · *gāmaṁ* to the village · *agacchiṁ* I went
- "I went from **my own** village to **that** village yonder."

The first is the Buddha pointing at an actual fire, and *amuṁ* means what the translation's "over there" means. The second sets the remote demonstrative directly against the possessive *saka-* 'one's own', which the possessive section below takes up: one short clause carrying both. The simile this passage applies had used *aññaṁ gāmaṁ* 'another village' where the application says *amuṁ gāmaṁ*: the text itself moves from a vague "another" to a pointed "that one", and the pronoun is what carries the shift.

The example also carries a warning that is easy to walk straight into. Searching the canon for *amuṁ* turns up *āgamuṁ*, which contains the letters and is a different word, an aorist of *āgacchati* 'comes'. A substring is not a form. Every Pāli form has to be identified, not merely recognised, and this chapter has now had to say so three times.

Distinct from the pronoun, and easily confused with it, are the adjectives *amuka* and *asuka* 'such-and-such a one', derived from this stem but declined as ordinary pronominal adjectives.[^amuka-adjective] *Amuṁ gāmaṁ* is the pronoun; *amukasmiṁ gāme* 'in such-and-such a village' is the adjective.

[^amuka-adjective]: Perniola §47, p. 57, lists *amuka* and *asuka* among the pronominal adjectives, not among the demonstratives; Gair and Karunatillake list *asuka*, 'such and such', in the vocabulary of Lesson 12. *Amukasmiṁ me gāme vā nigame vā* 'in such-and-such a village or town of mine' is attested in the Pāyāsi Sutta.

#### The anaphoric *ena-*/*na-*

Beside those three stems sits a fourth, which no table in this book declines because it has no full paradigm to decline. Collins states it clearly: there is a defective pronoun (defective because it appears in only some cases) built on the stem *na-*, carrying the same range of meaning as *sa* and *esa*.[^anaphoric-witnesses] Reference distinguishes it, not distance. It is **anaphoric**, "pointing back": it picks up something the sentence has already named, where a demonstrative proper points out at something in the world.

The history accounts for the shape. Oberlies gives it in two steps: the old anaphoric *ena(ṁ)-* was used only as the accusative of all three genders, *enaṁ*; and then, on the model by which *ta(ṁ)-* had thrown off *eta(ṁ)-*, a new anaphoric *na-* was built to *ena-* and inflected as *(e)ta(ṁ)-* does. A stem that began life as a single accusative acquired a paradigm by analogy, and never finished acquiring one. What survives is four forms:

- *naṁ* and *enaṁ* — accusative singular, all three genders
- *ne* — accusative plural masculine
- *nesaṁ* — dative and genitive plural

There is no nominative, and that absence is the most conspicuous part of the defect. Where the sense calls for a subject, the ordinary *so*, *sā* and *taṁ* supply it.

[^anaphoric-witnesses]: Collins §2.2 (p. 65) for the inventory and for "defective"; Oberlies §42(3), p. 186, for the derivation: "After the pattern of *ta(ṁ)-* : *eta(ṁ)-* a new anaphorical pronoun *na-* is created to *ena(ṁ)-* which inflects like *(e)ta(ṁ)-*." DPD reaches the same analysis from the other direction, parsing *naṁ* as masc., fem. and nt. acc. sg. of *ta* with the construction *ta > na + aṁ*, and *enaṁ* as acc. sg. of *eta* with *eta > ena + aṁ*. Collins assigns *nesaṁ* to the masculine; DPD gives it as masculine and neuter. Collins adds a fifth form, *nassa*, in the dative and genitive singular; it is not taught here, because *nassa* is also the second person singular imperative of *nassati* 'perish' (the canon's ordinary way of saying "get lost"), and the two readings need separating by a dictionary that treats both.

- Agamāsi kho, bhikkhave, so kummo taṁ padesaṁ. **Tamenaṁ** luddo papatāya vijjhi. (SN 17.3 — S ii 227)
- *agamāsi* went (aor.) · *kho* indeed · *bhikkhave* monks · *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *kummo* turtle · *taṁ* that (acc. sg. m.) · *padesaṁ* place · *tamenaṁ* < *taṁ* that (acc. sg.) + *enaṁ* it (acc. sg.) · *luddo* hunter · *papatāya* with a harpoon · *vijjhi* pierced
- The turtle did go to that place, monks. A hunter pierced **it** with a harpoon.

- Tato **naṁ** dukkhamanveti, cakkaṁva vahato padaṁ. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)
- *tato* thereupon · *naṁ* him (acc. sg., any gender) · *dukkhamanveti* < *dukkhaṁ* suffering + *anveti* follows · *cakkaṁva* < *cakkaṁ* wheel · *va* like · *vahato* of the draught ox (gen. sg.) · *padaṁ* foot
- Then suffering follows **him**, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws it.

The second of those is the first verse of the Dhammapada, which many readers will have by heart, and the *naṁ* in it has probably gone by unparsed every time. That is the argument for teaching this stem at all: it is not rare, it is invisible.

The commonest form of all is *tamenaṁ*, which is *taṁ* and *enaṁ* run together: a demonstrative reinforcing an anaphoric, the strengthening the emphasis section below describes, hardened into a fixed word.[^tamenam-sandhi] The Poṭṭhapāda Sutta uses it six times as the stock opening of a reply, *Tamenaṁ evaṁ vadeyyuṁ*, "They would say to him". Once the joint is visible the word stops being a lump of vocabulary and becomes two accusatives.

[^tamenam-sandhi]: DPD classes *tamenaṁ* as sandhi, *taṁ* + *enaṁ*, pronoun plus pronoun, and its own example is the passage quoted here. Oberlies §42 n.7, p. 184, records the general construction, that *so*, *sā* and *taṁ* are used to strengthen other pronouns and usually precede them.

### Reflexive pronouns

English marks the reflexive with a dedicated set of words (*myself*, *yourself*, *himself*, and six more), used when the object of a clause is the same as its subject. Pāli has no such set. What it has is a noun, *attā*, "self", whose oblique cases are pressed into service as a reflexive for all three persons, both genders and both numbers.[^attan-reflexive] One word covers what English spreads across nine, and the reader has to supply the person from the verb.

The forms are the ordinary declension of an *-an* stem noun, and four of them carry nearly all the traffic:

- acc. *attānaṁ* — "myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, oneself"
- instr./abl. *attanā* — "by oneself, with oneself"; and "than oneself"
- gen./dat. *attano* — "of one's own, one's own"; "for oneself"
- loc. *attani* — "in oneself"

[^attan-reflexive]: Oberlies §42(7) rem. (c), p. 191: "The oblique cases of *atta(n)-* are employed in sg. as reflexive pronoun of all three persons and genders and of both numbers." Perniola §40, p. 52, lists the reflexive as one of his seven pronoun classes; neither gives its membership, which came from Duroiselle §§328–331. DPD's glosses carry the same spread: *attānaṁ* is given as "self; himself; herself; itself; yourself; oneself (object)", acc. sg. of *atta*, Oberlies's claim reached lexically instead of grammatically.

- Nagaraṁ yathā paccantaṁ, guttaṁ santarabāhiraṁ; Evaṁ gopetha **attānaṁ**, khaṇo vo mā upaccagā. (Dhp 315 — Dhp p. 45)
- *nagaraṁ* city · *yathā* as · *paccantaṁ* frontier · *guttaṁ* guarded · *santarabāhiraṁ* within and without · *evaṁ* so · *gopetha* guard! (2nd pl. imv.) · *attānaṁ* yourselves (acc. sg.) · *khaṇo* the moment · *vo* for you (2nd enclitic) · *mā* do not · *upaccagā* let it pass by
- As a frontier city is guarded within and without, so guard **yourselves**. Do not let the moment pass you by.

The verb there is a second person plural, *gopetha*, and its object is a singular *attānaṁ*, which is no mismatch but the essence of the construction. *Attānaṁ* has no number of its own to contribute. It takes both person and number from the verb, and English has to say "yourselves" because English has no other way to say it.

The instrumental *attanā* has a second life beyond the plain reflexive. Wijesekera finds it throughout the Nikāyas with active verbs "in an emphatic sense 'by himself, personally'": "he did it himself", not "he did it to himself", a different sense.[^attana-emphatic] The distinction matters, because the emphatic use turns up in one of the most familiar passages in the canon.

[^attana-emphatic]: Wijesekera §102 and the surrounding discussion, citing *upāsako attanā ca saddhāsampanno hoti* (AN 8.25 — A iv 221, where he prints A IV.221 and omits the *ca*) and *attanā jātidhammo samāno* (MN 26). DPD gives *attanā* as instr. sg. "with oneself; by oneself" and, separately, abl. sg. "from oneself; than oneself"; the comparison at *attanā piyataro* rests on the ablative.

- Kiṁ nu kho ahaṁ **attanā** jātidhammo samāno jātidhammaṁyeva pariyesāmi …? (MN 26 — M i 163)
- *kiṁ nu kho* why on earth · *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *attanā* myself (instr. sg.) · *jātidhammo* liable to birth · *samāno* being (prp. of *atthi*) · *jātidhammaṁyeva* < *jātidhammaṁ* what is liable to birth · *yeva* precisely · *pariyesāmi* I seek
- Why, being **myself** liable to birth, do I seek out just what is liable to birth?

That is the Bodhisatta's question in the Ariyapariyesanā, and *attanā* is emphatic: "liable to birth *myself*, and still looking for more of it", not "liable to birth in myself". The whole search rests on the word.

Two further reflexives exist, both rare, and they are here chiefly because of where they survive. *Ātuma* and *tuma* are the same Sanskrit *ātman* that gave *attā*, arriving by different routes; Duroiselle ranks them, *ātumā* "very rarely used" and *tuma* "still less frequent".[^atuma-tuma] Neither he nor the grammars remark that both are attested in the same place. DPD's citation for *ātuma* is the Duṭṭhaṭṭhaka Sutta and its citation for *tuma* is the Cūḷabyūha Sutta, and both of those are in the Aṭṭhakavagga, a stratum of verse generally taken to be among the oldest in the canon. It seems reasonable to read two obsolescent forms of one word, surviving together in the archaic poetry and scarce everywhere else, as a fact about the age of that poetry, not a coincidence of two lexical entries.

[^atuma-tuma]: Duroiselle §328. DPD confirms both: *ātuma*, masc., *atta* group, from Sanskrit *ātman*; *tuma*, masc., "abbrev of *ātuma*", *ātuma > tuma*, attested at *tumo sahā hoti nihīnapañño* (Snp 4.12, Cūḷabyūhasutta). Bomhard p. 42 raised *ātumā* first, as the nominative beside *attā*, and so it is: DPD's *ātuma* belongs to the *atta* group. The same string is also a homonym, the feminine *ātumā*, a town between Kusinārā and Sāvatthī, *ātumāyaṁ viharāmi bhusāgāre* in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta.

Alongside these stand two indeclinables, *sayaṁ* and *sāmaṁ*, both meaning "oneself, personally, of one's own accord".[^sayam-samam] They do not decline, they do not agree with anything, and they carry no case; they simply intensify. English *himself* in "he said it himself" is the closest equivalent.

[^sayam-samam]: Duroiselle §330 treats both as reflexive emphatics. DPD derives *sayaṁ* from *sa* (*saya + aṁ*, Sanskrit *svayaṁ*), and *sāmaṁ* likewise from *sa* (*sa + āmaṁ*, Sanskrit *svayam*), glossing the latter "oneself; by oneself; for oneself" and "individually; personally". Both derivations put them in the same family as the possessive *sa(ka)-* below, not with *attā*.

One verse carries three of these at once, and it is a verse about the vice of talking about oneself:

- Yo **attano** sīlavatāni jantu, anānupuṭṭhova paresa pāva; Anariyadhammaṁ kusalā tamāhu, yo **ātumānaṁ** **sayam**eva pāva. (Snp 4.3 — Sn p. 153)
- *yo* who (nom. sg. m.) · *attano* his own (gen. sg.) · *sīlavatāni* precepts and practices · *jantu* person · *anānupuṭṭhova* < *anānupuṭṭho* unasked · *va* just · *paresa* to others (dat. pl. of *para*) · *pāva* told · *anariyadhammaṁ* an ignoble nature · *kusalā* the skilled · *tamāhu* < *taṁ* that (acc. sg.) + *āhu* they call · *ātumānaṁ* himself (acc. sg.) · *sayameva* < *sayaṁ* of his own accord · *eva* just
- The person who tells others of **his own** precepts and practices unasked — the skilled call that an ignoble nature, in one who speaks of **himself** **unprompted**.

*Attano*, *ātumānaṁ* and *sayaṁ* stand within four lines of each other, and *paresa*, a dative plural of the pronominal adjective *para*, irregular for *paresaṁ*, is a fourth point this chapter teaches, in the second line.

### Possessive pronouns

Pāli has no possessive pronoun corresponding to English *my*, *your*, *his*. It has the genitive of the personal pronoun, with the same function: *mama* or *mayhaṁ* for "my", *tava* or *tuyhaṁ* for "your", *tassa* for "his", and the enclitics *me*, *te*, *no*, *vo* beside them. Learn the personal paradigms above and you already have the possessives; nothing new is declined here.

What is new is a rule about which case to reach for, and two grammars were needed to state it. Perniola establishes that the genitive is what Pāli uses for possession. Wijesekera, following Havers, gives the division that Perniola's half leaves open: with the first and second person pronouns the dative is used (his "sympathetic dative", marking the person who has a stake in the action, not merely the owner of something), and the genitive with all other pronouns, with nouns, and with participles.[^possessive-division] Since *mayhaṁ* and *tuyhaṁ* are dative and genitive at once, the two halves of the rule rarely conflict on the page. Where they do, the sense is the guide.

[^possessive-division]: Wijesekera §102, following Havers: "with pronouns of the first and second persons the sympathetic dat. is employed. But the gen. with all other pronouns, nouns and participles." Perniola §45(b), p. 57, supplies the other half, that possession is expressed by the genitive of the personal pronoun. Neither source carries both statements, and the two were only put together for this chapter; the reader should treat the division as a well-attested tendency, not as a rule without exceptions.

- Yo vo, ānanda, mayā dhammo ca vinayo ca desito paññatto, so **vo** mamaccayena satthā. (DN 16 — D ii 154)
- *yo* which (nom. sg. m.) · *vo* to you (2nd enclitic) · *ānanda* Ānanda · *mayā* by me (1st instr. sg.) · *dhammo* teaching · *ca* and · *vinayo* training · *desito* taught · *paññatto* laid down · *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *mamaccayena* < *mama* of me (1st gen. sg.) + *accayena* with the passing · *satthā* teacher
- Ānanda, the teaching and training I have taught and laid down will be **your** Teacher after I am gone.

That is the Buddha's instruction at the Parinibbāna, and the pronoun carrying it is a sympathetic dative *vo*: "the teacher for you, in your interest, now that I am going", not "the teacher belonging to you". English has to render it "your", which loses the whole sense of the case. The same sentence has *mama* in the genitive inside the compound *mamaccayena*: the two halves of Wijesekera's rule in one clause, the dative for the second person and the genitive elsewhere.

Beside the genitives stands a true possessive adjective, *saka-* or *sa-*, "one's own", which serves all three persons alike and declines as an ordinary adjective.[^saka-possessive] Pāli reaches for it when the possessor is the subject of the clause: the possessive counterpart of the reflexive *attano* above, and often interchangeable with it.

[^saka-possessive]: Oberlies §42(7) rem. (d), p. 191: "The possessive pronoun for all three persons is *sa(ka)-* 'own' (*sva[ka]-*)." DPD gives *saka* as an adjective from *sa*, *sa + ka*, Sanskrit *svaka*, "one's own; my own", and separately as a neuter noun, "one's own property". Also from *mama* is the derived *māmaka*, which DPD glosses "follower; devotee; disciple", literally "one of mine": a noun, not a possessive, and best left out of the paradigm.

- Tasmātiha, bhikkhave, abhikkhaṇaṁ **sakaṁ** cittaṁ paccavekkhitabbaṁ. (SN 22.100 — S iii 151)
- *tasmātiha* < *tasmā* therefore (abl. sg.) + *t* + *iha* here · *bhikkhave* monks · *abhikkhaṇaṁ* repeatedly · *sakaṁ* one's own (acc. sg. n.) · *cittaṁ* mind · *paccavekkhitabbaṁ* should be reviewed
- Therefore, monks, **one's own** mind should be reviewed again and again.

The remote-pronoun example above, *ahaṁ kho sakamhā gāmā amuṁ gāmaṁ agacchiṁ*, is the same word in the ablative, and it shows what *saka-* is for: the sentence has no possessor named anywhere, and needs none, because *saka-* means "belonging to whoever the subject is".

### Interrogative pronouns

An interrogative pronoun asks about a thing or a person: English 'what?', 'which?', 'who?', 'whose?'. Words like 'where?', 'why?' and 'when?' ask instead about place, reason and time; they are interrogative adverbs, not pronouns, and Chapter 12 sets out the Pāli ones.

As with the demonstratives, the interrogatives can refer to persons or to things alike. They are built on the stem *ka-*, and they follow the pronominal declension, so most of the table below will already be familiar from *ta(d)*.

|            | sg. m.                  | sg. n.                  | sg. f.                              | pl. m.    | pl. n.    | pl. f.    |
|------------|-------------------------|-------------------------|-------------------------------------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| nom.       | *ko*                    | *kiṁ*                   | *kā*                                | *ke*      | *kāni*    | *kā*      |
| acc.       | *kaṁ*                   | *kiṁ*                   | *kaṁ*                               | *ke*      | *kāni*    | *kā*      |
| inst.      | *kena*                  | *kena*                  | *kāya*                              | *kehi*    | *kehi*    | *kāhi*    |
| abl.       | *kasmā*, *kismā*        | *kasmā*, *kismā*        | *kāya*                              | *kehi*    | *kehi*    | *kāhi*    |
| gen./dat.  | *kassa*, *kissa*        | *kassa*, *kissa*        | *kassā*                             | *kesaṁ*   | *kesaṁ*   | *kāsaṁ*   |
| loc.       | *kismiṁ*, *kimhi*       | *kismiṁ*, *kimhi*       | *kāya(ṁ)*, *kassaṁ*, *kissaṁ*       | *kesu*    | *kesu*    | *kāsu*    |

Appendix A.2 does not carry an interrogative paradigm, so this table is the book's only statement of it. Only the double stem needs comment. Beside *ka-* stands a second stem, *ki-*, and three of the oblique cases admit forms built on it: ablative *kismā*, genitive *kissa*, locative *kismiṁ* and *kimhi*.[^ki-stem] They are the same pronoun in its other stem, not alternatives to be learnt separately, and once that is seen a form like *kismā* stops looking irregular.

[^ki-stem]: Oberlies §42(4), p. 187, lists the *ki(ṁ)-* forms that supplement the *ka-*paradigm as *kismā, kissa, kimhi, kismiṁ*; Perniola §43, p. 56, gives the same set independently ("Sg. G *kissa*, Ab *kismā*, L *kimhi*"); DPD parses *kismā* as "masc & nt abl sg of *ka*, irreg", construction *ka > ki + smā*, Sanskrit *kasmāt*. Earlier drafts of this table had *kasmā* alone in the ablative while accepting the *ki-* stem in the genitive and locative, an omission, not a choice. Perniola also gives a feminine genitive *kissā* and plural genitives *kesānaṁ*/*kāsānaṁ*; neither turned up in a search of the CST root texts, so they are recorded here, not taught.

- **Kiṁ**, bhante, kusalaṁ? (DN 26 — D iii 61)
- *kiṁ* what (nom. sg. n.) · *bhante* venerable sir · *kusalaṁ* skilful
- Venerable sir, **what** is skilful?

- **Kassa** nu tvaṁ, bhikkhuni, pāsaṇḍaṁ rocesi? (SN 5.8 — S i 133)
- *kassa* whose (gen. sg. m.) · *nu* — question particle · *tvaṁ* you (2nd nom. sg.) · *bhikkhuni* nun (voc.) · *pāsaṇḍaṁ* creed · *rocesi* do you approve of
- **Whose** creed do you approve of, nun?

- **Ko** āpanno? **Kiṁ** āpanno? **Kismiṁ** āpanno? Kathaṁ[^kathan-adverb] āpanno? **Kaṁ** bhaṇatha? (Bu Pc 12 — Vin iv 35)
- *ko* who (nom. sg. m.) · *āpanno* has fallen into (an offence) · *kiṁ* what (acc. sg. n.) · *kismiṁ* in what (loc. sg.) · *kathaṁ* how · *kaṁ* whom (acc. sg. m.) · *bhaṇatha* do you speak of
- **Who** has committed an offence? **What** has he committed? **In what** has he committed it? How has he committed it? **Whom** are you speaking of?

[^kathan-adverb]: *Kathaṁ* is an interrogative adverb, not a pronoun, so it is not bolded with the rest.

#### The strengthening particle *-su*

An interrogative is often reinforced by the particle *-su* or *-ssu*, written onto the end of it: *kiṁsu*, *kaṁsu*, *kenassu*.[^kimsu-particle] The particle adds no meaning a translation can carry (it is the difference between "what?" and "what on earth?"), but it matters for reading, because *kiṁsu* opens a long run of riddle verses in the first *saṁyutta*, and without it you will look for a word that is not there.

[^kimsu-particle]: Perniola §43, p. 56: "The interrogative pronoun is sometimes strengthened with the particle *-su / -ssu*: *kaṁ-su*, *kena-ssu*." No other source consulted for this chapter mentions it. DPD treats *kiṁsu* as a lexical item, *kiṁ + su*, Sanskrit *kaḥ svid*.

- “**Kiṁsu** janeti purisaṁ, **kiṁsu** tassa vidhāvati; **Kiṁsu** saṁsāramāpādi, **kismā** na parimuccatī”ti. (SN 1.56 — S i 37)
- *kiṁsu* < *kiṁ* what (nom. sg. n.) · *su* — emphatic particle · *janeti* gives birth to · *purisaṁ* a person · *tassa* his (gen. sg. m.) · *vidhāvati* runs about · *saṁsāramāpādi* < *saṁsāraṁ* transmigration + *āpādi* has entered · *kismā* from what (abl. sg.) · *na* not · *parimuccati* is freed
- **What** gives birth to a person? **What** of his runs about? **What** has entered transmigration? **From what** is he not freed?

Four interrogatives in four lines, three of them *kiṁsu* and the fourth the ablative *kismā* that the table above had been missing. It is a convenient verse, and it is also the reason the omission mattered: a reader working through the Devatāsaṁyutta meets *kismā* almost at once.

#### *Kati* 'how many' and its relatives

*Kati*, "how many?", is an interrogative the chapter's table cannot hold, because it has no singular and no gender: one form serves masculine, feminine and neuter alike, and only in the plural.[^kati-forms]

- nom./acc. *kati*
- instr./abl. *katīhi*
- gen./dat. *katīnaṁ*
- loc. *katīsu*

[^kati-forms]: Oberlies §42(4) rem. (c), p. 187, gives nom. pl. mfn. *kati*, instr. *katīhi* and gen. *katī-*; Perniola §43, p. 56, gives all four cases, independently and identically. DPD confirms the word as *ka + ti*, Sanskrit *kati*, and its headword example is the verse quoted here.

- “**Kati** jāgarataṁ suttā, **kati** suttesu jāgarā; **Katibhi** rajamādeti, **katibhi** parisujjhatī”ti. (SN 1.6 — S i 3)
- *kati* how many (nom. pl.) · *jāgarataṁ* among the waking (gen. pl.) · *suttā* asleep · *suttesu* among the sleeping (loc. pl.) · *jāgarā* awake · *katibhi* by how many (instr. pl.) · *rajamādeti* < *rajaṁ* dust + *ādeti* takes · *parisujjhati* is cleansed
- **How many** sleep among the waking? **How many** wake among the sleeping? **By how many** does one gather dust? **By how many** is one cleansed?[^katibhi-variant]

[^katibhi-variant]: The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *katibhi* here and records *katīhi* in its own apparatus as the Sinhalese reading; the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print *katīhi*, which is the form Oberlies (§42(4), p. 187) and Perniola (§43, p. 56) both give. The example is quoted as printed, and the two spellings are the same instrumental plural.

A single short verse declines the word twice, in parallel construction, so the nominative and the instrumental sit one line apart. Beside *kati* stand two more *ka-* derivatives. *Kittaka*, "how much? how many?", declines as an ordinary adjective; and *katara* and *katama*, "which?", decline like *ko* and are treated with the pronominal adjectives below.

On *katara* and *katama* the tradition and the canon do not agree, and the disagreement is instructive. The indigenous grammarians distinguish them sharply: *katara* asks "which of two?" and *katama* "which of many?", in a series that also has *itara* "the other of two" against *añña* "another of many".[^katara-katama] It is a tidy rule, the kind a textbook likes to teach. But DPD glosses *katara* "which of the two?; which of the many?", and the passage it cites for the word is the Buddha asking Puṇṇa *katarasmiṁ janapade viharissasi*, "in which country will you stay?", asked of the many countries of India, not of two. So the grammarians draw the distinction and the canon does not consistently observe it. Learn the rule, because it explains why two words exist; do not use it to decide a reading.

[^katara-katama]: Thānuttamo, *Māgadhabhāsā*, p. 55, reporting the traditional series: (2) *katara* "which [of two]?", (3) *katama* "which [of many]?", (5) *itara* "other [of two]", (6) *añña* "other [of many]". DPD's entry for *katara* is *ka + tara*, Sanskrit *katara*, glossed both ways, with *katarasmiṁ janapade viharissasī'ti* (MN 145) as its attestation.

### Indefinite pronouns

Pāli builds its indefinites ("someone", "anything", "whoever") out of material it already has. There is no new stem to learn and no new declension; there are four ways of putting old words together, and knowing only the first, you will misread the other three.[^perniola-indefinites]

[^perniola-indefinites]: Perniola §44, pp. 56–57, sets out all four. The chapter previously taught only the first. Method (c) was noted for the relative-clause chapter by an earlier reading of Oberlies §42 n.7, p. 184 ("the relative pronoun followed by a corresponding form of the 3. person personal pronoun acquires the meaning 'whoever, whichever'"), but Perniola's classification is the better home for it, since *yo so* is a way of saying "whoever", not a relative clause. Perniola adds *katici* and *katipaya* "some", plural only; DPD has no entry for *katici*, so it is recorded here, not taught.

**(a) Interrogative plus *-ci*.** The suffix *-ci*, rarely *-cana*, attaches to an interrogative form and cancels its question:

- *koci* — anyone, someone (nom. sg. m.)
- *kiñci* < *kiṁ* + *ci* — anything, something (nom./acc. sg. n.)
- *kenaci* — by anyone, by anything (instr. sg. m./n.)
- *kassaci* — of anyone, to anyone (gen./dat. sg. m./n.)

Any case of *ka-* can take it, so the list is a sample and not an inventory. Before a vowel the *-ci* often shows its older *-cid*, which is where *kocideva* comes from: *koci* + *eva*, with the *d* surfacing between them.[^kocideva]

[^kocideva]: DPD treats *kocideva* as a sandhi form, *koci + [d] + eva*, Sanskrit *kaḥ cid eva*, glossing it "some or other". The *d* is the original final of Sanskrit *cid*, not an insertion; Pāli otherwise drops it.

- Aho vata maṁ **koci** nimanteyya. (MN 124 — M iii 126)
- *aho vata* oh that! · *maṁ* me (1st acc. sg.) · *koci* someone (nom. sg. m.) · *nimanteyya* might invite
- Oh, if only **someone** would invite me!

Combined with *na*, the same forms express absence. Pāli has no separate word for "nobody" or "nothing"; it says "not anyone", "not anything", and lets the negative carry the sense. English can go either way in translation, and the second rendering below is often the better one.

- **Na** khvāhaṁ, āvuso, **kassaci** pāsaṇḍaṁ rocemi. (SN 5.8 — S i 133)
- *na* not · *khvāhaṁ* < *kho* indeed + *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *āvuso* friend · *kassaci* of anyone (gen. sg. m.) · *pāsaṇḍaṁ* creed · *rocemi* I approve of
- I do **not** approve of **anyone's** creed, friend — or, more naturally, I approve of **nobody's** creed.

- **N**'atthi no **kiñci** uttariṁ karaṇīyaṁ. (MN 39 — M i 271)
- *natthi* < *na* not + *atthi* there is · *no* for us (1st enclitic) · *kiñci* anything (nom. sg. n.) · *uttariṁ* further · *karaṇīyaṁ* to be done
- There is **nothing** further for us to do.

**(b) Reduplication.** A relative or demonstrative repeated becomes indefinite: *yo yo* "whoever", *yaṁ yaṁ* "whatever", and in the correlative clause *taṁ taṁ*, *tena tena* answering it. The doubling is not emphasis. It is the difference between "the condition" and "whatever condition".

- Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, **yaṁ yad**eva paccayaṁ paṭicca aggi jalati **tena ten**eva saṅkhyaṁ gacchati. (MN 38 — M i 259)
- *seyyathāpi* just as · *bhikkhave* monks · *yaṁ yadeva* < *yaṁ yaṁ* whatever (acc. sg. n.) + *eva* just · *paccayaṁ* condition · *paṭicca* dependent on · *aggi* fire · *jalati* burns · *tena teneva* < *tena tena* by that very one (instr. sg.) + *eva* just · *saṅkhyaṁ* reckoning · *gacchati* goes to
- Just as a fire burns dependent on **whatever** condition, and is reckoned by **that very** one, monks.

That is the sentence the Buddha uses to correct Sāti's view of consciousness, and the whole correction is carried by two reduplicated pronouns. A fire burning on logs is a log-fire; consciousness arising through the eye is eye-consciousness. Neither has a name of its own.

**(c) Relative plus demonstrative.** A relative followed by the corresponding form of *ta(d)* also gives "whoever, whichever": *yo so*, *ye te*, *yaṁ taṁ*. The canon uses it in one of its most formal registers, the description of the arahant: *yo so bhikkhu arahaṁ khīṇāsavo*, "whatever monk is a perfected one with defilements ended". Take *yo so* for a stray demonstrative and you will look for an antecedent that is not there.

**(d) Relative plus indefinite.** The relative combined with an indefinite from (a) gives *yaṁ kiñci* "whatever", *ye keci* "whatever ones", *yā kāci*. This is the commonest of the four after (a), because it opens the standard analytical formula for each of the aggregates.

- Tasmātiha, bhikkhave, **yaṁ kiñci** rūpaṁ atītānāgatapaccuppannaṁ … sabbaṁ rūpaṁ: 'netaṁ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā'ti … daṭṭhabbaṁ. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)
- *tasmātiha* < *tasmā* therefore + *t* + *iha* here · *yaṁ kiñci* whatever (nom. sg. n.) · *rūpaṁ* form · *atītānāgatapaccuppannaṁ* past, future or present · *sabbaṁ* all · *netaṁ* < *na* not + *etaṁ* this · *mama* mine · *nesohamasmi* < *na* not + *eso* that + *ahaṁ* I + *asmi* am · *na meso attā* not this my self · *daṭṭhabbaṁ* should be seen
- Therefore, monks, **whatever** form there is, past, future or present … all form should be seen as: "This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self."

Four pronoun classes are at work in that one formula (the indefinite *yaṁ kiñci*, the proximal *etaṁ* and *eso*, the possessive genitive *mama*, and the reflexive *attā*), a fair measure of how much of this chapter a reader needs in order to follow a single stock passage.

### Pronominal adjectives

A **pronominal adjective** is an adjective that declines, in part or in whole, with the endings of the pronouns instead of those of the nouns. The category looks like a miscellany until it is seen from the other side. The indigenous *sabbanāma* list at the head of this chapter counts *sabba*, *añña*, *itara*, *pubba*, *para*, *uttara* and the rest as pronouns outright; a European description narrows "pronoun" to what English would call one, and everything left over has to be housed somewhere. "Pronominal adjectives" is the name of that remainder. Their declension is a survival, not an anomaly.

The differences from the ordinary adjective declension show up in three places, and those three are the diagnostics: masculine nominative plural *-e* where a noun would have *-ā*; genitive plural *-esaṁ* and *-āsaṁ* where a noun would have *-ānaṁ*; and feminine locative singular *-assaṁ* beside *-āya(ṁ)*.

|            | sg. m.              | sg. n.              | sg. f.                  | pl. m.    | pl. n.    | pl. f.    |
|------------|---------------------|---------------------|-------------------------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| nom.       | *-o*                | *-aṁ*               | *-ā*                    | *-e*      | *-āni*    | *-ā*      |
| acc.       | *-aṁ*               | *-aṁ*               | *-aṁ*                   | *-e*      | *-āni*    | *-ā*      |
| inst.      | *-ena*              | *-ena*              | *-āya*                  | *-ehi*    | *-ehi*    | *-āhi*    |
| abl.       | *-asmā*, *-amhā*, *-ato* | *-asmā*, *-amhā*, *-ato* | *-āya*             | *-ehi*    | *-ehi*    | *-āhi*    |
| gen./dat.  | *-assa*             | *-assa*             | *-assā*                 | *-esaṁ*   | *-esaṁ*   | *-āsaṁ*   |
| loc.       | *-asmiṁ*, *-amhi*   | *-asmiṁ*, *-amhi*   | *-āya(ṁ)*, *-assaṁ*     | *-esu*    | *-esu*    | *-āsu*    |

The common members are:

- *añña* — other, another
- *aññatara* — a certain, one of
- *aññatama* — a certain one
- *ekacca* — some
- *eka* — one; a certain
- *itara* — the other
- *katama*, *katara* — which? (interrogative; see above)
- *(a)para* — the other, a further; beyond
- *pubba* — former, previous
- *uttara* — upper, northern
- *sabba* — all, whole

Also here, though built from a demonstrative, are *amuka* and *asuka* "such-and-such a one", which decline as pronominal adjectives and not as the remote pronoun they come from.

Pronominal adjectives work as adjectives, as nouns, and as pronouns, which on this book's definition includes what English grammar calls determiners.

- **Katame** ca, bhikkhave, saṅkhārā? (SN 12.2 — S ii 4)
- *katame* which (nom. pl. m.) · *ca* and · *bhikkhave* monks · *saṅkhārā* choices, volitional activities
- And **what**, monks, are choices?

- **Sabbe** sattā marissanti. (SN 3.22 — S i 97)
- *sabbe* all (nom. pl. m.) · *sattā* beings · *marissanti* will die
- **All** beings will die.

Both of those show the first diagnostic: *katame* and *sabbe* where a noun would give *-ā*.

#### The rule is a tendency, not a law

It is tempting to state this class as a clean rule (these adjectives take pronoun endings, full stop), and the temptation should be resisted, because the canon does not cooperate. Oberlies's formulation is that a number of adjectives are inflected "**in part or wholly**" according to the pronominal declension, and he supplies the minimal pairs that make the qualification necessary.[^pronominal-variation] The clearest of them is *uttara* in the feminine locative, which the canon declines both ways.

- Kiñcāpi te taṁ jalate vimānaṁ, Obhāsayaṁ **uttarassaṁ** **disāyaṁ**. (SN 6.6 — S i 148)
- *kiñcāpi* although · *te* your (2nd enclitic) · *taṁ* that (nom. sg. n.) · *jalate* shines · *vimānaṁ* palace · *obhāsayaṁ* lighting up (prp.) · *uttarassaṁ* northern (loc. sg. f., pronominal) · *disāyaṁ* in the quarter
- Though that palace of yours shines, lighting up the **northern quarter** …

- … na **uttarāya** **disāya** udakassa āyamukhaṁ … (DN 2 — D i 74)
- *na* not · *uttarāya* northern (loc. sg. f., nominal) · *disāya* in the quarter · *udakassa* of water · *āyamukhaṁ* inlet
- … there is no inlet of water from the **northern quarter** …

Same word, same case, same number, same gender; one pronominal ending and one nominal. Only the passage they stand in distinguishes them. These adjectives, then, take pronominal endings *variably*, and the three diagnostics above show where to look, not what to expect. That is a weaker rule than the one a handout would give, and it is the true one.

[^pronominal-variation]: Oberlies §42(7), pp. 190–191: "A number of adjectives are inflected, in part or wholly, according to the pronominal declension ([e.g.] loc. sg. fem. in *-assaṁ ~ -āya*, nom. pl. masc. in *-e ~ -ā*, gen. in *-esaṁ ~ -ānaṁ*)." His attested pairs are *uttarassaṁ disāyaṁ* beside *uttarāya disāya*, and nom. pl. masc. *aññatarā* (S IV 341,23) and *itarā* (Dīp VI 26) where the pronominal rule predicts *-e*. Earlier drafts of this chapter stated flatly that these adjectives "do *not* take noun endings", which the first of those pairs refutes.

### Translating pronouns

#### Distal versus proximal

A distal pronoun is generally best rendered 'that' or 'those', and a proximal one 'this' or 'these'. Where the difference carries no weight, a translation can be freer.

- **Idam**avoca bhagavā. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)
- *idamavoca* < *idaṁ* this (acc. sg. n.) + *avoca* said (aor.) · *bhagavā* the Blessed One
- **This** is what the Blessed One **said**.

#### Demonstrative pronouns as 'the'

Pāli has no definite article, and a demonstrative takes on its work.

- Eso nu te, mārisa, **so** bhagavā satthā? (MN 37 — M i 255)
- *eso* this (nom. sg. m.) · *nu* — question particle · *te* your (2nd enclitic) · *mārisa* sir (voc.) · *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *bhagavā* Blessed One · *satthā* teacher
- Sir, is this your teacher, **the** Blessed One?

#### Pronouns for emphasis

A demonstrative, most often a form of *ta(d)*, is frequently set in front of another pronoun to strengthen it. The construction is common, it rarely survives translation, and its rule is easy to state once and then hard to forget: the fronted pronoun agrees with the person contained in the verb, whatever that person is.[^emphasis-witnesses] Since Pāli verbs carry their person, a third-person *so* in front of a first-person verb is emphasis, neither a mistake nor a change of subject, and the reader's job is to feel it, not render it.

[^emphasis-witnesses]: Five sources agree. Geiger §106.1 heads his first subsection "before the pers. pron. of the first and second persons", citing *so ahaṁ* Sn 190, *svāhaṁ* Ja I 198,3, *tesaṁ vo, bhikkhave, tumhākaṁ* It 32,1 and *tesaṁ no amhākaṁ* M III 194,19, the last two being third-person genitive plurals fronting a second- and a first-person pronoun. Oberlies §42 n.7, p. 184, adds that *so* "may refer also to the person contained in a verbal form". Warder's Lesson 5 (p. 29) says the first and second person pronouns are the frequently emphatic ones and the third is less often so used, and gives *so 'haṁ* "I", literally "that I", as his one example. Thānuttamo p. 56 reports *sohaṁ* as pleonastic emphasis. DPD glosses *sohaṁ* as "I definitely" and *soyaṁ* as "this very", so the emphasis is in the lexicon as well as in the grammars. Earlier drafts of this chapter stated the reverse, restricting the emphatic reading to the third person and attributing that to Warder; the attribution and the claim were both wrong.

- **So** kho ahaṁ, bhante, evaṁ rakkhito … vihāsiṁ. (Ud 2.10 — Ud p. 19)
- *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *kho* indeed · *ahaṁ* I (1st nom. sg.) · *bhante* venerable sir · *evaṁ* thus · *rakkhito* protected · *vihāsiṁ* I dwelt (1st sg.)
- **I**, venerable sir, protected in this way … dwelt on.

- **Tassa** mayhaṁ, bho, evaṁ hoti. (DN 5 — D i 143)
- *tassa* of/to that (gen./dat. sg. m.) · *mayhaṁ* to me (1st dat. sg.) · *bho* sir · *evaṁ* thus · *hoti* it is
- Sir, it occurs to **me** thus.

- **So** karohi dīpam attano. (Dhp 236 — Dhp p. 35)
- *so* that (nom. sg. m.) · *karohi* make! (2nd sg. imv.) · *dīpaṁ* an island, a refuge · *attano* for yourself (gen./dat. sg.)
- Make **yourself** an island.[^dipa-gloss]

[^dipa-gloss]: DPD keeps two homonyms apart here: *dīpa* 'lamp, light', from *dippati*; and *dīpa* 'island, terra firma, refuge, place of safety', from *dvi* + *āpa*. Both readings of this verse are current in the tradition. The point illustrated is *so*, and it does not depend on which sense is chosen.

The first two are first-person verbs (*vihāsiṁ* "I dwelt", *hoti* with the dative *mayhaṁ*), and the third is a second-person imperative, *karohi* "make!". In every one of them the fronted pronoun is third-person *so* or *tassa*. So the construction is clearly not restricted to the third person; if anything the first and second are where it is commonest.

The same strengthening happens between two demonstratives, and there sandhi usually closes over the join.

- **Soy**aṁ[^so-ayam-assim] bhijjissati kāyo. (Thag 16.1 — Th p. 72, verse)
- *soyaṁ* < *so* that (nom. sg. m.) + *ayaṁ* this (nom. sg. m.) · *bhijjissati* will be broken up · *kāyo* body
- **This very** body will break up.

[^so-ayam-assim]: The text reads *soyaṁ*; the pair is built from *so ayaṁ*, which is not what is printed. DPD treats the joined form as a lexical item in its own right, "this very": the emphasis showing in the dictionary instead of the grammar.

#### Pronouns in compounds

A pronoun inside a compound behaves like any other member of a compound, but it is harder to analyse, because the compound stem carries no case, number or gender and the reader has to reconstruct all three. The stem of the distal *ta(d)* is *taṁ* or *tad*, and it can stand for *so*, *taṁ*, *tassa* or any other form of the paradigm. Before a consonant the *d* assimilates: *tak-*, *tap-*, *tal-*.

- Na **takkaro** hoti naro pamatto. (Dhp 19 — Dhp p. 3)
- *na* not · *takkaro* < *tad* that + *kara* doing · *hoti* is · *naro* a man · *pamatto* negligent
- The negligent man is not **one who does it**.

Unpacked, *takkaro* is *tassa karo*, "a doer of that": the genitive is not in the compound, and only the sense supplies it.

- Mahāaggikkhandho **tadāhāro** **tadupādāno** ciraṁ dīghamaddhānaṁ jaleyya. (SN 12.52 — S ii 85)
- *mahāaggikkhandho* great mass of fire · *tadāhāro* < *tad* that + *āhāra* nutriment · *tadupādāno* < *tad* that + *upādāna* fuel · *ciraṁ* long · *dīghamaddhānaṁ* for a long stretch of time · *jaleyya* would burn
- With **that as its nutriment** and **that as its fuel**, the great mass of fire would burn for a long time.

The other pronouns supply compound stems too, and a reader meets the interrogative most: *ki(ṁ)-*, which assimilates in the same way, *kin-* before *n*.[^ki-compounds] A single question in the Kathāvatthu runs three of them together, *kinnāmo so bhagavā, kiṁjacco, kiṁgotto*: "What is that Blessed One's name, of what birth, of what clan?"

[^ki-compounds]: Oberlies §42(4), p. 187, gives the stem *ki(ṁ)-* with *kiṁjacca-* and *kiṁsīla-* among its attestations. DPD carries both as compound adjectives: *kiṁjacca*, *kiṁ + jacca*, "of what caste (are you)?", and *kiṁsīla*, *kiṁ + sīla*. Oberlies §42(1), p. 182, also gives first and second person compound stems *maṁ-* and *tvaṁ-*, with five attestations including *tvaṁnātho 'smi*. Warder's caution, p. 28, stands beside them: "in practice the 1st and 2nd person pronouns are hardly used in compounds." Both statements are true, and the second governs what a reader should expect on the page.

- **Kiṁsīlo** **kiṁsamācāro**, kāni kammāni brūhayaṁ. (Snp 2.9 — Sn p. 56)
- *kiṁsīlo* < *kiṁ* what + *sīla* conduct · *kiṁsamācāro* < *kiṁ* what + *samācāra* behaviour · *kāni* what (nom./acc. pl. n.) · *kammāni* deeds · *brūhayaṁ* fostering (prp.)
- Of **what conduct**, of **what behaviour**, fostering what deeds …?

That verse opens a sutta the tradition named after its first word, the Kiṁsīla Sutta, which shows as well as anything that a *kiṁ-* compound is ordinary Pāli and not a curiosity.
