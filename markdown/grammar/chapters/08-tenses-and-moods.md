## Verbs: The Past and the Future

The three forms built on the present stem (the indicative, the imperative and the optative) are Chapter 7's. This chapter takes what is built from the root instead: the past, the future, and the conditional, together with the negation and prohibition that go with them.

One line of the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* has all three tenses in a row, and it is a good place to begin, because the form of each is visible against the other two:

- *na cāhu na ca bhavissati na cetarahi vijjati añño samaṇo vā brāhmaṇo vā bhagavatā bhiyyobhiññataro yadidaṁ sambodhiyaṁ.* (DN 16 — D ii 82)
- *na* not · *ca* and · *āhu* there has been (3 sg. aor.) · *na* not · *ca* and · *bhavissati* there will be (3 sg. fut.) · *na* not · *ca* and · *etarahi* now · *vijjati* there is found (3 sg. pres.) · *añño* another (m. nom. sg.) · *samaṇo* ascetic (m. nom. sg.) · *vā* or · *brāhmaṇo* brahmin (m. nom. sg.) · *bhagavatā* than the Blessed One (m. inst. sg.) · *bhiyyobhiññataro* of greater direct knowledge (m. nom. sg.) · *yadidaṁ* that is · *sambodhiyaṁ* as to awakening (f. loc. sg.)
- "There has not been, there will not be, and there is not now, another ascetic or brahmin whose direct knowledge is greater than the Blessed One's when it comes to awakening."

*Āhu*, *bhavissati*, *vijjati*: past, future, present, and each carries its tense in a different place. The present is the bare stem plus an ending, as Chapter 7 had it. The future inserts *-ssa-* between the stem and the same ending. The past does neither: *āhu* is a form of the root with nothing recognisable added at all.[^ahu-note] That asymmetry is the subject of this chapter.

[^ahu-note]: *Ahu* is the root aorist of *hoti/bhavati*, from *bhū-*; it has 460 occurrences in the root texts. The form here is *cāhu*, from *ca* + *ahu*, with the vowels merged by the sandhi of Chapter 2.

### The augment

Both of the past formations in this chapter and the conditional share one mark: a prefixed *a-*, called the **augment** (*vuddhi* in the Sanskrit tradition's sense of increase, though the Pāli grammarians simply call it *a-kāra-āgama*, 'the coming of an *a*'). It is inherited from Old Indic, where it was obligatory, and in Pāli it is not.

Two facts about it matter more than its history. It is not a negation. *Agamā* is 'he went', not 'he did not go'; the negative is the separate word *na*, treated at the end of this chapter. Having learnt the prefix *a-* as a negative on nouns and adjectives (*anicca* 'impermanent', *asukha* 'unpleasant'), you will misread a great many past-tense verbs unless the two are firmly separated.

And when a prefix is present, the augment goes between the prefix and the root, not in front of the whole word: *ud* + *a* + *pādi* gives *udapādi* 'it arose'; *saṁ* + *a* + *bhavi* gives *samabhavi* 'it came to be'. Here a reader is most likely to lose it, because the augment is then buried in the middle of a long word and looks like part of the stem.[^augment-prefix]

Whether it appears at all is not a free choice, and two sources state the pattern as a rule instead of a list of examples, arriving at it from opposite ends.[^augment-rule] Perniola grades it by the type of aorist. The root aorist **always** takes the augment; the *a*-aorist and the *s*-aorist **generally** do; and it is often dropped when the verb carries a prefix ending in *-a*, so *pahāsi* and *upapajjatha* stand beside augmented forms of the same verbs. Geiger grades it by the length of the word instead: short forms keep the augment, long ones shed it, and the shedding spreads as the language gets later. The two meet where it matters most, since the root aorist's forms are the monosyllables: *adā*, *agā*, *ahu*. In verse, the metre decides.

[^augment-prefix]: Both examples are canonical, and the obvious third one is not. *Udapādi* has 840 root-text occurrences (it is the verb of *cakkhuṁ udapādi* at SN 56.11 — S v 422, quoted in Chapters 3 and 4), and DPD constructs it *ud* + *a* + √*pad* + *i*; *samabhavi* is DPD's *saṁ* + *a* + *bhava* + *i*. But *paccadesesi* 'he confessed' does not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all, and neither does any other augmented aorist of *paṭideseti*: the verb is the Vinaya's own word for confessing an offence (*paṭidesemī* has 16 root-text occurrences and the gerundive *paṭidesetabbaṁ* 11), and the canon simply never puts it in this tense. The form is well made and it is a grammarians' construction, so it is not used here. Chapter 14 makes the same cross-reference with *udapādi*.

[^augment-rule]: Perniola §82, pp. 91–92, whose subsections a–d grade it by aorist type (always for the root aorist, generally for the other three), with two further conditions at e and f: the augment is often dropped after a prefix ending in *-a*, and in verse its use "often depends on the requirements of the metre". Geiger §158 (p. 153) sets out a five-part rule he credits to Wackernagel, *Wortumfang und Wortform*, GN 1906, pp. 154 ff., graded by syllable count: monosyllabic forms keep the augment (*adaṁ* 'I gave', *agā* 'he went'); dissyllabic forms from the imperfect, the simple aorist or the *s*-aorist keep it "also in the later language" (*adāsi*, *avocuṁ*); dissyllabic forms from the *is*-aorist take it arbitrarily in the two oldest periods and drop it in post-canonical prose; trisyllabic forms of the extended type keep it (*agamāsi*); and everything else of three syllables or more began early to drop it, at random first and then as a rule. Oberlies §48, p. 242, gives the same rule in three parts, also after Wackernagel: the augment is prefixed where the aorist would otherwise be monosyllabic (*adā*); it is used with all disyllabic aorists except continuations of the *is*-aorist, where it is facultative (*alabhi* or *labhi*); and it is facultative with longer aorists, generally apart from those enlarged within Pāli (*agamāsi*). Thānuttamo p. 94 says the augment is frequently left out of the aorist and gives, from Oberlies (2019), the two plainest cases where it is used, a disyllabic aorist and one that would otherwise be monosyllabic; Duroiselle §417, p. 172, gives examples on either side and says only that the augment is "of much more frequent occurrence in prose than in poetry", where metre decides it. That a rule exists at all matters, because an augment described only as "frequently left out" invites a reader to think the two forms are free variants of each other, and in the third person singular they are not: *agamā* and *gamā* do not occur in equal numbers.

### The aorist

Pāli had inherited three separate past tenses from Old Indic: the imperfect, the perfect and the aorist. It kept one. The aorist absorbed the work of all three, and it is the ordinary past tense of the canon; a page of narrative prose is made of it. Oberlies accordingly heads his §48 "the preterite", not "the aorist", because in Pāli the two words have come to mean the same thing.

The complication is that the aorist is four formations, not one. Old Indic formed its aorist in several ways, and Pāli inherited the several ways without inheriting the reasons for choosing between them, so which type a verb takes is a fact about that verb and has to be learnt with it. The four are conventionally numbered, and Appendix A.3 sets out all four in full. The account below gives what a reader needs in order to recognise them. To learn the commonest forms before the system, begin at the end of the chapter instead, under "Irregular finite verbs", where five verbs are given whose aorists are best learnt whole.

Two features run through all four. Every type uses the **secondary endings**: the shorter set, contrasted in Chapter 7 with the primary endings that the present and the future take. And in every type the third person singular is the form a reader meets most, because so much of the canon is narrative about what somebody did.

#### Type 1: the root aorist

The endings go straight onto the root, with the augment, and no other element comes between.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-ṁ* |
| 2 sg. | *-o*, *-ā* |
| 3 sg. | *-ā* |
| 1 pl. | *-mhā*, *-mha* |
| 2 pl. | *-ttha* |
| 3 pl. | *-ū*, *-u*, *-uṁ* |

This is the oldest type and the smallest: it survives on a handful of roots and no more. Perniola names them (*dā-* 'give', *gā-* 'go', *ṭhā-* 'stand', *bhū-* 'be', *kar-* 'do'), giving *adā*, *agā*, *aṭṭhā*, *ahu*, *akā*.[^root-aorist-set] Those five are among the commonest verbs in the language, so a type with almost no members is nevertheless met constantly. In the first and second person plural it has no forms at all; the gap is filled from the other types.

[^root-aorist-set]: Perniola §83, p. 92. He notes that *bhū-* loses its initial mute consonant and keeps only the aspiration, giving *a-hu*, and that *kar-* takes the *guṇa* grade: *a-kar-t* > *akart* > *akatt* > *akā*. All five are thoroughly canonical: *adā* 43 occurrences in the root texts, *agā* 11, *aṭṭhā* 6, *ahu* 460, *akā* 15.

#### Type 2: the *a*-aorist

A vowel *-a-* is inserted between root and ending. This is the type that produces the most irregular-looking forms, because the root itself is often reshaped in the process.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-aṁ*, *-a*, *-ā* |
| 2 sg. | *-o*, *-a*, *-ā*, *-asi* |
| 3 sg. | *-ā*, *-a* |
| 1 pl. | *-āma*, *-amhā* |
| 2 pl. | *-attha* |
| 3 pl. | *-uṁ* |

The single most useful form in the whole chapter belongs here. *Vac-* 'speak' has the *a*-aorist *avoca* 'he said', and it is everywhere in the canon, because it is what stands at the end of the formula introducing direct speech:

- *Atha kho te bhikkhū sāvatthiyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkamiṁsu; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavantaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdiṁsu. Ekamantaṁ nisinnā kho te bhikkhū bhagavantaṁ etadavocuṁ.* (AN 10.27 — A v 49)
- *atha kho* then · *te* those (m. nom. pl.) · *bhikkhū* monks (m. nom. pl.) · *sāvatthiyaṁ* in Sāvatthī (f. loc. sg.) · *piṇḍāya* for alms (m. dat. sg.) · *caritvā* having wandered · *pacchābhattaṁ* after the meal · *piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā* returned from the almsround (m. nom. pl.) · *yena … tena* to where · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *upasaṅkamiṁsu* they approached (3 pl. aor.) · *upasaṅkamitvā* having approached · *bhagavantaṁ* the Blessed One (m. acc. sg.) · *abhivādetvā* having paid homage · *ekamantaṁ* to one side · *nisīdiṁsu* they sat down (3 pl. aor.) · *nisinnā* seated (m. nom. pl.) · *etadavocuṁ* < *etaṁ* this (n. acc. sg.) + *avocuṁ* they said (3 pl. aor.)
- "Then those monks, having wandered for alms in Sāvatthī and returned from the almsround after the meal, approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to him, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those monks said this to the Blessed One."

Three aorists in one sentence, two of them type 4 and the third *avocuṁ*. Note that *etadavocuṁ* is one printed word and two: the *-d-* is the old stem-final of *etad* surfacing before a vowel, as Chapter 2 sets out. Unless you resolve that junction you will look for a verb *davocuṁ* and not find it.

*Avoca* needs a note of its own, because its shape is not obvious. Perniola derives it as a reduplicated and weakened aorist: *a-va-vac-a-t* > *a-va-uc-a-t* > *avoca*, the first syllable of the root doubled, then weakened to *-uc-*, then contracted.[^avoca] Nothing of *vac-* is left on the surface, and the verb has no present in *vac-* either: its present is *vadati* and its passive *vuccati*. The three have to be learnt as a set.

[^avoca]: Perniola §84, p. 93, in the unnumbered paragraph on *vac-* that follows his subsection (b); (b) itself is about the roots that lengthen or alter their vowel, and carries none of this. Its paradigm, as he gives it, is *avocaṁ*, *avoca*, *avoca*, *avocumha*, *avocuttha*, *avocuṁ*, the first and second plural taking *-u-* and the *s* of the *s*-aorist. Of these, *avocuttha* is not in the root texts, appearing only in the commentaries; the canon uses *avacuttha* instead, 24 occurrences.

The verb 'see' also belongs here, and its aorist is the likeliest to stop a reader. *Dassati* 'sees' has the *a*-aorist *addasā* 'he saw' (290 occurrences in the root texts), first person *addasaṁ*, from *a-dras-s-at*, with everything assimilated.

#### Type 3: the *s*-aorist

An *-s-* is inserted between the root or stem and the ending. This type produces the *-si* forms, and it needs watching, because the third person singular *-si* is identical to the second person singular of the present indicative.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-siṁ* |
| 2 sg. | *-si* |
| 3 sg. | *-si* |
| 1 pl. | *-simha*, *-mha* |
| 2 pl. | *-sittha*, *-ttha* |
| 3 pl. | *-suṁ*, *-ṁsu* |

So *desesi* is either 'he taught' or 'you teach', and only the sentence decides. In practice the aorist reading is far commoner, because *deseti* is a verb of narrative; but the ambiguity is real and it recurs in every verb of the *-e-* class.

The verbs that take this type include several of the highest frequency: *karoti* 'does' has *akāsi*, *suṇāti* 'hears' has *assosi*, *tiṭṭhati* 'stands' has *aṭṭhāsi*, *bhāsati* 'speaks' has *abhāsi*, *gacchati* 'goes' has *agamāsi* beside its root aorist *agā*.

- *Atha kho rājā māgadho ajātasattu vedehiputto yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā ekamantaṁ aṭṭhāsi.* (DN 2 — D i 50)
- *atha kho* then · *rājā* the king (m. nom. sg.) · *māgadho* of Magadha (m. nom. sg.) · *ajātasattu* Ajātasattu (m. nom. sg.) · *vedehiputto* son of Vedehī (m. nom. sg.) · *yena … tena* to where · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *upasaṅkami* he approached (3 sg. aor.) · *upasaṅkamitvā* having approached · *ekamantaṁ* to one side · *aṭṭhāsi* he stood (3 sg. aor.)
- "Then Ajātasattu, king of Magadha, son of Vedehī, approached the Blessed One; having approached, he stood to one side."

Two aorists of two different types in one sentence (*upasaṅkami* of type 4, *aṭṭhāsi* of type 3), and neither is marked as anything but past.

#### Type 4: the *is*-aorist

An *-i-* or *-is-* is inserted between the stem and the ending. Appendix A.3, following Ñāṇatusita, calls this the *i*-aorist; Perniola and Oberlies call it the *is*-aorist, after the Sanskrit suffix it descends from. They are the same thing.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-iṁ*, *-isaṁ* |
| 2 sg. | *-i* |
| 3 sg. | *-i* |
| 1 pl. | *-imha*, *-imhā* |
| 2 pl. | *-ittha* |
| 3 pl. | *-iṁsu*, *-isuṁ* |

Learn this type first. Perniola states outright what the other sources leave a reader to infer: formed from the present stem, the *is*-aorist "has become the most common aorist in Pāli and can be formed from any present stem ending in *-a*".[^is-productive] It alone is still alive: a verb that needs an aorist and has no inherited one takes this. So if you know a verb's present stem you already know its most likely aorist, and the other three types are then a list of exceptions, not a system to be memorised in parallel.

[^is-productive]: Perniola §87c, p. 98, whose examples are *cintaya-* : *cintayi*, *pūjaya-* : *pūjayi*, *pāpuṇa-* : *pāpuṇi*. He adds that it is also formed from the present stem of passive verbs, which is how a passive gets a past tense at all; see Chapter 9. Of the three examples he gives there, only *vimucci* is canonical (24 occurrences in the root texts): *chijji* is in the commentaries alone and *muccimsu* occurs nowhere at all.

The paradigm on *upasaṅkamati* 'approaches', whose stem is *upasaṅkama-*:

|     | singular | plural |
|-----|----------|--------|
| 1st | *upasaṅkamiṁ* 'I approached' | *upasaṅkamimha* 'we approached' |
| 2nd | *upasaṅkami* 'you approached' | *upasaṅkamittha* 'you approached' |
| 3rd | *upasaṅkami* 'he, she, it approached' | *upasaṅkamiṁsu* 'they approached' |

- *idha mayaṁ, bhante, yena assajipunabbasukā bhikkhū tenupasaṅkamimha; upasaṅkamitvā assajipunabbasuke bhikkhū etadavocumha.* (MN 70 — M i 474)
- *idha* here · *mayaṁ* we (nom. pl.) · *bhante* venerable sir (voc.) · *yena … tena* to where · *assajipunabbasukā* the monks of Assaji and Punabbasu (m. nom. pl.) · *bhikkhū* monks (m. nom. pl.) · *upasaṅkamimha* we approached (1 pl. aor.) · *upasaṅkamitvā* having approached · *assajipunabbasuke bhikkhū* those monks (m. acc. pl.) · *etadavocumha* < *etaṁ* this (n. acc. sg.) + *avocumha* we said (1 pl. aor.)
- "Here, venerable sir, we approached the monks of Assaji and Punabbasu; having approached, we said this to them."

That line holds something else, unrelated to the aorist. As a bare word, *upasaṅkamimha* does not occur in the canon at all, because the editions never print it bare. Every one of its eleven root-text occurrences is joined to the *tena* before it, as *tenupasaṅkamimha*.[^upasankamimha] A form can be perfectly ordinary Pāli and still be invisible to a word search, and the reason is always the same: sandhi.

[^upasankamimha]: Counted in the root texts of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. The same holds of *adesesi*, the augmented aorist of *deseti*: zero as a bare word, 34 occurrences as *dhammamadesesi* 'he taught the Dhamma'. Chapter 7 records the identical pattern for *asmi*.

#### What the aorist does

Chiefly, it reports the past: the simple past of a narrative, nine-tenths of its use. That use is easiest to watch in the Vinaya's case law, because it repeats a frame and changes one word at a time. Four consecutive cases under the rule against stealing open with the same eleven words:

- Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacittaṁ **uppādesi**. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)
- *tena samayena* at that time · *kho pana* now · *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *rajakattharaṇaṁ* to the dyers' drying-ground (n. acc. sg.) · *gantvā* having gone · *mahagghaṁ* costly (n. acc. sg.) · *dussaṁ* cloth (n. acc. sg.) · *passitvā* having seen · *theyyacittaṁ* a thought of theft (n. acc. sg.) · *uppādesi* produced (3 sg. aor.)
- "Now at that time a certain monk went to the dyers' drying-ground, saw a costly cloth, and **produced** a thought of theft."

- … theyyacitto **āmasi**. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — "… and, **with theft in mind, touched** it."
- … theyyacitto **phandāpesi**. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — "… and, with theft in mind, **made** it **stir**."
- … theyyacitto ṭhānā **cāvesi**. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — "… and, with theft in mind, **shifted** it from its place."

*Āmasi* is a type 4 aorist on the *-a-* stem of *āmasati*. The other three are type 3, on the *-e-* stems of three causatives: *uppādeti*, *phandāpeti* and *cāveti*. So the two commonest types stand in the same slot of the same sentence, four times over, while the rest of the page stays still.

The frame also settles the ambiguity the type 3 table warned about. *Uppādesi*, *phandāpesi* and *cāvesi* all have the form of a second person singular present. Not one of them can be one here: the subject is *aññataro bhikkhu*, nominative and third person, standing in the same place in all four sentences. The sentence decides.[^theyyacitta-shift]

[^theyyacitta-shift]: The compound moves as well, and it moves for a reason that belongs to another chapter. *Theyyacittaṁ* in the first sentence is the object of the verb, a neuter noun; *theyyacitto* in the other three agrees with the monk instead. DPD keeps the two apart as separate entries: *theyyacitta* neuter, 'intention to steal', and *theyyacitta* adjective, 'intending to steal, with theft in mind'. That is the possessive use of a compound, and Chapter 13 sets it out.

Two further uses of the aorist need naming because they will otherwise read as errors.

It also has the sense of a **present perfect**: *dhammaṁ desesiṁ* is at least as well rendered 'I have taught the Dhamma' as 'I taught it'. Pāli has no separate perfect in living use, so the aorist covers that ground too.

And after *mā* it loses its past sense entirely and forms a prohibition, which is treated below. This is a survival of the Old Indic injunctive, a form that was never past to begin with and merely looks like the aorist now, not a stretched use of the past.

The middle voice of the aorist is nearly extinct. Perniola finds only stray third-person singulars (*alattha* 'he obtained', from *labh-*) and, in the *is*-aorist, a scatter of second and third singulars: *maññittho*, *pucchittha*.[^aor-middle] A reader will meet these and should recognise them; nobody needs to produce them.

[^aor-middle]: Perniola §§86e, 87d. He files both stray third singulars under the *s*-aorist's attanopada, p. 96, adding that from *alattha* "a whole a-aorist has been built" at his §84b, where this chapter meets it. His other example there, *udapattha* from *uda-pat-*, does not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, and is not given here. *Maññittho* (4 occurrences in the root texts) and *pucchittha* (6) are canonical.

### The tenses that vanished

Old Indic had an imperfect and a perfect as well, and Pāli has the residue of both. Neither is productive, and a reader needs them only in order to parse what the texts happen to print.

Nothing useful can be said of the **imperfect**, and the frankest sources say so. Duroiselle's own account, which Thānuttamo quotes, is that the imperfect is supposed to be formed from the present stem and the aorist from the root, "however, this is not an absolute measure and, in the end, these two can hardly be told apart".[^imperfect] Where a grammar labels a form an imperfect and another labels the same form an aorist, nothing hangs on which is right.

[^imperfect]: Thānuttamo, "Imperfect", p. 96, citing Duroiselle's 1997 re-edition; the words quoted above are Thānuttamo's. In the 1921 edition the passage is §407, remark (a), p. 169: "But even this is not an absolute criterion and the fact remains that these two tenses can scarcely be differentiated." Geiger §120, p. 117, and Oberlies §48, p. 228, both fold the imperfect into the preterite for the same reason.

The **perfect** is different, because although it has all but disappeared, the forms that survive are extremely common. Perniola's judgement is that in the canonical books the perfect is represented by *āha* in the second and third person singular, and by *āhu*, *āhaṁsu* for the third plural.[^perfect] *Āha* 'he said', 'he has said' is one of the most frequent verb forms in the whole literature, and without knowing it as a perfect you will look in vain for a present *āhati*. Beside it stands *vidū* 'they knew', from *vid-*.

[^perfect]: Perniola §88, p. 98. He adds that *āhaṁsu* is itself built on the analogy of the aorist, and that the handful of further perfects in the post-canonical books (*jagāma*, *babhūva*) are straight borrowings from Sanskrit. Counted in the root texts: *āha* 277 occurrences, *āhu* 88, *āhaṁsu* 26, *vidū* 50, *viduṁ* 3. The last figure but one counts a string rather than a word: *vidū* is also an adjective 'wise' and a masculine noun 'a knower', and at least four of the fifty stand in the nominative singular masculine, not as a verb.

### The future

English has no true future tense: it builds one with a helping verb, 'he *will* go'. Pāli has a real one, formed by inserting *-(i)ssa-* between the stem and the personal ending.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-(i)ssāmi* |
| 2 sg. | *-(i)ssasi* |
| 3 sg. | *-(i)ssati* |
| 1 pl. | *-(i)ssāma* |
| 2 pl. | *-(i)ssatha* |
| 3 pl. | *-(i)ssanti* |

These are the present indicative's own endings, unchanged, on a future stem instead of the present stem: the primary endings of Chapter 7 with *-(i)ssa-* interposed. Perniola puts the consequence usefully: the future "is inflected like the present tense", and lengthens its vowel before the first-person endings for the same reason the present does.[^fut-inflect] Only the infix has to be learnt. Appendix A.3 completes the paradigm with a middle-voice future, built the same way on the middle's own present endings; by the future's time *attanopada* had all but stopped being used at all, and this book asks a reader to recognise the tense only in the active, as Chapter 7 already warned.

[^fut-inflect]: Perniola §80a, p. 90, who also notes that in the first person singular the final vowel is sometimes dropped: *labhissāmi* beside *labhissaṁ*.

On *gacchati* 'goes', whose stem is *gaccha-*:

|     | singular | plural |
|-----|----------|--------|
| 1st | *gacchissāmi* 'I will go' | *gacchissāma* 'we will go' |
| 2nd | *gacchissasi* 'you will go' | *gacchissatha* 'you will go' |
| 3rd | *gacchissati* 'he, she, it will go' | *gacchissanti* 'they will go' |

- *… yaṁ kammaṁ karissāmi—kalyāṇaṁ vā pāpakaṁ vā—tassa dāyādo bhavissāmi.* (AN 5.57 — A iii 74)
- *yaṁ* which (n. acc. sg.) · *kammaṁ* deed (n. acc. sg.) · *karissāmi* I will do (1 sg. fut.) · *kalyāṇaṁ* good (n. acc. sg.) · *vā* or · *pāpakaṁ* bad (n. acc. sg.) · *vā* or · *tassa* of that (n. gen. sg.) · *dāyādo* heir (m. nom. sg.) · *bhavissāmi* I will be (1 sg. fut.)
- "Whatever deed I do, good or bad, of that I shall be the heir."

#### When the *-ss-* disappears

A future built directly on a root ending in a consonant assimilates: the *s* meets the final consonant of the root and the two collapse into a double consonant that is no longer *-ss-*. The result is a set of futures that do not look like futures at all, and several are common enough to learn as vocabulary.

| Future | Gloss | From |
|---|---|---|
| *dakkhati* | will see | *dassati* (*dṛś-*) |
| *lacchati* | will get | *labhati* |
| *vakkhati* | will say | *vadati* (*vac-*) |
| *bhecchati* | will split | *bhindati* |
| *sakkhissati* | will be able | *sakkoti* |

- *Atthi, kassapa, maggo atthi paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipanno sāmaṁyeva ñassati sāmaṁ dakkhati.* (DN 8 — D i 165)
- *atthi* there is (3 sg. pres.) · *kassapa* Kassapa (m. voc. sg.) · *maggo* a path (m. nom. sg.) · *paṭipadā* a practice (f. nom. sg.) · *yathāpaṭipanno* one practising in accordance with which (m. nom. sg.) · *sāmaṁyeva* < *sāmaṁ* by oneself + *eva* indeed, joined by the hiatus glide *y* · *ñassati* will know (3 sg. fut.) · *sāmaṁ* by oneself · *dakkhati* will see (3 sg. fut.)
- "There is a path, Kassapa, there is a practice, practising in accordance with which one will know for oneself, will see for oneself."

Two irregular futures side by side. *Ñassati* 'will know', from *jānāti*, is the other kind of irregularity: a future built straight on the root *ñā-*, not on the present stem, and not an assimilation, so that *jānissati* and *ñassati* both exist and mean the same thing.

> **The editions differ here.** At this line the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *dakkhati* and records in its own apparatus that the Sinhalese edition reads *dakkhiti*; the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print *dakkhiti*, which is what that note predicts. The variation is in the ending, not the stem (a competing *-iti* future beside *-ati*), and the same alternation gives *hessati* beside *hehiti*. No doctrine depends on it, but anyone comparing two printed editions will meet it.

In verse a further set of contracted futures appears, in which the *-ss-* has weakened to *-h-*. The two commonest verbs in the language both have them: *bhavati* gives *hessati*, *hehiti*, and *karoti* gives *kāhati*, *kāhāmi*, *kāhasi*.[^h-futures] They are metrically convenient, a syllable shorter, and that largely explains why they exist.

[^h-futures]: Perniola §80e, p. 90, traces the chain: *bhossati* > *hossati* > *hosati* > *hohati* > *hohiti*, and *bhavissati* > *havissati* > *hessati*; likewise *karssati* > *kāssati* > *kāsati* > *kāhati* > *kāhiti*. The intervocalic sibilant becoming *-h-* is a regular Middle Indic change. The endpoints are canonical and most of the steps between them are not. *Hessati* has 76 occurrences in the root texts and *hehiti* 22, and *hohiti*, *kāhati*, *kāhiti*, *kāhāmi* and *kāhasi* stand in the root texts beside them; but *bhossati*, *hosati*, *hohati*, *havissati*, *karssati* and *kāssati* do not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all, and *hossati* and *kāsati* only outside the root texts. Those are Perniola's reconstruction of a mechanism, not forms to learn.

#### What the future does

Future time, chiefly. But it is also the ordinary way of stating a **general law**: what always happens, put more emphatically than the present would put it: *manussā marissanti*, 'human beings will die'. And it draws an **inference**: *na vatimāni manussabhūtassa padāni bhavissanti* (AN 4.36 — A ii 37), 'these cannot be the footprints of a human being', where nothing about the future is meant at all and the sense is 'it must be that'.[^fut-uses] That idiom is frozen in *bhavissati* 'it must be' and, negated, *na bhavissati* 'it cannot be'.

[^fut-uses]: Thānuttamo, "Future Indicative — Usage", p. 98, drawing the general-law and inference uses from Collins p. 91 and Warder p. 55, and the idioms from Duroiselle (§613, pp. 321–322 in the 1921 edition) and Oberlies (2019) pp. 448 n. 3 and 478 n. 2. He lists a further set of attitudinal uses (regret, disapproval, surprise, determination, habit) after Gair and Karunatillake p. 127; those are matters of tone and are best learnt from reading rather than from a list.

### The conditional

The conditional is the rarest finite form in the language and the easiest to build, because it is the future with a past ending on it: take the future stem, prefix the augment, and use the secondary endings instead of the primary ones.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *-(i)ssaṁ* |
| 2 sg. | *-(i)ssa*, *-(i)sse* |
| 3 sg. | *-(i)ssā*, *-(i)ssa* |
| 1 pl. | *-(i)ssāma*, *-(i)ssamhā* |
| 2 pl. | *-(i)ssatha* |
| 3 pl. | *-(i)ssaṁsu* |

Its sense follows from its form. A future seen from a point in the past is something that was going to happen and did not, so the conditional is the **counterfactual**: 'if he had …, then he would have …'. Oberlies notes that the optative usually stands in for it, and so the conditional is scarce.[^cond-rare]

[^cond-rare]: Warder p. 331: the conditional "tense is rarely used", though it appears regularly when a false or impossible hypothesis is stated. Thānuttamo p. 99 reports this, and adds from Oberlies (2019) p. 502 that the optative usually stands in for the conditional. Oberlies §50, p. 250, gives the forms the canon attests. Perniola §81, p. 91, gives the paradigm on *labhati* (*alabhissaṁ*, *alabhissa*, *alabhissāma*, *alabhissatha*, *alabhissaṁsu*), and not one of the five stands anywhere in the root texts of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. Two of them do occur outside the canon: *alabhissa* five times in the commentaries and six in the grammarians' texts, *alabhissaṁsu* five times in the grammarians' alone. Geiger §157 cites *alabhissa* from the Dhammapada commentary and *alabhissāma* from the Jātaka commentary, reading the Pali Text Society's text, not this one. So it is a paradigm the canon does not use and the commentators occasionally did. The endings above are Appendix A.3's active endings; the verb they are shown on here is *bhavati*, whose conditional *abhavissa* has 143 occurrences in the root texts and is the form a reader will meet. Appendix A.3 also completes a middle-voice conditional, on the same rule as the middle future above; if the active is this rare, the middle is rarer still, and this book teaches only the active.

- *No cedaṁ, bhikkhave, paṇḍito sucintitacintī ca abhavissa subhāsitabhāsī ca sukatakammakārī ca, kena naṁ paṇḍitā jāneyyuṁ.* (AN 3.3 — A i 102)
- *no* not · *ce* if · *idaṁ* this (n. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *paṇḍito* the astute person (m. nom. sg.) · *sucintitacintī* one who thinks well-thought thoughts (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *abhavissa* were to be, had been (3 sg. cond.) · *subhāsitabhāsī* one who speaks well-spoken speech (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *sukatakammakārī* one who does well-done deeds (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *kena* by what (n. inst. sg.) · *naṁ* him (m. acc. sg.) · *paṇḍitā* the astute (m. nom. pl.) · *jāneyyuṁ* would know (3 pl. opt.)
- "If, monks, an astute person were not one who thinks well, speaks well and acts well, by what would the astute know him?"

The construction is fixed: the conditional stands in the protasis, the clause carrying the condition, and the **optative in the apodosis**, the clause carrying the consequence. That division of labour is the normal one, though either mood can appear on either side.

### Negation and prohibition

Three words do this work, and they are not interchangeable.

***Na*** negates a finite verb, and it is the ordinary negative.

- *… yāvassa bhikkhū āpattiṁ na passanti.* (AN 8.10 — A iv 169)
- *yāva* as long as · *assa* for him (m. gen. sg.) · *bhikkhū* the monks (m. nom. pl.) · *āpattiṁ* the offence (f. acc. sg.) · *na* not · *passanti* they see (3 pl. pres.)
- "… as long as the monks do not see an offence of his."

***No*** has the same function with more weight behind it, and negates a whole statement instead of a verb: *no hetaṁ, bhante*, 'certainly not, venerable sir'.

***Mā*** forms a prohibition: a distinct word with its own syntax, not a negative at all. **It takes the aorist**, and the aorist then loses its past sense entirely:

- *Alaṁ, puṇṇa, tiṭṭhatetaṁ; mā maṁ etaṁ pucchī'ti.* (MN 57 — M i 387)
- *alaṁ* enough · *puṇṇa* Puṇṇa (m. voc. sg.) · *tiṭṭhatetaṁ* < *tiṭṭhatu* let it stand (3 sg. imper.) + *etaṁ* this (n. nom. sg.) · *mā* do not · *maṁ* me (acc. sg.) · *etaṁ* this (n. acc. sg.) · *pucchi* ask (2 sg. aor.)
- "Enough, Puṇṇa, let it be. Do not ask me that."

- *Mā bhāyi, vakkali.* (SN 22.87 — S iii 122)
- *mā* do not · *bhāyi* fear (2 sg. aor.) · *vakkali* Vakkali (m. voc. sg.)
- "Do not fear, Vakkali."

This is the standing construction, and so an aorist in the canon is not automatically a past tense. *Mā* also occurs with the optative (*mā, bhikkhave, pāpake akusale vitakke vitakkeyyātha*, 'monks, do not think bad, unskilful thoughts', SN 56.7 — S v 417), where the prohibition is softened towards advice. And it shades from prohibition into wish: *mā bhāyi* is as much 'may you not be afraid' as 'don't be afraid', and the setting decides which.

Beside *mā*, the negated optative of Chapter 7 states a rule about what one ought not to do: *na seveyya* 'one should not resort to'. The two come out as 'don't' in English and are different constructions: *mā* forbids a particular act, *na* + optative lays down a precept. All three of these words are indeclinables, and Chapter 12 takes them again as a word class, with *alaṁ* 'enough' beside them and the negative prefix *a-*, which negates with no particle at all.

### Irregular finite verbs

A minority of verbs are irregular in the present; a much larger number are irregular in the aorist, and the four types above are the reason. Five of the commonest verbs in the canon have aorists that cannot be worked out from the present, and these are best learnt whole.

| Verb | 1 sg. | 2 sg. | 3 sg. | 1 pl. | 2 pl. | 3 pl. |
|------|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| *gacchati* 'goes' | *agamāsiṁ*, *agaṁ* | *agamā* | *agamā*, *agā* | — | *agamittha* | *agamaṁsu* |
| *dassati* 'sees'[^dassati-gloss] | *addasaṁ* | *addasā* | *addasā*, *addasa* | *addasāma* | — | *addasaṁsu* |
| *vadati* 'says' | *avocaṁ* | *avoca* | *avoca* | *avocumha*, *avocumhā* | *avacuttha* | *avocuṁ* |
| *hoti* 'is, becomes' | *ahosiṁ*, *ahuṁ* | *ahosi*, *ahū* | *ahosi*, *ahu* | *ahesumha* | *ahuvattha* | *ahesuṁ*, *ahuṁ* |
| *karoti* 'does' | *akāsiṁ* | *akāsi* | *akāsi*, *akā* | *akaramhā* | *akattha* | *akaṁsu* |

The dashes are not omissions. Four forms that the grammars print for these verbs are not in the canon, and none of them is given here: *agamhā* and *addasatha* do not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, and *aguttha* and *agū* only in the grammarians' own works.[^irreg-gaps] Two of those four cells had a canonical form to put in their place, *agamittha* and *agamaṁsu* above. Two did not, and stand empty. A paradigm with holes in it is a truer picture of the language than one completed by rule, when the paradigm is of a single irregular word rather than a display of endings.

[^dassati-gloss]: The table gives the verb as *dassati* 'sees', not *dissati*. *Dissati* is the **passive**, 'is seen', as Chapter 9 has it, deriving *dissati* from *dassati* 'sees'. The aorist series *addasaṁ*, *addasā* belongs to the active verb. A gloss of *dissati* as 'sees' is not invented, though: DPD carries a fourth *dissati* meaning 'sees', and its one source is Kaccāyana's *Ākhyātakappa* 471, where *passa*, *dissa* and *dakkha* are listed as substitutes for the root *disa*. It is a form the grammarians licence and the canon does not use in that sense.

[^irreg-gaps]: Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. Not in the root texts: *agamhā* occurs nowhere, and neither does *addasatha*; *aguttha* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 1) and *agū* (root texts 0, aṭṭhakathā 0, ṭīkā 0, later works 3) are in the grammarians' texts only. *Avocuttha* is likewise left out: it is commentary-only against 24 root-text occurrences of *avacuttha*. Perniola §83, p. 92, supplies the structural reason behind the first of these gaps, and states it more widely than this book needs: the root aorist has neither a first nor a second person plural. The *gacchati* row shows the consequence and the repair together: the second plural is filled from the *a*-aorist (*agamittha*), and the first is not, because the form that would fill it is unattested.

#### How the page prints them

A table gives each aorist as a free word, and that is not how a reader meets the commonest of them. Counted as the words stand in the root texts:[^page-prints-counts]

| As a free word | Inside a junction |
|---|---|
| *avoca* 245 | *etadavoca* 3,091 · *idamavoca* 302 |
| *ahosi* 1,559 | *etadahosi* 1,000 |
| *upasaṅkami* 29 | *tenupasaṅkami* 1,671 |

*Avoca* is the starkest case. For every time it stands alone in the canon, *etadavoca* 'said this' stands more than twelve times, and having learnt the form from the table above you will not see it on the page until the junction of Chapter 2 is undone. *Upasaṅkami* 'approached' is not irregular at all; it is here because it hides in the same way, joined to the *tena* of the stock approach, *yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami*.

Nor are the five irregulars the five commonest aorists. *Nisīdi* 'sat down' stands 1,078 times as a free word and *udapādi* 'arose' 840, both regular, and each outnumbers *addasā* (290), *akāsi* (143) and *agamāsi* (139). These five are to be learnt whole because they cannot be worked out, not because nothing is commoner.

### One habit at the page

Everything in this chapter comes down to one habit at the page. Faced with a verb you do not know, look for the augment, remembering that it stands after a prefix and not in front of it; then ask what is left, and which of the four types that leaves. Where the form ends in *-si*, do not choose between 'he taught' and 'you teach' from the word itself. Read the subject. And where a common verb will not parse at all, undo the junction before reaching for the dictionary.
