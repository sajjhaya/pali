## Word Formation: Prefixes and Suffixes

The last chapter was about words joined together. This one is about words built up: the pieces that go on the front and the back of a stem, and what they do to it. The two are the same operation seen from different distances, so the tradition treats them side by side, and this book does too.

The reader's problem here differs from the last chapter's. A compound is long and looks difficult and is in no dictionary; a derivative is short, looks easy, and is in the dictionary under a spelling one would not have guessed. *Nepuññaṁ* 'skill' is *nipuṇo* 'skilful' with a suffix, and it is filed under *ne-*. *Ākiñcaññaṁ* 'nothingness' is *akiñcano* 'having nothing' with a suffix, and it is filed under *ā-*. Derivation moves the front of the word, and a dictionary is looked up by the front of the word. That fact matters more to a reader than any list of suffixes, and this chapter is arranged to reach it early.

Before any of it, though, there is something the tradition says about words that a Buddhist grammar has no business leaving out.

### There are no roots in reality

All the schools of Buddhism hold that language is **conventional**: *vohāra*, agreement, usage. Sounds mean nothing in themselves; meanings are assigned to groups of sounds by social convention, and those meanings then drift through use, which the tradition calls *rūḷhi*. The Brahmanical view current at the time was the opposite: the Vedic language is eternal, and existed before anyone used it. On the Buddhist view, as Warder puts it, "there are no roots in reality, but we find it convenient to group words around them for descriptive purposes."[^wf-vohara]

[^wf-vohara]: Warder pp. 250–251. He adds, on the second of them, that some Brahmanical grammarians held the same about analysis generally (that in reality only sentences exist), so the disagreement is not simply Buddhist against Brahmanical. The point that matters for this chapter is that the tradition whose grammar we are learning did not think a word's derivation revealed its nature.

This has a practical consequence and a doctrinal one, and they point the same way. Practically: a derivation describes a word and does not decode it, and to take it for a decoding is to be wrong regularly. Doctrinally: the Buddha's own etymologies in the suttas are teaching devices, not claims about origins, and the grammar here gives no warrant for treating a word's parts as its truth. Derivation is good for recognition: seeing that a word one does not know is built out of two one does, and knowing where to look it up.

### Two divisions, and a residue

The tradition sorts derivation in two.[^wf-two-divisions]

A ***kita*** suffix is added **directly to a root** and makes a stem: √*kar* plus *-aka* gives *kāraka* 'doer'. The words so formed are the *kitakanāma*, the primary derivatives, and the class is larger than it looks: participles, absolutives and infinitives are all *kita* formations, so three chapters of this book have been teaching *kita* suffixes without using the name.

A ***taddhita*** suffix is added to a stem that already exists, whether that stem is itself a primary derivative or an ordinary noun: *sīla* plus *-vant* gives *sīlavant* 'virtuous'. These are the secondary derivatives, the *taddhitanāma*.

[^wf-two-divisions]: Duroiselle §563 for the division and the names; Perniola §108, p. 135; Warder p. 251; Thānuttamo p. 135, citing Kacc 601. Collins p. 104 gives the scale of the thing: "Aggavaṁsa devotes a full chapter to each, a total of 72 pages in Smith's edition." Kacc 624 adds a claim the Western sources do not make, that every *kita* affix carries the sense of agent, which is a statement about the tradition's analysis, not about Pāli, and is not followed here.

The division is real and it matters less than a grammar's table of contents suggests. Perniola states the reason flatly: "The distinction between primary and secondary suffixes is not of great importance since the same suffixes may be used both as primary and as secondary": *-a* is primary in one word and secondary in the next.[^wf-division-soft] Thānuttamo, whose whole chapter is organised by the division, opens it by quoting that sentence. So the two names are needed because the grammars use them, and they are not a reason to memorise a suffix inventory twice.

[^wf-division-soft]: Perniola §108, p. 136, quoted by Thānuttamo p. 135. His own pair is *khipa* against *sīlavanta*, neither of which occurs in the canon; the point stands on *-a* in *cāga* against *-a* in *sātthaṁ*.

There is a third class, and how the sources handle it is more instructive than the class itself. Kaccāyana recognises the ***uṇādi*** affixes (named for the first of them, *uṇ*, plus *ādi* 'and the rest') for words whose connection to a root is, in Duroiselle's words, "not clear either in meaning or in form". He then declines to give them a chapter, folding them in with the *kita* suffixes and marking each with an asterisk.[^wf-unadi] That is a frank way of saying that the class is a residue: what is left when the analysis has done what it can. A reader meets its members constantly and never needs the label.

[^wf-unadi]: Duroiselle §565. Thānuttamo gives them a short chapter of their own, pp. 148–150. The asterisk convention is Duroiselle's own, and anyone consulting his lists needs it: an asterisked entry is a warning that the derivation is a formality.

### Prefixes

Pāli has a closed class of about twenty prefixes, and closed classes are good news: they can be learnt once. The tradition calls them *upasagga*, and Aggavaṁsa's etymology of the term is itself the definition: they "approach and adhere to" nouns and verbs, "specifying their meaning".[^wf-upasagga-name]

[^wf-upasagga-name]: Collins p. 125, translating Sadd 886: *upecca … sajanti lagganti tesaṁ atthaṁ visesenti ti upasaggā*. The count of twenty is Kacc 221's, given in full by Thānuttamo pp. 122–124 and independently by Collins §5.1 on Saddanīti's authority. Appendix A.4's prefix list, which comes from Ñāṇatusita, also has twenty entries but is not the same twenty: it admits the negative *a-*, which the tradition classes among the particles, not the prefixes, and it merges *ni-* and *nī-*, which Kaccāyana and Saddanīti keep apart. Neither list is wrong for its purpose (his is an inventory of what a reader meets at the front of a word), but they are lists of different things.

| Prefix | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *ati-* | beyond, across, excessively | *atikkamati* goes beyond |
| *adhi-* | over, onto, superior to | *adhigacchati* attains |
| *anu-* | after, along, in accordance with | *anugacchati* follows |
| *apa-* | away, off | *apeti* goes away |
| *api-* | on, over, close upon | *apidhānaṁ* a lid |
| *abhi-* | towards, facing, over | *abhikkamati* goes forward |
| *ava-*, *o-* | down, off | *okkamati* descends |
| *ā-* | to, near, as far as; reversal | *āgacchati* comes |
| *u-*, *ud-* | up, out, forth | *uggacchati* rises |
| *upa-* | to, near, alongside | *upagacchati* approaches |
| *du-*, *dur-* | badly, hard, wrongly | *dukkara* hard to do |
| *ni-* | down, into | *nisīdati* sits down |
| *nī-*, *nir-* | out, away, without | *nikkhamati* goes forth |
| *pa-* | forth, forward | *pabbajati* goes forth |
| *parā-* | away, opposed | *parābhavati* comes to ruin |
| *pari-* | around, completely | *parijānāti* fully understands |
| *pati-*, *paṭi-* | back, against, in return | *paṭipucchati* asks in return |
| *vi-* | apart, variously, without | *vigacchati* departs |
| *saṁ-* | together, fully | *saṅgacchati* meets |
| *su-* | well, easily, thoroughly | *sugati* a good destination |

Five of them also work as free words governing a case (*anu*, *apa*, *abhi*, *ā* and *paṭi*), and only those; the rest are bound.[^wf-free-prefixes] Chapter 12 gives the wider picture, which is that Pāli has almost no prepositions left and uses adverbs, nouns in oblique cases, and absolutives instead.

[^wf-free-prefixes]: Collins p. 126: *anu* with the accusative, *apa* with the ablative, *abhi* with the accusative, *ā* with the ablative, *paṭi* with the accusative. Thānuttamo reproduces the list at p. 124 and credits it.

#### What a prefix actually does

Here the tradition is more useful than any modern grammar, because it names three effects a prefix can have, not one. A prefix may be *dhātvatthavisesako*, "that which modifies or enhances the meaning of the root"; or *dhātvatthabādhako*, "that which debars the meaning of the root", reversing it; or *dhātvatthānuvattako*, "that which keeps on alongside the meaning of the root": a prefix that adds nothing at all.[^wf-three-types]

[^wf-three-types]: Thānuttamo p. 124, after Thitzana and Collins p. 125. The third name is the valuable one: it makes "this prefix means nothing here" a recognised category of the grammar, not a confession of defeat.

The third category saves a reader time. *Vigacchati* is *vi-* plus *gacchati*, and it does mean 'goes away'; *vipassati* is *vi-* plus *passati*, and it means 'sees clearly', with the *vi-* contributing what one would not have predicted; and there are dozens of verbs where the prefix contributes a colouring so faint that no gloss can catch it. The tradition's answer is that this is a fact about the language, not a failure of the reader.

Bodhi arrives at the same place from a sutta-reader's side and states the consequence: "often one cannot deduce the meaning of a verb simply by adding the nuance of the prefix to the meaning of the basic verb. It is thus advisable always to learn the meaning of a prefixed verb from a dictionary or glossary and to interpret it in context."[^wf-bodhi-warning]

[^wf-bodhi-warning]: Bodhi, *Reading the Buddha's Discourses in Pāli*, "Prefixes", pp. 22–23. His seventeen-prefix list omits *apa*, *api* and *parā* and separates *ni* from *nī*/*nir*: the practical selection, not the traditional class.

The clearest demonstration is a single root run through the prefixes. √*ñā* 'know' gives *jānāti* 'knows', and from it:

| Verb | Sense | Noun | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| *abhijānāti* | knows directly | *abhiññā* | direct knowledge |
| *ājānāti* | understands | — | — |
| *pajānāti* | discerns, understands | *paññā* | wisdom |
| *paṭijānāti* | acknowledges, claims | *paṭiññā* | a claim |
| *parijānāti* | fully understands | *pariññā* | full understanding |
| *vijānāti* | knows distinctly | *viññāṇa* | consciousness |
| *sañjānāti* | perceives | *saññā* | perception |

Seven prefixes, seven verbs, six nouns, and the nouns are six of the most doctrinally loaded words in the canon. Nothing in the prefixes would have told anyone that *saññā* is 'perception' while *viññāṇa* is 'consciousness'; the senses were fixed by use long before any grammarian looked at them. Bodhi notes that *viññāṇa* may once have carried the distinctness its prefix suggests, and that the nuance "has faded into the background" by the time of the Nikāyas.[^wf-nana-family] This is *rūḷhi*, drift through usage, happening in front of us, and it is the best argument in the chapter for learning words instead of deducing them.

[^wf-nana-family]: Bodhi, "Prefixes", p. 23, whose paragraph this table condenses. He makes the same observation about *saññā*, which "may have originally denoted a syncretic knowledge, a nuance lost by the time of the Nikāyas". *Ājānāti* is added here because a reader meets it constantly in *aññā*, 'final knowledge', and in the name Aññāsi Koṇḍañña.

#### What happens at the seam

Chapter 2 divided sandhi into junction inside a word and junction between words, and deferred most of the first kind to this chapter and the last. The join inside a compound was Chapter 13's. What is left is the join between a prefix and what follows it, and there are only a few things to know.

Some prefixes have a hidden final consonant which appears when a vowel follows and assimilates when a consonant does. Warder writes these with the consonant in brackets (*u(d)*, *du(r)*, *puna(d)*, *saki(d)*), a good habit to borrow.[^wf-brackets] So *ud-* plus *gacchati* gives *uggacchati*, the *d* assimilating to the following *g*; *dur-* plus *labha* gives *dullabha* 'hard to get', the *r* assimilating to the *l*; *nī-* plus √*kam* gives *nikkhamati* 'goes forth'; *puna(d)* plus *bhava* gives *punabbhava* 'renewed existence'. If you do not know the consonant is there you will look for *nikkhamati* under a root beginning with *kh*.

[^wf-brackets]: Warder p. 255, in his section on junction consonants. He is explicit that the line between a morphological final and a merely phonetic one is arbitrary, and that the medieval grammarians treat them all as phonetic; the brackets are his own compromise, and they are useful because they are visible.

Learn *saṁ-* in full, because it changes form more than any other and is everywhere. Before a vowel the *m* stays: *samaya*, *samādhi*, *samudaya*. Before a stop it becomes the nasal of that stop's own class: *saṅkhārā*, *sañjānāti*, *saṇṭhāna*, *sandiṭṭhika*. Before a labial it stays *m*: *samphassa*, *sambodhi*. Before *y*, *v*, *s* and *h* it becomes niggahīta: *saṁyojana*, *saṁvara*, *saṁsāra*, *saṁhita*. And before *l* it assimilates outright: *sallekha*.[^wf-sam] Seven forms, one prefix. Recognise them and you recover a great deal of vocabulary at a stroke.

[^wf-sam]: Bodhi, "The prefix *sam*", p. 18, which is the fullest treatment of a single prefix in any source consulted for this chapter and is written at a reader's pace, not a philologist's. Perniola §143, p. 178, adds one further oddity, which explains an aspirate that otherwise has no source: *saṁ* with *karoti* inserts an *-s-*, which leaves the *kh* of *abhisaṅkharoti* 'forms, concocts' and of *saṅkhāra* itself. DPD writes the construction without the *s*, as *abhi* + *saṁ* + √*kar* + *o* + *ti*, so the *s* is a historical explanation, not a segment anyone need posit; the Sanskrit is *saṁskaroti*.

**Prefixes stack**, and when they do they behave as one: *adhi* plus *ā* plus *vasati* gives *ajjhāvasati* 'inhabits'; *saṁ* plus *anu* plus *passati* gives *samanupassati* 'regards'; *saṁ* plus *ud* plus *ā* plus *carati* gives *samudācarati* 'behaves towards'.[^wf-stacking] Three prefixes on one root is not rare.

[^wf-stacking]: Perniola §143 N.B., p. 178. Chapter 8 makes the related point about the augment, which in a prefixed verb is inserted between the prefix and the stem, not at the front of the word: *ud* plus *a*- plus *pādi* gives *udapādi*.

#### Prefixes that are not *upasagga*

Three common word-initial elements are not in the traditional class, and the reason matters, because two of them behave differently.

**The negative *a-*, *an-*** before a vowel, is a particle, not a prefix, in the tradition's terms, and unlike an *upasagga* it attaches to almost anything: nouns, adjectives, participles, whole compounds. *Anāgata* 'not come, future', *avitatha* 'not unreal', *appaṭivedha* 'non-penetration'. Where the following word begins with a consonant cluster the vowel is often absorbed and the consonant doubles, which is how *paṭivedha* becomes *appaṭivedha*.[^wf-negative-a]

[^wf-negative-a]: Bodhi's note on *appaṭivedhā*, p. 71, traces the doubling to the *-r-* of Sanskrit *prati*, lost in Pāli but leaving the cluster behind. The same explanation covers *appamāda* 'diligence' and its relatives, and it is why the negative of a *p*-initial word so often has *pp*.

***Sa-*** 'with, having', and ***vīta-*** 'devoid of', are nouns and participles used as prefixes, and they turn what follows into an adjective, that is, into a *bahubbīhi*. *Sadevaka* 'with its gods', *samāraka* 'with Māra', *sabrahmaka* 'with Brahmā'; *vītarāga* 'free of lust'. Chapter 13 treats these under the *bahubbīhi*, which is where they belong.[^wf-sa-vita]

[^wf-sa-vita]: Bodhi, on the *saūmi sāvaṭṭa* passage, p. 32, and on *sadevake loke samārake sabrahmake*, p. 74, where the *-ka* is the adjectivalising suffix of the last section of this chapter and the *sa-* is the possessive prefix. Two pieces of word formation in one word, which is normal.

### Primary derivation: building on a root

The *kita* suffixes go on a root, and mainly they change its vowel. So the grades come first, and the two kinds of noun the suffixes make, the name of an action and the name of its doer, come after.

#### Three grades of the root vowel

A root can appear in a derivative in any of three grades, and the grade is often the only thing distinguishing two words that share a root and mean different things. Chapter 7 introduced the ladder for the verb; it is the same ladder here.[^wf-grades]

[^wf-grades]: Perniola §109(e), p. 137, extends to the root what his §109(a) has just said of the suffix: "Just as the suffix, so also the root may appear in its zero, guna or even vṛddhi grade." His §111, pp. 138–139, then sorts the whole of the *-a* derivation by the grade of the root (weak, then guṇa, then vṛddhi, with a fourth group built on the present stem instead), the fullest display of the rule in any source read. The gradation table itself is at Appendix A.3.

| Grade | Root | Derivative | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| weak | √*dis* | *disa* | seeing (only bound) |
| *guṇa* | √*budh* | *bodha* | understanding (only bound) |
| *guṇa* | √*gam* | *gama* | going (only bound) |
| *guṇa* | √*muh* | *moha* | delusion |
| *guṇa* | √*gah* | *gaha* | grasping |
| *vuddhi* | √*vad* | *vāda* | speech, doctrine |
| *vuddhi* | √*caj* | *cāga* | giving up, generosity |
| *vuddhi* | √*raj* | *rāga* | passion |
| *vuddhi* | √*har* | *hāra* | carrying |

The pair that makes the rule teachable is Warder's. From √*kar*, the plain suffix *-a* gives ***kara*** 'making, causing', with no change to the root; the suffix the grammarians write *ṇa* gives ***kāra*** 'maker, doer', with *vuddhi*. Two suffixes of identical shape, distinguished only by what they do to the vowel in front of them.[^wf-anubandha] *Kumbhakāro* is a potter; *dukkara* is 'hard to do'.

[^wf-anubandha]: The *ṇ* is not pronounced and is not part of the suffix. It is an *anubandha*, an "exponent": a fictitious letter attached to a suffix to tell it apart from another of the same shape, and *ṇ* is chosen because no Pāli word begins with it (Warder pp. 251–252). It signals *vuddhi*: Kacc 621 calls the *ṇ*-marked affixes the *kārita* affixes and defines them as those before which the root undergoes gradation (Thānuttamo p. 135). So a grammar's *ṇa*, *ṇvu*, *ṇya*, *ṇika* are a reader's *-a*, *-aka*, *-ya*, *-ika*, each with the vowel strengthened. DPD keeps the same distinction with a notation of its own: it gives *kara* as `√kar + a` and *kāra* as `√kar + *a`, the asterisk marking the suffix that strengthens. Anyone reading its construction field regularly is reading Kaccāyana's *ṇ* under another sign.

Two further pieces of notation from the same source explain a whole family of short words: the suffix the grammarians call *ra* elides the root's final consonant and its vowel, leaving *-ga* from √*gam* and *-ja* from √*jan*; and a **zero suffix**, called *kvi*, does the same with nothing added, so that √*gam* yields the *-ga* of *urago* 'snake', literally 'chest-goer'.[^wf-kvi] These are the bound final members Chapter 13 listed and could not explain: *-ga*, *-ja*, *-da*, *-ṭṭha*, *-pa*, *-ññū*, *-vidū*, *-bhū*. **They cannot stand alone.** They exist only at the end of a compound, and this is where they come from.

[^wf-kvi]: Warder p. 252, whose formulation is the clearest: "The words thus formed cannot stand alone, but only as the second members of compounds." He says it of the *ṇa* group, not of *kvi*, but it holds of both, and he prints his *kvi* examples with the hyphen that shows it: *ura-ga*, *saṁ-gha*. His own illustration of *kvi* on a consonant-final root is *saṁ-gha*, read as from √*han*; DPD assigns *saṅgha* instead to *saṁ-* + √*ghaṭ* + *a*, from the verb *saṅghaṭeti* 'joins together', with Sanskrit *saṁgha* [*saṁghaṭ*] beside it, and that reading is followed here. *Uragaṁ*, his other example, DPD confirms as *ura-* + *-ga*.

#### Action nouns

An action noun names the doing of the thing. English makes them with *-ing*, *-ment*, *-ation*; Pāli has four, and between them they account for an enormous share of the doctrinal vocabulary.

***-ana***, neuter, and ***-anā***, feminine, are the commonest and the most transparent. *Dassana* 'seeing', *sāsana* 'instruction', *ṭhāna* 'standing, place', *karaṇa* 'doing', *bhājana* 'vessel', *vāhana* 'carrying'; *vedanā* 'feeling', *cetanā* 'intention', *codanā* 'reproof'. After a root ending in *r* or *h* the *n* is retroflex: *kāraṇa*, *maraṇa*.[^wf-ana]

[^wf-ana]: Perniola §118, pp. 148–150; Thānuttamo, s.v. *-ana*, citing Kacc 549 for the retroflex rule and Kacc 622 for the tradition's name for this affix, *yu*, which becomes *ana*: another case where a grammar's affix and a reader's suffix look nothing alike. DPD parses *vedanā* as √*vid* + *anā*, literally 'causing to know', which is a useful corrective to deriving it from the finished verb *vedeti*.

***-ti***, feminine, is the second, and it misleads beginners, because a noun in *-ti* looks like a third-person singular verb. *Gati* 'going, destination', *mati* 'thought', *sati* 'mindfulness', *khanti* 'patience', *vimutti* 'liberation', *patti* 'attainment', *cuti* 'passing away', *ñāti* 'relative'. The root is normally in its weak grade, and the consonant meeting the *t* often assimilates, so *vimutti* does not look like *muñcati* and *ñatti* does not look like *ñāpeti*.[^wf-ti]

[^wf-ti]: Perniola §120, pp. 151–154. The test in practice is the sentence, not the word: a *-ti* noun takes case endings (*satiyā*, *satiṁ*, *vimuttiyā*) and a verb does not, so any inflected occurrence declares itself. It is the bare nominative that has to be read from context.

***-a*** with *vuddhi*, already met above, makes a mass of them: *vāda*, *cāga*, *rāga*, *hāra*, *vihāra*, *uppāda*. And ***-tā*** makes them from stems, not roots, which puts it in the next section.

#### Agent nouns

An agent noun names the doer. Four suffixes cover nearly all of them.

***-tar***, declined as at Appendix A.1, gives the agent nouns closest to the verb: *satthā* 'teacher' (√*sās*), *kattā* 'maker', *netā* 'leader', *dātā* 'giver', *bhāsitā* 'speaker'. These are Chapter 3's *r*-stems, and a reader meets the nominative in *-ā*.[^wf-tar]

[^wf-tar]: DPD gives *satthā* as √*sās* + *tar* + *ā*. The stem is *satthar*; the citation form in most grammars is the stem and the form in most texts is the nominative, a mismatch to expect, not to be surprised by.

***-aka***, which the grammarians call *ṇvu* and which therefore carries *vuddhi* where the root allows, gives adjectival agents that decline like ordinary *-a* stems: *kāraka* 'doer', *sāvaka* 'hearer, disciple', *dāyaka* 'giver', *yācaka* 'beggar', *upāsaka* 'lay follower'. The feminine is in *-ikā*: *upāsikā*, *dāyikā*, *paricārikā* 'attendant'.[^wf-aka]

[^wf-aka]: Perniola §122, pp. 154–156; Thānuttamo, s.v. *-aka*, citing Kacc 622 for *ṇvu* → *aka* and Kacc 527 for the sense. DPD parses *upāsaka* as *upa* + √*ās* + *aka*, 'one who sits near', the etymology the tradition itself gives, which the word's use (a layperson who attends on the Saṅgha) still carries.

***-in***, feminine *-inī*, is at once an agent suffix and a possessive one, and the difference is only in what it is added to: added to a root it makes a doer, added to a noun it makes a haver. *Gavesī* 'seeker', *dāyī* 'giver', *brahmacārī* 'one who lives the holy life', *musāvādī* 'one who speaks falsely'; and on the possessive side *saññī* 'having perception', *pakkhī* 'winged, a bird', *hatthī* 'having a hand, an elephant'. The stem is *-in* and the nominative singular is *-ī*, so a dictionary's *hatthin* and a text's *hatthī* are the same word.[^wf-in]

[^wf-in]: Warder p. 251 files *-in* among the primary suffixes ("possessive nouns (the *kita* suffix *-in*)") and then on p. 252 counts some of the possessives in *-in* among the secondary nouns instead, the blurring Perniola §108, p. 136, predicted. *Bhāginī* 'partaker (f.)' and *hatthinī* 'cow elephant' show the feminine. The related *-āvī*, feminine *-āvinī*, is rarer and expresses a settled habit: *bhayadassāvī*, 'one who is in the habit of seeing danger'.

***-ū***, finally, makes agent nouns straight from a verbal root: *pāragū* 'gone to the far shore', *vedagū* 'one who has attained knowledge', *kālaññū* 'knowing the right time', *atthaññū* 'knowing the meaning', *abhibhū* 'conqueror'. Four of the five are the bound finals Chapter 13 met, and the last is not: *abhibhū* is a prefix on a root, and it is a word in its own right. The suffix is not tied to compounding.[^wf-u-agent]

[^wf-u-agent]: Perniola §116, p. 147: "The suffix *-ū* is used to form agent nouns from a verbal root and to form some feminine stems", illustrated with *pāra-gū*, *kāla-ññū*, *abhi-bhū* and *vadhū*. He says nothing there about compounding, and two of his four illustrations are not compounds. The *-ññū* words are a set to learn together, because the canon uses them in lists: *dhammaññū*, *atthaññū*, *mattaññū*, *kālaññū* and the rest run through several suttas as a description of what a monk should be.

### Secondary derivation: building on a word

The *taddhita* suffixes go on a stem that already exists, and they strengthen a vowel too. But not the same one, and that difference makes a derivative hard to look up.

#### The vuddhi is at the front

Here is the rule this chapter exists to teach. When a secondary suffix is added, the first vowel of the word is strengthened, not the vowel next to the suffix.[^wf-front-vuddhi] The ladder is the ordinary one: *a* becomes *ā*, *i* becomes *e*, *u* becomes *o*. A vowel already long, or long by position because a cluster follows, stays as it is.

[^wf-front-vuddhi]: Duroiselle §580(iv) states it as a property of the class: "The guna affects mostly the first syllable of the word to which the suffix is added." Warder p. 253 says the same for his list of *ṇa*, *ṇeyya*, *(ṇ)ika*, *(ṇ)iya*, *(ṇ)aka*, *(ṇ)ya*, *ima* and *ssa*: "with strengthening (lengthening, *vuddhi*) of the first vowel of the word". The first two are unbracketed there because his footnote to the third announces that the exponents are bracketed only from that point on. Duroiselle's §580(iii) is its companion and is needed just as often: "The final vowel of a word is often elided before a Taddhita suffix."

| Base | Derivative | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| *kusalaṁ* 'skilled' | *kosallaṁ* | proficiency |
| *nipuṇo* 'skilful' | *nepuññaṁ* | skill |
| *paṇḍito* 'wise' | *paṇḍiccaṁ* | erudition |
| *puthujjano* 'ordinary person' | *pothujjaniko* | of the ordinary person |
| *punabbhavo* 'renewed existence' | *ponobhavikā* | leading to renewed existence |
| *gilāno* 'sick' | *gelaññaṁ* | sickness |
| *upa-* + √*nī* 'lead on' | *opaneyyika* | leading onwards |
| *nigamo* 'town' | *negamā* | townsfolk |
| *janapado* 'country' | *jānapado* | countryman |
| *garu* 'heavy, venerable' | *gāravo* | respect |
| *adhipati* 'lord' | *ādhipateyyaṁ* | lordship, dominion |

So the dictionary entry is where it is. Meet *nepuññaṁ*, look under *ni-*, and you will not find it; the word is filed under *ne-*, and the rule turns a dead end into a lookup.

It does not apply to everything, and one of the two exceptions needs stating. The possessive suffix *-in* does **not** take the front vuddhi: from *vicikicchā* 'doubt' the canon makes *vicikicchī* 'doubting', not *vecikicchī*.[^wf-no-vuddhi] Nor do the primary suffixes generally, which strengthen the root itself if they strengthen anything.

[^wf-no-vuddhi]: *Vicikicchī* has six root-text occurrences in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and *vecikicchī* one. The rule belongs to the *ṇ*-marked *taddhita* affixes, and *-in* is not one of them.

When the suffix is ***-ya***, a second thing happens on top of the vuddhi: the *y* assimilates to whatever consonant precedes it, and the results are so unlike their bases that the words look unrelated. A dental gives *-cca*, *ṇ* or *n* gives *-ñña*, *l* gives *-lla*, *d* gives *-jja*.

- *paṇḍito* 'wise' + *-ya* → *paṇḍiccaṁ* 'erudition'
- *samaṇo* 'ascetic' + *-ya* → *sāmaññaṁ* 'the ascetic life'
- *kusalaṁ* 'skilled' + *-ya* → *kosallaṁ* 'proficiency'
- *bahussuto* 'much-learned' + *-ya* → *bāhusaccaṁ* 'learning'
- *su-* + *mano* 'mind' + *-ya* → *somanassaṁ* 'gladness'
- *akiñcano* 'having nothing' + *-ya* → *ākiñcaññaṁ* 'nothingness'

*Sāmaññaṁ* and *ākiñcaññaṁ* are two of the most familiar words in the canon, and neither is recognisable from its base without the rule.[^wf-ya-assim]

[^wf-ya-assim]: Perniola §113(c), p. 142, for the assimilations, of which *satya* > *sacca* is his own; the vuddhi is in the unnumbered paragraph following that subsection, p. 143, where he states that the secondary neuter nouns made from adjectives this way "take the vṛddhi of the first syllable unless the vowel is long by position" — and where two of the words this section discusses stand as his examples. Warder pp. 253–254 for *gelaññaṁ* < *gilāna* + *(ṇ)ya* and *ārogyaṁ* < *a* + *rogo* + *(ṇ)ya*, both given in his own citation forms. DPD parses *bāhusacca* as *bahu* + *suta* + *ya*, *somanassa* as *su* + √*man* + *as* + *ya*, and *paṇḍicca* simply as *paṇḍita* + *ya* with Sanskrit *pāṇḍitya* beside it. The assimilation *-ty-* → *-cc-* in the first and third is the same one that makes *sacca* out of Sanskrit *satya*, which Chapter 2 met from the phonological side.

#### Abstract nouns

Pāli makes abstract nouns freely, from any noun or adjective, and with the two commonest suffixes you can read a great many words never met before.

***-tā*** is always feminine and ***-tta***, or *-ttaṁ*, almost always neuter, and the two are often both available from the same stem.[^wf-ta-tta] *Suññatā* 'emptiness', *devatā* 'deity', *lahutā* 'lightness', *thiratā* 'firmness', *kataññutā* 'gratitude'; *manussatta* 'the state of being human', *ekatta* 'oneness', *sāvakatta* 'discipleship', *dukkhatā* 'painfulness'.

[^wf-ta-tta]: Warder pp. 252–253. *Nānatta* 'diversity' and *itthatta* 'this state of being' are two the canon uses constantly; *sattatta* 'existence' is a double abstract, *sant* plus *-tta* plus *-tta*, which shows how mechanical the process is.

*-tā* is distinctive in attaching to things much larger than a stem. It goes on a future passive participle (*karaṇīyatā* 'the being-to-be-done', *bhabbatā* 'possibility') and on a whole compound, where a reader meets it most: *appicchatā* 'having few wishes', *anāgāmitā* 'the state of non-return', *kammaññatā* 'workability', *katapuññatā* 'the fact of having made merit'.[^wf-ta-compound] English does the same thing with *-ness*, and just as unrestrictedly.

[^wf-ta-compound]: Perniola §120, pp. 151–154, whose list this follows. *Kammaññatā* is itself doubly derived (*kamma* plus *-ya* plus *-tā*), and *akammaññatara* adds a comparative on top of that, which is three suffixes on one stem.

The suffix ***-ya***, with the front vuddhi and the assimilations above, is the third abstract and the one that produces the least recognisable results. *Sāmañña*, *bāhusacca*, *ākiñcañña*, *ārogya* 'health', *dussīlya* 'unvirtuousness', *kāveyya* 'poetry'.

#### Having, belonging, descending

The remaining secondary suffixes sort by sense, not by form, and the classes overlap; what follows is the working set.

**Possession**, 'having X', is the largest class. ***-vant*** and ***-mant*** decline like the *(m/v)ant* stems at Appendix A.1 and are the commonest: *sīlavā* 'virtuous', *bhagavā* 'fortunate, the Blessed One', *paññavā* 'wise', *balavā* 'strong', *cakkhumā* 'having eyes', *satimā* 'mindful', *hirimā* 'having a sense of shame'. Note the nominative singular in *-ā*, the form a text prints; the *-vant* and *-mant* of a dictionary entry are stems.[^wf-vant] ***-in*** serves the same purpose, as above. ***-ika*** and ***-ita*** and a plain ***-a*** also form possessives (*daṇḍika* 'having a staff', *saddha* 'having faith', *pañña* 'having wisdom' in compounds), and ***-ālu*** expresses a settled disposition: *abhijjhālu* 'covetous by nature'.

[^wf-vant]: Chapter 4 gives the declension, and the feminine on the weak stem in *-tī*: *sīlavant* becomes *sīlavatī* 'virtuous'. The rule that separates these from present participles in *-ant* is the base: a participle is built on a present stem, a possessive on a noun.

**Belonging, connection, origin** are expressed by ***-ika***, which Thānuttamo lists under sixteen distinct senses with a Kaccāyana rule for each, and of ***-iya*** and ***-eyya***.[^wf-ika-senses] The senses read as a spread, not a list: *nāvika* 'sailor' (living by a boat), *kālika* 'temporal', *mānasika* 'mental', *kāyika* 'bodily', *dhammika* 'righteous', *cetasika* 'mental factor', *māgadhika* 'Magadhan', *nerayika* 'hell-bound', *attaniya* 'belonging to a self', *upādāniya* 'liable to clinging', *saṁyojaniya* 'liable to fetter', *dakkhiṇeyya* 'worthy of offerings', *pātheyya* 'provisions for a journey'. What they share is a relation, and what the relation is has to come from the base and the context.

[^wf-ika-senses]: Thānuttamo, s.v. *-ika*, with Kacc 350, 351, 367 and others: living by means of, going by means of, relating to, playing upon, mixed with, making, connected with, carrying upon, born in, studying, performed by, bartered for, possession, collection, measure. The count is reproduced instead of the list because a suffix with sixteen senses cannot be decoded; a reader can only recognise it and then look up the word.

**Descent**, the patronymic, uses ***-āyana***, ***-āna***, ***-era***, ***-eyya*** and a bare vuddhi. *Kaccāyana* and *Kaccāna* are both 'descendant of Kacca' and both are canonical names; *sāmaṇera* 'novice' is *samaṇa* plus *-era*, literally the ascetic's own; *Vāseṭṭha* is of the Vasiṭṭha clan; *Māgadha* is of Magadha.[^wf-patronymic] The class matters less for reading grammar than for reading names, where a reader will mostly meet it.

[^wf-patronymic]: Perniola §123, p. 156: a short section of miscellaneous suffixes, not a treatment of descent, and only its last entry, *-āyana* / *-āna*, is the patronymic proper; Thānuttamo, s.vv. *-āyana*, *-āna*, *-era*, *-eyya*, with Kacc 345–349. Warder p. 254 adds the case that has no marker at all: *Gotamo* the clan name is *Gotamo* the ancestor plus a suffix that changes nothing, so the derivative and its base are identical and only context separates them.

#### The suffix that changes nothing

***-ka***, and its longer form ***-aka***, is the suffix a reader most needs to be warned about, because it does five different things and one of them is nothing at all.[^wf-ka-five]

[^wf-ka-five]: Perniola §122, pp. 154–156, is the fullest treatment of this suffix in any source read, and the five senses above compress it; they are not his own division. He separates ten groups: the suffix on a root and on a verbal form; the agent made from a root and the agent made from an action noun; the diminutive and the contemptuous; the sense that changes nothing, on nouns and on adjectives; the adjectives made from adverbs and pronouns; and *-ika* 'belonging'. Among the words he files under the sense that changes nothing are *udaka*, which no reader would think to analyse, and *ācariyaka* beside *ācariya*, which is the same word twice.

It makes **agent nouns**, as above. It makes **diminutives**: *puttaka* 'little son', *kumāraka* 'boy', *kuṭikā* 'hut'. It makes **contemptuous** forms, which is the diminutive turned sour: *samaṇaka* 'a wretched little ascetic', *muṇḍaka* 'shaveling'. It turns adverbs and pronouns into **adjectives**: *adhika* 'exceeding', *māmaka* 'mine, devoted to me'. And it attaches to nouns and adjectives and **changes nothing whatever**: *khuddaka* is *khudda* 'small', *jeṭṭhaka* is *jeṭṭha* 'eldest', *ācariyaka* is *ācariya* 'teacher', *mahallaka* is 'old'.

The last of these trips a reader up, because a *-ka* that means nothing looks like a *-ka* that means something. There is no test. When a word in *-ka* does not make sense as a diminutive or an agent, try it as the plain word, and it usually is one. Chapter 13's *-ika* on a finished compound (*ponobhavikā*, *anulomikaṁ*) is a sixth use, on the far side of the boundary between the two chapters.

### Verbs made from nouns and adverbs

One formation remains, and it is common in the canon and absent from every source consulted for this chapter except one.[^wf-verb-compound]

[^wf-verb-compound]: Perniola §§144–145, pp. 178–179. He files it under "verbal compounds". Warder p. 77 n. 1 rules it out of compounding: "Finite verbs are not compounded with nouns." So Chapter 13 leaves this formation out and hands it here.

An **adverb or a noun may be compounded with *karoti* or *bhavati*** to make a fixed expression that behaves as a single verb. *Pātukaroti* 'makes manifest' and *pātubhavati* 'becomes manifest'; *āvikaroti* 'makes clear'; *purakkharoti* 'puts in front, honours'; *antaradhāyati* 'disappears', where *antara* 'interval' stands unchanged in front of the verb.

More often the first member takes a final ***-ī*** or ***-i*** before the verb, and this small formation carries a surprising amount of doctrinal vocabulary:

| Compound verb | Sense |
|---|---|
| *bahulīkaroti* | makes much of, cultivates |
| *sacchikaroti* | realises for oneself |
| *tuṇhībhūto* | fallen silent |
| *sītibhūto* | become cool |
| *vasībhūto* | become a master |

*Bahulīkaroti* occurs 134 times in the root texts, almost always in the phrase *bhāvitā bahulīkatā*, 'developed and cultivated', which closes the description of every practice the Buddha teaches. *Sacchikaroti* is the verb of realisation itself.[^wf-i-formation]

[^wf-i-formation]: Perniola §145, p. 179, for the formation, and §114(e), p. 145, for the same suffix approached from the other end, as an adverb-former. Of the nine verbs he lists at §145, two are canonical in the shape he prints them — *bahulīkaroti* with 134 root-text occurrences, *sacchikaroti* with 86 root-text occurrences — and the other seven, *vasīkaroti* among them, occur nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. Two of the seven fail differently: his *vasībhavati* and *sītibhavati* are unattested, but the past participles answering to them, *vasībhūto* and *sītībhūto*, are ordinary canonical Pāli, so what fails there is the citation form, not the formation. His §114(e) illustration is the same thing again: he cites it as *dubbalīkaraṇa*, which occurs once, and that in a commentary, while the locative *dubbalīkaraṇe* 'in the weakening' has 51 root-text occurrences: a word the canon uses only in an oblique case. The related abstract *ekībhāvo* 'solitude' belongs to the commentaries, not the canon.

### A verse made of derivatives

The Maṅgala Sutta is among the first things a Buddhist learns by heart, and almost every substantive in it is a derived noun. Three of its verses show four of this chapter's formations at work in eight lines.

- Asevanā ca bālānaṁ, paṇḍitānañca sevanā; Pūjā ca pūjaneyyānaṁ, etaṁ maṅgalamuttamaṁ. (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 46)
- *asevanā* < *a-* not + √*sev* + *-anā* — non-association (f. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *bālānaṁ* with fools (m. gen. pl.) · *paṇḍitānaṁ* with the wise (m. gen. pl.) · *ca* and · *sevanā* association · *pūjā* honouring · *ca* and · *pūjaneyyānaṁ* of those worthy of honour (m. gen. pl.) · *etaṁ* this · *maṅgalamuttamaṁ* < *maṅgalaṁ* blessing + *uttamaṁ* highest
- Not associating with fools, and associating with the wise, and honouring those worthy of honour: **this is the highest blessing.**

*Asevanā* and *sevanā* are one action noun in *-anā*, the same word twice with and without the negative particle, and the verse rests on the pair. *Pūjaneyyānaṁ* is the future passive participle in *-aneyya*, 'to be honoured', in the genitive plural.

- Bāhusaccañca sippañca, vinayo ca susikkhito; (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 47)
- *bāhusaccaṁ* < *bahu* much + *suta* heard + *-ya* — learning (nt. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *sippaṁ* craft · *ca* and · *vinayo* discipline · *ca* and · *susikkhito* < *su-* well + *sikkhita* trained
- **Learning** and a craft, and discipline well trained in;

***Bāhusaccaṁ*** is the whole of the last two sections in one word: a compound base, the suffix *-ya*, *vuddhi* of the first vowel, and the assimilation of *ty* to *cc*. Nothing of *bahussuta* survives on the surface.

- Mātāpitu upaṭṭhānaṁ, puttadārassa saṅgaho; (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 47)
- *mātāpitu* mother and father (dvanda, gen. dual sense) · *upaṭṭhānaṁ* < *upa-* near + √*ṭhā* + *-ana* — attending on (nt. nom. sg.) · *puttadārassa* of children and spouse (m. gen. sg.) · *saṅgaho* < *saṁ-* together + √*gah* + *-a* — support (m. nom. sg.)
- **Attending on** mother and father, **supporting** children and spouse;

*Upaṭṭhānaṁ* is the *-ana* action noun on a prefixed root, and *saṅgaho* is the *-a* action noun on another, with the prefix in its assimilated shape. Between them the two verses have used the *-anā*, *-ana*, *-a*, *-ya* and *-aneyya* suffixes and three prefixes, and there is nothing unusual about them; this is simply what Pāli verse is made of.

The same is true of the formula the reader met in the last chapter. *Sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko*: four epithets of the Dhamma, all four adjectives in *-ika*, and the last of them shows the front strengthening clearly: the prefix *upa-* has become *opa-*. The other three do not, and each for a different reason, which is a fair sample of how the rule behaves in practice: *saṁ-* has no vowel to strengthen, the negative *a-* is not one of the affixes that trigger it, and *ehi-* is an imperative, not a stem.[^wf-dhamma-formula]

[^wf-dhamma-formula]: AN 6.10 — A iii 285 and elsewhere. DPD's constructions: *sandiṭṭhika* = `saṁ + √dis + ti + ka`; *akālika* = `na + √kal + *a + ika`; *ehipassika* = `ehi + passa + ika`; *opaneyyika* = `upa + √nī + *eyya + *ika`. Warder p. 253 gives the fourth as *opanayika*, the form the Visuddhimagga prints once; the canon has *opaneyyiko* fifty-five times in the root texts, and DPD's *-eyya-* is what accounts for it.

### Where word formation is treated elsewhere

The comparative and superlative suffixes (*-tara*, *-tama*, *-iya*, *-iṭṭha*, *-ma*) are *taddhita* affixes and are taught in the chapter on gender and adjectives, where a reader meets them. **The numeral suffixes** (*-ma* and *-tiya* for the ordinals, *-khattuṁ*, *-dhā*, *-so*, *-guṇa*) are in the chapter on numerals. **The adverb-forming suffixes** (*-to*, *-tra*, *-ttha*, *-dā*, *-thā*) are *taddhita* too, and are in the chapter on indeclinables, since what a reader needs about them is what they do in a sentence.

The participle, absolutive and infinitive suffixes are all *kita*, and have their own two chapters. The denominative, causative, passive and desiderative stems are in the chapter on derived conjugations: they build verbs, not nouns, and the tradition sorts them separately. The join inside a compound is in the chapter on compounds; this chapter has taken only the join after a prefix. And the full inventory of suffixes and prefixes, as Ñāṇatusita tabulates it, is at Appendix A.4.

### What derivation will not tell you

Three cautions, in ascending order of discomfort, and all three are the sources' own.

Words from one root diverge. Warder is blunt about the consequence: "In the case of nouns, however, it is usually more convenient to learn the separate words without much attention to derivation, especially as the meanings of nouns derived from a common root are often widely divergent and it is these distinctions of meaning, rather than any similarity, which it is essential to discover and remember."[^wf-warder-caution] The *ñā* family above is the demonstration: seven prefixes on one root give seven words no arithmetic would predict.

[^wf-warder-caution]: Warder p. 251.

A prefixed verb is a word, not a sum. This is Bodhi's warning, quoted in full above, and so a good glossary is worth more than a good rule.

And a derivative may look like its base. *Gotamo* the clan name is *Gotamo* the ancestor plus a suffix that leaves no trace, and only the context says which is meant. So the absence of a visible suffix is not evidence that no derivation has taken place, and that sums up fairly why this chapter claims to help a reader recognise words, not generate them.

None of this makes the chapter useless; it makes it a chapter about reading more than parsing. Know that the vuddhi is at the front, that *-tā* attaches to anything, that *-ka* often means nothing, and that a *-ti* noun is not a verb, and you will get through a page of canon markedly faster. The chapter claims no more.
