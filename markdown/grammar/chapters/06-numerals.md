## Numerals

Numerals are among the most used words in any language, and heavily used words resist change. So the Pāli numerals keep more of their Old Indic shape than almost anything else in the nominal system, and they pay for that conservatism in irregularity: the lowest numbers, which are used most, are the least predictable.

The irregularity is best met head on, not apologised for, because it is not arbitrary. Most of it comes from a single event in the history of the language, the loss of the dual, and once that is named, several forms that otherwise have to be memorised become consequences instead.

One caution before the paradigms. It is often said that a numeral simply takes the case of the noun it counts, so that its own inflection can be read off the noun. That is true of the low numerals and it is not the general rule: Pāli has four different ways of joining a numeral to what it counts, and only the first is agreement. They are set out under "How a numeral joins its noun" below, and to assume agreement throughout is to misparse the genitive construction every time it appears.

### One

The numeral *eka* 'one' is inflected as follows:

|            | sg. m.               | sg. n.               | sg. f.     | pl. m.    | pl. n.    | pl. f.     |
|------------|----------------------|----------------------|------------|-----------|-----------|------------|
| nom.       | *eko*                | *ekaṁ*               | *ekā*      | *eke*     | *ekāni*   | *ekā*      |
| acc.       | *ekaṁ*               | *ekaṁ*               | *ekaṁ*     | *eke*     | *ekāni*   | *ekā*      |
| inst.      | *ekena*              | *ekena*              | *ekāya*    | *ekehi*   | *ekehi*   | *ekāhi*    |
| abl.       | *ekasmā*, *ekamhā*   | *ekasmā*, *ekamhā*   | *ekāya*    | *ekehi*   | *ekehi*   | *ekāhi*    |
| gen./dat.  | *ekassa*             | *ekassa*             | *ekissā*   | *ekesaṁ*  | *ekesaṁ*  | *ekāsaṁ*   |
| loc.       | *ekasmiṁ*, *ekamhi*  | *ekasmiṁ*, *ekamhi*  | *ekissā*   | *ekesu*   | *ekesu*   | *ekāsu*    |

Nothing here has to be learned separately: *eka* declines as a pronominal adjective, like the *-o / -aṁ / -ā* pattern set out in the chapter on pronouns.[^eka-pronominal]

[^eka-some]: Perniola §50(a), p. 64: "*Eka* can also be declined in the plural in the sense of 'some'. Here also it follows the pronominal declension. In this meaning, however, the word *ekacce* is more common", *ekacce* taking the pronominal ending in the nominative plural and the *a*-stem endings elsewhere, hence genitive plural *ekaccānaṁ*. Oberlies §43(1), p. 192, reports the same division of labour from Saddanīti 284,33, where *ekacca-* supplies the feminine plural that *ekā-* does not. In the **singular** *eka* likewise often means 'a certain, a', not 'one', which is as close as Pāli comes to an indefinite article.

[^eka-pronominal]: Oberlies §43(1), p. 192, states the point directly ("the numeral *eka-* 'one' inflects as a pronominal adjective"), and the paradigm above matches the pronominal-adjective endings throughout. Only the feminine *ekissā* follows the *ki-* / *ima-* type, not the plain *-assā*. Bomhard p. 47 gives the same paradigm.

*Eka* alone among the numerals has a singular, and it alone does not always count. In the plural it means 'some', not 'ones', and to insist on the numerical sense there is to make nonsense of a common idiom.[^eka-some] Every numeral above it has a plural only, for the obvious reason.

- Na, bhikkhave, **ekasmiṁ** āvāse dve uposathāgārāni sammannitabbāni. (Kd 2 — Vin i 107)
- Bhikkhus, two uposatha halls are not to be determined **in one** monastery.

- Santi, bho Gotama, **eke** samaṇabrāhmaṇā evaṁvādino evaṁdiṭṭhino. (SN 36.21 — S iv 230)
- There are, sir Gotama, **some** renunciants and brahmins with such a doctrine, such a view.

### Two till four

The numerals *dvi* 'two', *ti* 'three' and *catur* 'four' are inflected as follows:

|             | *dvi* (all genders) | *ti* m.    | *ti* n.   | *ti* f.        | *catur* m.  | *catur* n.  | *catur* f.  |
|-------------|---------------------|------------|-----------|----------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| nom./acc.   | *dve*, *duve*       | *tayo*     | *tīṇi*    | *tisso*        | *cattāro*, *caturo* | *cattāri*   | *catasso*   |
| inst./abl.  | *dvīhi*             | *tīhi*     | *tīhi*    | *tīhi*         | *catūhi*, *catubbhi* | *catūhi*, *catubbhi* | *catūhi*, *catubbhi* |
| gen./dat.   | *dvinnaṁ*           | *tiṇṇaṁ*, *tiṇṇannaṁ* | *tiṇṇaṁ*, *tiṇṇannaṁ* | *tissannaṁ*    | *catunnaṁ*  | *catunnaṁ*  | *catunnaṁ*  |
| loc.        | *dvīsu*             | *tīsu*     | *tīsu*    | *tīsu*         | *catūsu*    | *catūsu*    | *catūsu*    |

Note where the genders collapse, because the pattern is not the one a reader expects. *Dvi* makes no gender distinction at all. *Ti* and *catu* distinguish all three genders in the nominative and accusative, and only masculine-neuter against feminine in the genitive. And in the instrumental, ablative and locative both behave as plain *i*-stems, identical across the genders, the *-ī-* of *tīhi* and *tīsu* following the vowel of *tīṇi*, and the *-ū-* of *catūhi* the same pattern one number up.[^ti-catur-oberlies]

Two forms in that table deserve attention. *Tiṇṇannaṁ* is a genitive with the ending applied twice, built on analogy with *pañcannaṁ*, *channaṁ* and *sattannaṁ*: the numeral has been reanalysed as though it belonged to the class above it.[^tinnannam] And *cattāro* beside *caturo* is a single form with two functions: Oberlies takes the pair as nominative *and* accusative for both, the case distinction having collapsed under the influence of the feminine, where *catasso* had always served both.[^cattaro-caturo]

[^ti-catur-oberlies]: Oberlies §43(3)–(4), p. 193. He gives *tayo* / *tīṇi* / *tisso*, genitive *tiṇṇaṁ* (masc./ntr.) against *tissannaṁ* (fem.), the latter taking its geminate *-ss-* from the nominative *tisso*, and notes that the instrumental, ablative and locative, "identical for all genders, show the inflexion of an *i*-stem". Thī 518 uses *tīṇi* of the **feminine** (*sakhiyo tīṇi janiyo*), which he flags as exceptional, citing Norman 1971: 178–179.

[^tinnannam]: Oberlies §43(3), p. 193, citing Vin I 162,36 and Mil 309,8: "The gen. also has a double ending (*tiṇṇannaṁ*) … by analogy with *pañcannaṁ*, *channaṁ*, *sattannaṁ*."

[^cattaro-caturo]: Oberlies §43(4), p. 193: 'four' "has two forms for nom. *and* acc. masc. (owing to case confusion and after the model of fem.)". The stems used in composition are *catu(r)-* and *cātu(r)-*; compare *temāsa-* 'three months, the rainy season' (M I 438,10).

- Imāni **cattāri** ariyasaccāni. (AN 3.61)
- These are the **four** noble truths.

The gender distinctions in that table are easiest to see where one text prints two of them. The account of what a newly ordained monk is told closes two of its sections with the same formula, one section apart:

- **Cattāro** nissayā niṭṭhitā. (Kd 1 — Vin i 96)
- *cattāro* four (m. nom. pl.) · *nissayā* the supports (m. nom. pl.) · *niṭṭhitā* are finished (m. nom. pl. pp.)
- "The **four** supports are finished."

- **Cattāri** akaraṇīyāni niṭṭhitāni. (Kd 1 — Vin i 97)
- *cattāri* four (n. nom. pl.) · *akaraṇīyāni* the things not to be done (n. nom. pl.) · *niṭṭhitāni* are finished (n. nom. pl. pp.)
- "The **four** things not to be done are finished."

*Nissaya* is masculine and *akaraṇīya* neuter, and the numeral and the participle both follow the noun. The two sentences differ in no other way.

*Tayo*, the masculine of 'three', is as easy to find:

- **Tayo** pattassa vaṇṇā—ukkaṭṭho patto, majjhimo patto, omako patto. (Bi Np 1 — Vin iv 243)
- *tayo* three (m. nom. pl.) · *pattassa* of a bowl (m. gen. sg.) · *vaṇṇā* sizes (m. nom. pl.) · *ukkaṭṭho* large · *patto* bowl · *majjhimo* medium · *omako* small
- "There are **three** sizes of bowl: the large bowl, the medium bowl, the small bowl."

The genitive there belongs to *vaṇṇā* and not to the numeral, and *pattassa* being singular is what shows it. *Tayo* is in plain agreement with the noun it counts: the first of the four constructions set out below, and not the second.

- Athāparaṁ **dvīhi** dhammehi niyyāti abhijjhāya ca byāpādena ca. (AN 3.94 — A i 242)
- *atha* then · *aparaṁ* further · *dvīhi* by two (inst. pl.) · *dhammehi* by things (m. inst. pl.) · *niyyāti* goes out · *abhijjhāya* by covetousness (f. inst. sg.) · *ca* and · *byāpādena* by ill will (m. inst. sg.) · *ca* and
- Then further he is delivered from **two** things: covetousness and ill will.

The second example is harder than it looks, because the English and the Pāli do not use the same case and the numeral is no help in deciding which. *Dvīhi* is instrumental *and* ablative, as the table above shows, so it cannot decide; and *dhammehi* is likewise syncretic. *Byāpādena* settles it, for it can only be instrumental singular: an ablative there would be *byāpādā* or *byāpādasmā*. So the whole phrase is instrumental, and the English "from" is a translator's necessity, not a report of the case.

This is the **instrumental of separation**, and Wijesekera treats it directly. With verbs expressing separation, he writes, "the abl. is the more logical construction … but the use of the inst. is due to the psychological fact that underlying both union and separation there is the notion of mutuality". He gives *sumuttā mayaṁ tena mahāsamaṇena* 'we are completely free from that great recluse' and *maraṇadhammā sattā maraṇena parimuccanti* 'beings subject to death are released from death', and notes that the two cases can appear co-ordinately in one sentence.[^inst-separation] The lesson for reading numerals is the general one: where a numeral's form is syncretic, the case is decided by its noun, and where the noun is syncretic too, by whatever else the phrase governs.

[^inst-separation]: Wijesekera §73.c, "With verbs expressing separation". His co-ordinate example is *na parimuccanti jātiyā jarāmaraṇena … na parimuccanti dukkhasmā* (S I.24), where two instrumentals and an ablative serve the same purpose in one breath. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī commentary marks both constructions for *niyyāti* explicitly (As 214): *Lokato niyyāti vaṭṭato niyyātīti niyyānikaṁ. Niyyāti vā etenāti niyyānikaṁ*: 'it goes out from the world, goes out from the round' with the ablative, 'or one goes out **by means of** this' with the instrumental. Bodhi renders the sutta line "when he departs from two states, longing and ill will", which takes the sense without committing the English to a case.

### The lost dual, and *ubho* 'both'

Old Indic had three numbers, not two: singular, **dual** and plural. Pāli has lost the dual, and the loss is the reason *dvi* 'two' looks the way it does. The dual exists above all *for* a numeral meaning 'two', so when the category went, the word had to be rebuilt out of whatever was left. Oberlies puts it clearly: "Due to the loss of the dual the numeral 'two' … had to be remodelled."[^dual-loss] Its nominative-accusative *dve* is an old dual form transferred to the masculine, and every other case in its column is borrowed wholesale from 'three'. So *dvi* has no gender distinction and no paradigm of its own worth the name: it is an assembly.

One word escaped the demolition. ***Ubha-*** 'both' keeps a true dual ending in its nominative and accusative *ubho*, from Old Indic *ubhau*, and generalised that *o* across the rest of its inflection:

|            | *ubha* 'both' (all genders) |
|------------|------------------------------|
| nom./acc.  | *ubho*                       |
| inst./abl. | *ubhohi*                     |
| gen./dat.  | *ubhinnaṁ*                   |
| loc.       | *ubhosu*                     |

So *ubho* is the last dual in the language, not an irregular plural, and the *-o* that looks like a masculine nominative singular is nothing of the kind.[^ubho-dual] Beside it stands *ubhaya-*, which means the same thing and declines as an ordinary *a*-stem: the regularised replacement, which is how a language usually disposes of a category it no longer has.

- **Appamādo** kho, mahārāja, eko dhammo, yo **ubho** atthe samadhiggayha tiṭṭhati. (SN 3.17 — S i 86)
- *appamādo* diligence · *kho* indeed · *mahārāja* great king (voc.) · *eko* one · *dhammo* thing · *yo* which · *ubho* both (acc.) · *atthe* benefits (m. acc. pl.) · *samadhiggayha* having secured · *tiṭṭhati* stands
- "Diligence, great king, is the one thing that secures **both** benefits."

The next sentence says which two: *diṭṭhadhammikañceva atthaṁ samparāyikañca*, the benefit belonging to this visible life and the benefit belonging to what comes after. *Ubho* is used as a dual exists to be used: pointing at a pair already known to both speakers, not counting to two.

[^dual-loss]: Oberlies §43(2), p. 192. He gives the stems from which 'two' was reassembled (*d[u]vā-*, *d[(u)v]i-*, *d[v]e-*, *du[v]-* and *bā-*) and notes that the nominative/accusative feminine and neuter *d(u)ve* (compare Old Indic *duvé*) was transferred to the masculine, its ending then matching *ime*, *te*, *sabbe*. The locative *duve* is used only in the late Apadāna (443,1/3), and the 'eastern' locative *duvesu* stands beside *dvīsu*.

[^ubho-dual]: Oberlies §43(2), pp. 192–193: "*ubha-* has generalised the *o* of the nom. (< *ubhau*)", giving *ubho*, *ubhohi*, *ubhinnaṁ*, *ubhosu*. He notes *ubhaya-* inflects as an *a*-stem, used in both singular and plural with a plural verb, and that it is contaminated with *dvi-* to give *dubhaya-* (Saddanīti V 1451). The indigenous tradition counts *ubha* among the twenty-seven *sabbanāmāni* (see the pronoun chapter), so a reader meets it in lists of pronouns as often as in lists of numerals. Perniola p. 65 prints the same four forms independently, classing *ubho* with *dvi* as "declined only in the plural".

### Five till eighteen

From five to eighteen the difficulty stops. These numerals have no gender at all, they exist only in the plural, and they all take one set of endings:

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| *pañca* | five | *dvādasa* | twelve |
| *cha* | six | *terasa* | thirteen |
| *satta* | seven | *cuddasa* | fourteen |
| *aṭṭha* | eight | *paṇṇarasa* | fifteen |
| *nava* | nine | *soḷasa* | sixteen |
| *dasa* | ten | *sattarasa* | seventeen |
| *ekādasa* | eleven | *aṭṭhārasa* | eighteen |

The teens are each a unit compounded with 'ten', and the second element shows up in two shapes: *-dasa* in *ekādasa*, *dvādasa* and *cuddasa*, and *-rasa* in *terasa*, *paṇṇarasa*, *sattarasa* and *aṭṭhārasa*. Both are inherited, and neither is a misprint for the other.[^teens]

They are inflected as follows:

| gender     | m. & n. & f. |
|------------|--------------|
| nom./acc.  | *-a*         |
| inst./abl. | *-ahi*       |
| gen./dat.  | *-annaṁ*[^pancannam-dental] |
| loc.       | *-asu*       |

[^teens]: Oberlies §43(1), p. 196, gives the variants and their derivations: 'thirteen' is *terasa-* / *telasa-* < *trayadaśa-*, with a further form in *-ḷ-* < *-ḍ-*; 'fourteen' *catuddasa-* also shows "abnormal loss of *-t-*", giving *cuddasa-*; and "the genuine MIA forms of 'fifteen' are *paṇṇarasa-* and *pannarasa-*". So the two forms are several separate developments that happen to converge on two endings, not one rule applied unevenly, and the list has to be learnt, not derived.

[^pancannam-dental]: The genitive/dative is dental *-annaṁ*, not retroflex *-aṇṇaṁ*. DPD derives the form transparently (*pañca* + *ānaṁ* > *annaṁ*, Sanskrit *pañcānām*), and *pañcaṇṇaṁ* does not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, against ready attestation of *pañcannaṁ*, *channaṁ*, *sattannaṁ*, *aṭṭhannaṁ* and *dasannaṁ*.

- **Pañca** saññā bhāvitā bahulīkatā mahapphalā honti mahānisaṁsā. (AN 5.62 — A iii 79)
- **Five** perceptions, developed and cultivated, are of great fruit, great benefit.

- **Aṭṭhahi**, bhikkhave, ākārehi itthī purisaṁ bandhati. (AN 8.17 — A iv 196)
- Bhikkhus, a woman binds a man **in eight** ways.

### Nineteen and upwards

Something changes at nineteen, and it is not that the words merely get longer. Up to eighteen a numeral is an **adjective**: it has no gender of its own, it takes the gender of whatever it counts, and it stands in that noun's case. From nineteen upwards a numeral is a **noun**. It has a gender of its own, it is normally singular however many things it counts, and the thing counted must be attached to it by some other means than agreement.

Perniola states the boundary as a flat rule ("The numerals from 1 to 18 are adjectives, from 20 onwards are nouns") and leaves nineteen unassigned. Oberlies puts it at nineteen and supplies the reason: nineteen is *ekūnavīsati*, 'twenty less one', a word built on 'twenty'. It belongs with what follows it, not with what precedes.[^nineteen-boundary]

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 19 | *ekūnavīsati* | 70 | *sattati* |
| 20 | *vīsati*, *vīsa* | 80 | *asīti* |
| 30 | *tiṁsati*, *tiṁsa* | 90 | *navuti* |
| 40 | *cattālīsa*, *cattārīsa* | 100 | *sataṁ* |
| 50 | *paññāsa*, *paṇṇāsa* | 1,000 | *sahassaṁ* |
| 60 | *saṭṭhi* | 100,000 | *satasahassaṁ*, *lakkhaṁ* |

Between the decades the unit and the decade simply compound: *ekavīsati* 'twenty-one', *dvattiṁsa* 'thirty-two', *caturāsīti* 'eighty-four'. Where 'two' is the unit the compounding form is normally *dvā-*, not *dve-*, and its vowel shortens before a double consonant: hence *dvāvīsa* 'twenty-two' beside *dvattiṁsa*. That *dvā-* has a further development to *bā-*, and for 'twenty-two' the canon prefers it: *bāvīsati* and *bāvīsa* are both commoner than *dvāvīsa*.[^dvavisa-forms]

[^dvavisa-forms]: The grammars give *dvāvīsati* and *dvattiṁsati*, and neither is root-text Pāli. They are the grammars' forms, carrying the *-ti* of *vīsati* through into the compound, and the canon drops it: *dvattiṁsa* stands ten times in the root texts against *dvattiṁsati*'s none (three occurrences in the whole of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, all in the grammatical literature), and *dvāvīsati* has a single root-text occurrence, in the Visuddhimagga, not the canon proper. For 'twenty-two' the attested forms are *bāvīsa* (15), *dvāvīsa* (4) and *bāvīsati* (3 outside the Visuddhimagga); DPD derives the third from the first two, *dvi* > *dva* > *bā* + *vīsati*, so they are one word and not three. All of *bāvīsa*'s occurrences and all of *dvāvīsa*'s but one are in the Paṭṭhāna's counting summaries, standing in series with *aṭṭhārasa* 18, *dvādasa* 12, *tevīsa* 23 and *pañcavīsa* 25: cardinals, though DPD lemmatises the bare *dvāvīsa* as an ordinal and gives *dvāvīsā* for the cardinal. The rule the paragraph teaches is unaffected (*dvā-* keeps its long vowel before a single consonant and shortens before a double one), and the attested pair shows it more cleanly than the grammars' pair did. The edition itself sets a trap: the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana files the Visuddhimagga among the root texts, so a small root-text count may be Buddhaghosa in the fifth century, not the canon, and here it was.

Three formations do what a modern reader expects arithmetic to do. All three should be recognisable on sight, but they are not equally canonical, and the difference matters. *Ekūna-* 'less one' counts **down** from the next round number, and it is everywhere in the suttas: *ekūnavīsati* 19, *ekūnatiṁsa* 29, *ekūnapaññāsa* 49. *Uttara-* 'over' counts up from the round number instead, so *vīsuttarasataṁ* is 'a hundred with twenty over', 120 — but no canonical text says it, or any of its fellows. That formation belongs to the commentaries and the later chronicles.[^uttara-postcanonical] And the halves are handled by prefixing *aḍḍha-* 'half' to an **ordinal**, which names the hundred being half-completed, not the one already finished: *diyaḍḍha-* is 'one and a half', so *diyaḍḍhasataṁ* is 150, literally 'a hundred and the second half-done'.[^halves-ordinal]

That last formation is more than a curiosity. It is how the canon states the size of the Pātimokkha:

- Sādhikamidaṁ, bhante, **diyaḍḍhasikkhāpadasataṁ** anvaddhamāsaṁ uddesaṁ āgacchati. (AN 3.84 — A i 230)
- *sādhikaṁ* with more besides · *idaṁ* this · *bhante* venerable sir · *diyaḍḍha·sikkhāpada·sataṁ* one-and-a-half-hundred training rules (n. nom. sg.) · *anvaddhamāsaṁ* every fortnight · *uddesaṁ* to the recitation · *āgacchati* comes
- "Sir, **this hundred and fifty training rules** comes up for recitation every fortnight, and more besides."

The Vajjian monk who says this is complaining that he cannot train in so many rules. Grammatically, *diyaḍḍhasikkhāpadasataṁ* is a single neuter **singular** (one word, one ending) for a hundred and fifty things, and that the verb *āgacchati* is singular to match. A numeral of this size does not agree with what it counts; it swallows it.

The declension follows the ending, not the number, and there are four patterns:[^higher-declension]

| Ending | Declines as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *-ti* (*vīsati*, *saṭṭhi*, *asīti*, *navuti*, *koṭi*) | feminine *i*-stem, like *jāti* | *vīsati*, *vīsatiṁ*, *vīsatiyā*, *vīsatiyaṁ* |
| *-ā* (*cattālīsā*, *paññāsā*) | feminine *ā*-stem, like *vanitā* | *vīsā*, *vīsaṁ*, *vīsāya*, *vīsāyaṁ* |
| *-aṁ* (*sataṁ*, *sahassaṁ*) | neuter *a*-stem, singular and plural | *sataṁ*, *satena*, *satassa*; *satāni*, *satehi*, *satānaṁ*, *satesu* |
| *-a* (*vīsa*, *tiṁsa*) | usually uninflected | *vīsa* |

[^nineteen-boundary]: Perniola §50, p. 64; Oberlies §43(1), p. 196: "The numerals 'one' through 'eighteen' are used as adjectives, unless they inflect as neuters (sg.) in analogy with *vīsaṁ* (< *viṁśat*). The numerals 'nineteen' and upwards are neuter or feminine substantives in *-aṁ* or *-ā* and *-ti* respectively." The two are not in conflict: Perniola's silence about nineteen is what Oberlies's derivation fills.

[^halves-ordinal]: Oberlies §43(3), p. 198: "To denote fractional sense *aḍḍha-* is added to the next higher ordinal: *aḍḍhatiya-* 'two and a half', *aḍḍhuḍḍha-* 'three and a half'." Perniola §53, p. 68, gives the series as *diyaḍḍha*, *aḍḍhatiya* / *aḍḍhateyya*, *aḍḍhuḍḍha* for 1½, 2½, 3½, and p. 64 gives *diyaḍḍhasataṁ* 150 and *diyaḍḍhasahassaṁ* 1500. Both halves of the system are attested: *diyaḍḍhasikkhāpadasataṁ* at AN 3.84, and *aḍḍhateyyāni niruttisatāni* '250 kinds of language' in the Paṭisambhidāmagga, where the surrounding count (*pañcavīsatisataṁ* 125, then 250, then *pañca ñāṇasatāni* 500) confirms the arithmetic from the passage itself. Perniola's examples for 108 are *aṭṭhādhika sataṁ* and *aṭṭhuttara sataṁ*, which he prints as two words; the texts write them as single words, and as such *aṭṭhuttarasata* occurs three times and *aṭṭhādhikasataṁ* once. The note on the *uttara-* numerals below says where they occur.

[^uttara-postcanonical]: The *uttara-* numerals are **post-canonical**. Across the whole of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana the type occurs only outside the root texts: *vīsuttarasataṁ* once in the commentaries, *dasuttarasataṁ* three times there and three more in the later grammatical and chronicle works, *aṭṭhuttarasata* three times in those works, *catuttarasate* and *dvādasuttarasata-* likewise, and *aṭṭhādhikasataṁ* once in the commentaries, and nothing at all in the four nikāyas or the Vinaya. The *ekūna-* type, by contrast, is canonical and common. Nor is there anything to choose between *vīsuttarasataṁ* and Perniola's *aṭṭhuttara sataṁ* (p. 63) on grounds of attestation: the two stand in the same relation to the canon, which is outside it.

[^higher-declension]: Perniola §50(e)–(h), pp. 65–66. *Koṭi* 'ten million' is feminine and declines with the *-ti* group. The fourth row is the fact Oberlies reports from the syntactic side ("Often, however, the numerals are not inflected at all", p. 196), and it explains why a higher numeral so often gives a reader no case information at all.

### How a numeral joins its noun

The chapter opened with a warning, and here it is made good. It is often said that a numeral takes the case of the noun it counts. For *eka* through *aṭṭhārasa* that is true, and for everything above it is one option among four. Oberlies sets the four out in a single sentence: the numeral "may be used appositionally in the same case as the substantive, or else the qualified substantive may be put in the genitive plural. Often, however, the numerals are not inflected at all. Finally, a determinative compound may be formed."[^four-constructions]

**Agreement** is the first, and needs no comment beyond an example. The Mahāpadāna Sutta happens to give it twice over, in two different cases in consecutive sentences:

- … lābhinī bodhisattamātā hoti **pañcannaṁ** kāmaguṇānaṁ. Sā **pañcahi** kāmaguṇehi samappitā samaṅgībhūtā paricāreti. (DN 14 — D ii 13)
- *lābhinī* one who obtains (f. nom. sg.) · *bodhisattamātā* the Bodhisatta's mother · *hoti* is · *pañcannaṁ* of five (gen. pl.) · *kāmaguṇānaṁ* of the kinds of sensual pleasure (m. gen. pl.) · *sā* she · *pañcahi* with five (inst. pl.) · *kāmaguṇehi* with the kinds of sensual pleasure (m. inst. pl.) · *samappitā* provided · *samaṅgībhūtā* endowed · *paricāreti* amuses herself
- … the Bodhisatta's mother obtains the **five** kinds of sensual pleasure. Provided and endowed with the **five** kinds of sensual pleasure, she amuses herself.

*Pañcannaṁ* against *pañcahi*: one word, two cases, each matching its noun, and nothing more.

**The genitive** is the second, and it catches a reader trained on the first. Here the numeral is a noun in its own right and the thing counted hangs off it in the genitive plural: 'a thousand *of* moons', not 'a thousand moons':

- Tasmiṁ **sahassadhā** loke **sahassaṁ** candānaṁ **sahassaṁ** sūriyānaṁ … **cattāri** mahāsamuddasahassāni. (AN 10.29 — A v 59)
- *tasmiṁ* in that · *sahassadhā* thousandfold · *loke* world · *sahassaṁ* a thousand (n. nom. sg.) · *candānaṁ* of moons (m. gen. pl.) · *sūriyānaṁ* of suns (m. gen. pl.) · *cattāri* four (n. nom. pl.) · *mahāsamudda·sahassāni* thousands of great oceans (n. nom. pl.)
- "In that thousandfold world there are a **thousand** moons, a **thousand** suns … and **four** thousand great oceans."

The Buddha is describing the thousandfold world-system, and the sentence is a gift to a grammarian: it runs the genitive construction eleven times and then, without a break, switches to the fourth construction for the oceans. *Sahassaṁ candānaṁ* is a singular neuter noun governing a genitive; *mahāsamuddasahassāni* is a compound, plural, with *cattāri* agreeing with it. Two constructions, one sentence, and the reason for the switch is arithmetic: four thousand cannot be said with one *sahassaṁ*.

**No inflection at all** is the third, and it is less a construction than the absence of one. The numerals in *-a* commonly stand as they are, and a reader gets no case from them; the noun has to be read first and the numeral attached to it afterwards.

**The compound** is the fourth, and in practice the canon reaches for it whenever a large round number qualifies a noun:

- Tānipi kho **pañcabhikkhusatāni** … tikkhattuṁ citakaṁ padakkhiṇaṁ katvā bhagavato pāde sirasā vandiṁsu. (DN 16 — D ii 163)
- *tāni* those (n. nom. pl.) · *pi* too · *kho* indeed · *pañca·bhikkhu·satāni* five hundreds of bhikkhus (n. nom. pl.) · *tikkhattuṁ* three times · *citakaṁ* the pyre (m. acc. sg.) · *padakkhiṇaṁ katvā* having circumambulated · *bhagavato pāde* the Blessed One's feet · *sirasā* with the head · *vandiṁsu* paid homage
- "Those **five hundred bhikkhus** too … circumambulated the pyre three times and paid homage with their heads at the Blessed One's feet."

Note what the compound costs and what it buys. *Pañcabhikkhusatāni* is **neuter**, because *sata* is neuter, even though the five hundred are monks; *tāni* is neuter to agree with it, and only the verb *vandiṁsu* remembers that people are meant. Expect the gender of the counted noun to survive and you will misread the pronoun.

Small numerals compound too, and there the gender comes out the other way. The Vinaya's definition of 'inhabited area' runs four of them in series:

- **Ekakuṭikopi** gāmo, **dvikuṭikopi** gāmo, **tikuṭikopi** gāmo, **catukuṭikopi** gāmo … (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)
- *ekakuṭiko* having one hut (m. nom. sg.) · *pi* also · *gāmo* an inhabited area (m. nom. sg.) · *dvikuṭiko* having two huts · *tikuṭiko* having three · *catukuṭiko* having four
- "An area with **one hut** is an inhabited area, an area with **two huts** is an inhabited area, with **three huts**, with **four huts** …"

The stems in composition are *eka-*, *dvi-*, *ti-* and *catu-*, and none of them is a cell of the table above; a numeral stands there in its bare stem, as any other first member does. The gender goes the other way from *pañcabhikkhusatāni* because the compound is of a different class. *Ekakuṭika* is possessive ('having one hut'), so it takes the gender of what it describes, and *gāmo* is masculine; *pañcabhikkhusatāni* is determinative, so it keeps the neuter of its own last member *sata*, and Chapter 13 sets the two classes out. What decides is the class of the compound, not the numeral inside it.[^numeral-compound-class]

[^numeral-compound-class]: Oberlies's fourth construction (§43, p. 197) names the determinative compound specifically, and the possessive is not among his four. It has the same syntactic function (a numeral and its noun in a single word) and differs only in what the word then agrees with, so it is given here as an extension of his fourth, not as a fifth. DPD reads *ekakuṭika* as *eka* + *kuṭi* + *ka*, an adjective 'with one hut', and cites this passage for it. None of the four is printed bare anywhere in the canon, because the edition joins *pi* to each: *ekakuṭikopi* has 1 root-text occurrence, and *dvikuṭiko*, *tikuṭiko* and *catukuṭiko* do not occur at all. The resolution on the gloss line above is an analysis and not a quotation, which is the rule the chapter on sandhi states.

[^four-constructions]: Oberlies §43(1), pp. 196–197. His footnote there adds a fifth possibility from outside the canon: in texts like the Dīpavaṁsa a numeral (and *ubho* with it) can keep its case ending as the first member of a compound, *tīṇi-vassamhi nigrodho catuvassamhi bhātaro* (Dīp VII 31–32). Perniola reaches the third of the four from the morphological side, not the syntactic, at §50(h): "Numerals ending in *-a* are usually uninflected."

### Ordinal numbers

The first ten ordinals are:

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| *paṭhama* | first | *chaṭṭha* | sixth |
| *dutiya* | second | *sattama* | seventh |
| *tatiya* | third | *aṭṭhama* | eighth |
| *catuttha* | fourth | *navama* | ninth |
| *pañcama* | fifth | *dasama* | tenth |

Only the first four have to be learnt as items. *Paṭhama* is built on the prefix *pa-* 'in front', not on *eka* at all; *dutiya* and *tatiya* are formed with a suffix *-tiya*; and *catuttha* and *chaṭṭha* are formed with *-tha*, so the fourth and sixth break the pattern the fifth establishes.[^ordinal-formation] From five upwards the rule is simply *-ma* added to the cardinal, and it does not stop: *ekādasama* 'eleventh', *vīsatima* 'twentieth', *sattatima* 'seventieth'. Between eleven and eighteen the suffix may also be dropped altogether, so that the cardinal serves as its own ordinal: *terasa* both 'thirteen' and 'thirteenth'. All of them inflect as ordinary *a*-stem adjectives, agreeing with their noun in gender, number and case.

The feminine deserves care, because the two grammars this chapter leans on most disagree about it, and the canon settles the question against the one that is usually right. Oberlies states that the feminine "is always in *-ā*, while those in *-ī* are used to denote dates". Perniola gives a different rule: the first three are in *-ā* (*paṭhamā*, *dutiyā*, *tatiyā*), and from the fourth upwards the feminine is in *-ī*, *catutthī*, *pañcamī*, *ekādasamī*. The Mahānidāna Sutta runs both across three consecutive sentences, with the same feminine noun each time:

- Ayaṁ **tatiyā** viññāṇaṭṭhiti. … Ayaṁ **catutthī** viññāṇaṭṭhiti. … Ayaṁ **pañcamī** viññāṇaṭṭhiti. (DN 15 — D ii 69)
- *ayaṁ* this (f. nom. sg.) · *tatiyā* third (f. nom. sg.) · *catutthī* fourth (f. nom. sg.) · *pañcamī* fifth (f. nom. sg.) · *viññāṇaṭṭhiti* station of consciousness (f. nom. sg.)
- "This is the **third** station of consciousness. … This is the **fourth** station of consciousness. … This is the **fifth** station of consciousness."

Nothing about a lunar fortnight is in view; these are stations of consciousness, being counted. The third is *tatiyā* and the fourth and fifth are *catutthī* and *pañcamī*, which is Perniola's rule set out by the text itself. So the feminine of the first three ordinals is in *-ā*, and from the fourth upwards it is normally in *-ī*.[^ordinal-feminine]

What is true in Oberlies's account is narrower than he states it, and the *-ī* forms are most often met within that narrower claim. Three of them have hardened into names for **dates**: the eighth, fourteenth and fifteenth days of the lunar fortnight, the uposatha days:

- Cātuddasī pañcadasī aṭṭhamī ca pakkhassa. (MN 4 — M i 20)
- *cātuddasī* the fourteenth (f.) · *pañcadasī* the fifteenth (f.) · *aṭṭhamī* the eighth (f.) · *ca* and · *pakkhassa* of the fortnight (m. gen. sg.)
- The fourteenth, the fifteenth, and the eighth of the fortnight.

The Bodhisatta is choosing the nights on which to go and sit in the frightening shrines of the forest, and he picks the three the tradition already marks. That is a lexical fact about three words, not a grammatical one about a whole class: *aṭṭhamī* still means simply 'eighth' elsewhere, and DPD accordingly carries it twice, once as the ordinal and once as the day.

[^ordinal-formation]: Perniola §51(a)–(e), pp. 66–67: *paṭhama* from the prefix *pa-* (Sanskrit *pra-*) 'in front' plus *ṭhama*; *dutiya*, *tatiya* with the suffix *-tiya*; *catur-tha* > *catuttha* and *cha-tha* > *chaṭṭha*; *pañc-ama*, *satt-ama*, *aṭṭh-ama*, *nav-ama*, *das-ama* with *-ama*; and for eleven to eighteen either *-ama* or bare *-a*, *ekādas-ama* or *ekādas-a*. Oberlies §43(2), p. 197, states the higher series independently ("The higher numbers (except for '60th') may be formed by adding *°ma-* to the cardinal: *vīsa-*, *vīsatima-* …, *saṭṭhitama-*. They are all inflected as *a*-stems"), and both he and Perniola make 'sixtieth' the exception, *saṭṭhitama-*, where Duroiselle p. 107 gives *saṭṭhima*. **Neither form is canonical.** *Saṭṭhitama-* does not occur at all in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, under any ending; *saṭṭhimo* occurs once, in a grammarian's own list of the numerals (*paññāso, paññāsamo. Saṭṭhyādito purimasuttena mo, saṭṭhimo, sattatimo*), a derivation, not a use. Three grammars therefore disagree about a form the canon never has occasion to write, and 'sixtieth' is better treated as a gap in the attested series than as an irregularity in it. Thānuttamo p. 75, citing Kaccāyana 373 and 666, derives *paṭhama* instead from *putha* + *ama*, and DPD gives a third analysis, *pa* + *tama* > *ṭhama*, that is, the superlative suffix of Chapter 4, which Perniola's bare *ṭhama* leaves unanalysed.

    Duroiselle's second column is not safe to take forms from. At p. 106 he prints a doubled ordinal for four, five, six, seven and ten: *catuttha, turīya*; *pañcatha, pañcama*; *chaṭṭha, chaṭṭhama*; *sattha, sattama*; *dasma*. Of the extra forms, only *chaṭṭhama* is attested, and chiefly in verse (*chaṭṭhamaṁ bhagavā brūhi*, Snp 1.6 — Sn p. 19; also the Buddhavaṁsa, and a Kathāvatthu feminine *chaṭṭhamī*); *turīya*, *pañcatha* and *dasma* do not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, and *turīya* is not in DPD at all, while *sattha* is attested only in its unrelated senses. He was working from the indigenous grammarians, who generate such forms, and he does not mark which of them the canon uses.

[^ordinal-feminine]: Oberlies §43(2), p. 197: "Their feminine is always in *-ā*, while those in *-ī* are used to denote dates (*aṭṭhamī-* '8th day', *cātuddasī-* '14th day' [see CDIAL 4606], *pañcadasī-* '15th [day]')." Perniola §51(g) and p. 68: "The feminine of the first three numbers ends in *-ā*: *paṭhamā, dutiyā, tatityā* [so printed; read *tatiyā*]. The feminine of all the other numeral ordinals ends in *-ī*: *catutthī, pañcamī, ekādasamī / ekādasī, tiṁsatimī / timī*." (His last form is printed *timī*, checked against the page image; it is a second slip of the *tatityā* kind, for *tiṁsī*. Neither spelling is canonical (*tiṁsī* and *tiṁsatimī* occur nowhere and *timī* once, in a ṭīkā), so these are paradigm completions, not forms the texts use.) DN 15 — D ii 69 decides it, and the Kathāvatthu's *antarābhava* discussion decides it again with a masculine in the same breath: *pañcamī sā yoni, chaṭṭhamī sā gati, aṭṭhamī sā viññāṇaṭṭhiti, dasamo so sattāvāso*, three feminines in *-ī* against one masculine in *-o*. DPD gives *aṭṭhamī* under two headwords, 'eighth' (from the Sīvaka Sutta) and 'eighth day of the lunar fortnight' (from the Bhayabherava Sutta), the same division of labour. The *-ā* feminine is not extinct above the third (the Dhammasaṅgaṇī has *catutthāya bhūmiyā* 'of the fourth plane' beside *dutiyāya* and *tatiyāya*), so *-ī* is the norm, not the only option.

- **Dutiyo** pi samaṇo n'atthi. **Tatiyo** pi samaṇo n'atthi. **Catuttho** pi samaṇo n'atthi. (DN 16 — D ii 152)
- There is no **second** (type of) renunciant. There also is no **third** renunciant. There also is no **fourth** renunciant.

### What an ordinal does besides count

Three uses of the ordinal have nothing to do with ranking a series, and all three are common enough to trip anyone who knows only the counting sense.

The accusative is a temporal adverb. *Paṭhamaṁ* means 'the first time', not 'the first', and *dutiyaṁ* 'the second time'. The form is an ordinary neuter accusative singular used adverbially, as the accusative is throughout the language; what makes it need naming is that the English is a different part of speech.[^ordinal-adverb]

Compounded with *atta(n)-* 'self', an ordinal counts a company including oneself. *Attadutiya-* is 'oneself with one companion', literally 'self as second', and *attacatuttha-* is 'oneself with three others'. The idiom counts the speaker in, so the number is always one higher than the number of companions. For a reader of the Vinaya and the Suttas this is live material, since it is how the texts state the size of a travelling party: Ānanda takes his bowl and robe and enters Kusinārā *attadutiyo*, with one companion.[^atta-ordinal]

And an ordinal is occasionally read where a cardinal would be expected. The stock example is *kaṇṭhapañcamehi bandhanehi*, said of a child tied at the four limbs and the neck, and Oberlies takes it as an ordinal standing in for a cardinal: 'with five bonds'. The compound will not quite carry that. Its first member is *kaṇṭha-* 'neck', which the abbreviated citation drops, and with it in place the phrase reads 'with bonds of which the neck is the fifth': the ordinal numbering the last member of a set, not replacing a count. DPD glosses the compound both ways at once, 'in five places' for the sense and 'with neck as the fifth' for the literal, and that is the sound position. So the use is real but this is not its clearest case, and meeting an ordinal where the sense demands a cardinal, look first for a compound of this kind.[^ordinal-for-cardinal]

[^ordinal-adverb]: Oberlies §43(2), p. 197 n. 3: "The acc. of the ordinals is used to form temporal adverbs: *paṭhamaṁ* 'the first time', *dutiyaṁ* 'the second time' (see Geiger §119.3)." Perniola reaches the same forms from the other end, listing them at §54(a) as one of the five ways of forming a **multiplicative**: "by using the accusative neuter of the ordinal numerals: *paṭhamaṁ* 'for the first time', *dutiyaṁ* 'for the second time'". The two descriptions are of one form, and the difference is only whether it is filed under the ordinal or under the multiplicative.

[^atta-ordinal]: Oberlies §43(2), p. 197 n. 4: "Noteworthy are compounds of ordinal numbers with *atta(n)-*: *attaduttiya-* 'oneself with one companion', D II 147,21, *attacatuttha-* 'oneself with three others', M I 393,21." His *attaduttiya-* carries a double *t* that the texts do not. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana has *attadutiyo* and its inflections throughout and no instance of the doubled form; DPD's headword is *attadutiya*, from *atta* + *dutiya*, cited from the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (Oberlies's own D II reference), where Ānanda enters Kusinārā *attadutiyo*. *Attacatuttha-* is right as he gives it, attested as *attacatuttho* and *attacatutthe*.

[^ordinal-for-cardinal]: Oberlies §43(2), p. 197 n. 4: "Sporadically the ordinal number is used instead of the cardinal number: *pañcamehi bandhanehi* 'with five bonds', S IV 201,22, 202,9 (see Geiger §118.4 and Oberlies 1997: 10-11)." The canonical phrase is *kaṇṭhapañcamehi bandhanehi*, not *pañcamehi bandhanehi*. Bare *pañcamehi* occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana; *kaṇṭhapañcamehi* occurs fourteen times in the canon, including the Yavakalāpi Sutta at his own S IV reference and the Vekhanasa Sutta at MN 80. DPD reads *kaṇṭhapañcama* as 'in five places; in arms, legs and neck', literally 'with neck as the fifth'. On that reading the ordinal is the final member of a bahubbīhi with an ordinal sense, so the phrase does not demonstrate what it is cited for. The use itself is reported by Geiger §118.4 independently and is not in doubt; the example is.

### Numerals beyond counting

Four further classes are built on the numerals, and the indigenous tradition treats all four as part of the topic, not as afterthoughts to it. Thānuttamo divides the field into six kinds where a Western grammar tends to give two; the four below are the ones the previous sections have not covered.[^six-classes]

**Multiplicatives** answer 'how many times'. The main formation adds *-kkhattuṁ* to the stem (*sakiṁ* 'once', *dvikkhattuṁ* 'twice', *tikkhattuṁ* 'three times', *dasakkhattuṁ* 'ten times'), and the third of these is one of the most frequently read words in the canon, because it is what a visitor does on arrival and on departure:

- … **tikkhattuṁ** citakaṁ padakkhiṇaṁ katvā bhagavato pāde sirasā vandi. (DN 16 — D ii 163)
- *tikkhattuṁ* three times · *citakaṁ* the pyre (m. acc. sg.) · *padakkhiṇaṁ* keeping to the right · *katvā* having done · *bhagavato* of the Blessed One (m. gen. sg.) · *pāde* the feet (m. acc. pl.) · *sirasā* with his head (n. inst. sg.) · *vandi* paid homage
- … having circumambulated the pyre **three times**, he paid homage with his head at the Blessed One's feet.

Beside *-kkhattuṁ* stand two other means to the same end: the noun *vāra* 'turn, occasion' with a cardinal or an ordinal (*cattāro vārā* 'four times', *catuttha vāre* 'on the fourth occasion'), and the ordinal accusative already met above.

**Adverbs in *-dhā*** divide something into parts: *dvidhā* 'in two parts', *sattadhā* 'in seven parts', *bahudhā* 'in many ways', *sahassadhā* 'in a thousand ways'. The series has no canonical member for 'one', which is reasonable enough in a suffix whose business is dividing.[^ekadha]

[^ekadha]: *Ekadhā* 'in one way' does not head this list, though the pattern of the series invites it. It is a real Pāli word and a common one in the commentaries, but its seven occurrences in the root texts are all in the Visuddhimagga, and it has none in the canon. The corresponding gap in the *-kkhattuṁ* series is filled (*sakiṁ* 'once' serves), so the absence is specific to *-dhā*. A reader meets the seven first, in the warning the Buddha gives Ambaṭṭha when he will not answer a legitimate question put to him twice, with the yakkha Vajirapāṇi standing overhead, blazing iron thunderbolt in hand, waiting for the third:

- … ettheva te **sattadhā** muddhā phalissati. (DN 3 — D i 94)
- *ettheva* < *ettha* here · *eva* right · *te* your (gen. sg.) · *sattadhā* into seven parts · *muddhā* head (m. nom. sg.) · *phalissati* will split
- … **into seven pieces** your head will split, right here.

The related suffix *-so* is **distributive**, not divisive: *anekaso* 'repeatedly', *pañcaso* 'in fives'. Neither of those two is common (the suffix is met far more often on nouns than on numerals), so know the plainer way to distribute, which is simply to say the numeral twice: *aṭṭha aṭṭha* 'eight each'.[^distributive]

**Fractions** are usually built by naming a numeral and a word for 'part': *bhāga*, *kalā*, *aṁsa*, *koṭṭhāsa*. The two may stand apart, so that *kalaṁ … soḷasiṁ* is 'a sixteenth part', which the canon says forty times and never once in the nominative;[^solasi-case] or they may compound, so that *catu* and *bhāga* give *catubhāgaṁ* 'a quarter'.[^fraction-cardinal]

[^fraction-cardinal]: The numeral is not always an ordinal. Perniola §53, p. 68, gives only *soḷasi kalā*, where *soḷasī* is ordinal in shape, and generalising from that one example excludes the commoner type: DPD constructs *catubhāgaṁ* as *catu* + *bhāga*, literally 'four part', on the plain cardinal. 'Half' has words of its own, *aḍḍha* and *upaḍḍha*, and 'a quarter' is *pāda*, literally 'a foot'. The trap is that *aḍḍha-* prefixed to an **ordinal** gives one and a half, two and a half, three and a half, as the section on the higher numerals showed, but prefixed to a **cardinal** it means *half of* that number, so *dasaddha-* is **five**, not 'ten and a half'. Oberlies §43(3), p. 198 with n. 6: *aḍḍha-* added to the next higher ordinal gives *aḍḍhatiya-* 'two and a half' and *aḍḍhuḍḍha-* 'three and a half'; but "If *aḍḍha-* is added to a cardinal the whole compound denotes *half of* the numeral: *dasaddha-* (sic) 'five', Th 1244 (see Geiger §119.2)". Perniola §53 supplies the fraction words *bhāga*, *kalā*, *aṁsa*, *koṭṭhāsa*, the example *soḷasi kalā*, and *aḍḍha* / *upaḍḍha* for 'half' against *pāda* for 'a quarter'.

**Collectives** name a group as a thing in itself, with the suffix *-ka* or *-ya*: *duka* or *dvaya* 'a pair', *tika* or *taya* 'a triad', then *catukka*, *pañcaka*, *chakka*, *sattaka*, *aṭṭhaka*, *dasaka*. These are everywhere in the Abhidhamma, whose sections are named by them, and one of them titles a sutta: the Chachakka Sutta, MN 148, is 'the six sixes'. They also name the largest structure in the Sutta Piṭaka: the Aṅguttara's eleven books are the Book of the Ones, the Twos, the Threes and so on up, and *duka*, *tika*, *catukka* and *pañcaka* are the words that do the naming. Related are the adjectives in *°guṇa-* and *°vidha-*, which say how many kinds or folds: *diguṇa-* 'twofold', *catugguṇa-* 'fourfold', *pañcavidha-* 'of five kinds'.

[^six-classes]: Thānuttamo, *Māgadhabhāsā*, "Numerals (*saṅkhyā*)", p. 72, divides them into cardinal, ordinal, distributive, fractional, multiplicative and substantive, citing Kaccāyana throughout (Kacc 133, 134, 373–6, 380, 397, 646, 666). Oberlies §43(3), p. 198, reaches the same material as a single list of "numeral derivatives" and Perniola gives each a numbered section, §§52–55. The classes are the tradition's; the order here is not, since a reader meets the multiplicatives far more often than the fractions.

[^distributive]: Oberlies §43(3), p. 198 n. 4: "The adverbial suffix *-so* is added to numerals in a distributive sense (see Geiger §119.3). Quite often it is added also to nouns and adjectives (see Norman 1992: 204 [ad Sn 288])." Perniola §52, p. 68, gives the repetition type with *aṭṭha aṭṭha there amacce ca pesayi* 'to each he sent eight elders and eight court officials', and adds *ekekaso* 'one by one'. The *-so* numerals are thinner on the ground than any of these sources suggests, and each should be checked in the texts before it is taught as ordinary. *Ekekaso* occurs once, in a later work. *Anekaso* occurs six times, none of them in the canon — twice in the commentaries and four times in the grammatical texts. *Pañcaso* occurs nowhere, though DPD carries it as a headword, ablative singular of *pañca* against Sanskrit *pañcaśas*, with a grammarian's *pañcapañcaso* for its example. All three are real words of the language; none is a word a reader will meet in a sutta.

[^solasi-case]: The grammars cite this fraction in a nominative the canon does not have. Perniola §53, p. 68, gives it as *soḷasi kalā* and DPD lemmatises it *soḷasī*, feminine, 'one sixteenth', from *soḷasa* + *ī*. Neither nominative occurs in the root texts: *soḷasī* is absent from the root texts and the commentaries alike, with one occurrence apiece in the ṭīkās and outside them. The fraction is only ever the accusative *soḷasiṁ*, forty times in the root texts, agreeing with an accusative *kalaṁ*: *kalaṁ nāgghanti soḷasiṁ* 'they are not worth a sixteenth part' (SN 10.8 — S i 211), and the same idiom closing Dhammapada 70. Bare *soḷasi* attests six times and is not a word. Every one of the six is *soḷasi'nti*, which is *soḷasiṁ* before *ti* with the niggahīta assimilated: a sandhi at the end of a word hiding its true form as thoroughly as one at the beginning would.

### Why this chapter is longer than it looks

A numeral seems the least grammatical thing in a language: a closed list, learnable in an afternoon. Pāli spends more than that. Its numerals decline in three genders below five and stop declining above eighteen; they agree with their noun, or govern it in the genitive, or absorb it into a compound, or stand as an abstract noun and take it as a modifier, and a reader expects only the first of those four. The ordinals form their feminine one way below five and another way above it.

None of that is difficulty for its own sake. A small closed class old enough to have been reshaped several times, and common enough that none of the reshapings was ever tidied away, ends up like this. And the practical return is large, because this canon counts. The four truths, the five aggregates, the six senses, the eightfold path, the twelve links: unable to parse *channaṁ* or *catunnaṁ*, a reader has trouble with the titles of the lists the teaching is built from, and not merely with an occasional sentence.
