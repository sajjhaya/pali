## Sandhi

*Sandhi* is what happens where two sounds meet. The word means 'junction', and the phenomenon is not peculiar to Pāli: English does it constantly, in *isn't* and *I'll* and in the *beauty* that becomes *beauti-* before *-ful*. In Pāli it becomes a problem, not a curiosity, because Pāli writes the result and not the parts. Meet *esevanto* without knowing that three words are inside it, and there is no way to look any of them up.

So this chapter is less about a set of rules than about taking a written form apart. A reader does that dozens of times on any page of the canon, and no other skill so reliably separates those who can work through a sutta from those who cannot.

Two things have to be said before any of the patterns. The joins fall into four kinds, and the four can be worked out instead of memorised. And very often nothing happens at all. A reader is least prepared for that outcome, because a chapter full of rules trains the expectation that a rule must have applied.

### Junction inside a word and between words

The tradition divides sandhi in two, and the division is the right place to start because the two behave differently.

***Akkharasandhi***, 'junction of letters', happens **inside a single word**: where a prefix meets a root, where a root meets a suffix, and at every join in a compound.[^akkhara-pada] Every compound has at least one:

- *ariya* + *sacca* → *ariyasacca* 'noble truth'
- *na* + *ariya* → *anariya* 'ignoble'
- *saṁ* + *yutta* → *saññutta* 'connected'

[^akkhara-pada]: Thānuttamo p. 26, who gives the pair *akkharasandhi* / *padasandhi* after Gair and Karunatillake and Nhu Lien. Perniola divides the same material between two chapters instead of by two names (his ch. II §5 handles the internal joins as part of word-building, his ch. XVII the external ones). That comes to the same distinction, and it shows that internal sandhi belongs to morphology. Most of it is treated in this book under compounds and word formation; this chapter keeps what a reader needs in order to undo a join.

***Padasandhi***, 'junction of words', happens **between two separate words** that are habitually spoken together, and the editions write them joined:

- *na* + *atthi* → *natthi* 'there is not'
- *seyyathā* + *pi* → *seyyathāpi* 'just as'
- *tassa* + *iti* → *tassāti* 'of him, thus'

Perniola makes a point here that is easy to miss. In Sanskrit, external sandhi is the general rule of the sentence: words are joined as a matter of course. In Pāli they are not. Only a much narrower set is joined (adverbs, prepositions, pronouns, numerals, the forms of the verb *as* 'to be', and whatever else habit has run together), because a speaker pronounces those without a break.[^external-restricted] Pāli external sandhi is a fact about particular words, not a rule about sentences.

[^external-restricted]: Perniola §230, p. 285: "In building up a sentence in Sanskrit the words are joined together according to the rules of external sandhi. In Pali the words are kept separate. But as in a sentence the adverbs, the prepositions, the pronouns, the numerals, the forms of the verb *as* — are usually pronounced together with a preceding or a following word, they undergo some changes." He adds that longer words habitually used together are joined too, giving *uṭṭhāya āsanā* → *uṭṭhāyāsanā* 'having risen from the seat'.

Because there is no single scheme for showing where a join is, editors mark them variously or not at all, with an apostrophe, a hyphen, or a circumflex over the surviving vowel:

- *na* + *atthi* written *n'atthi*
- *puna* + *eti* written *puna-r-eti*
- *na* + *ahaṁ* written *nâhaṁ*

None of these is standard, and a reader will meet editions that use one, another or none. Recognising a join is therefore a matter of experience, not typography, and that is the reason this chapter exists.

### When nothing happens

Two vowels meeting do not have to combine. The tradition gives the case a name of its own, ***pakatisandhi***, 'natural junction', where the structural pattern is simply retained, and lists the environments in which it is the expected outcome.[^pakati] Oberlies arrives at the same place from the other direction: a hiatus, he says, is "generally permitted (including in compounds)", and the interesting question is what *bridges* one when a bridge appears.

[^pakati]: Thānuttamo p. 32, with Kaccāyana 23 and 24. His seven environments: a vowel before a consonant; a vowel before a vowel; *i* and *u* before a vowel-initial verb, and before any verb; a vowel before a vocative; a final long vowel not followed by *iti* and not in a compound; and a vowel before a particle beginning with anything other than *a*, *i* or *e*. Oberlies §25, p. 124, opens with the general permission.

The most famous line in the Dhammapada is an instance, and shows how ordinary the case is:

- Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)
- *manopubbaṅgamā* < *mano* mind + *pubbaṅgama* going before · *dhammā* mental states · *manoseṭṭhā* < *mano* + *seṭṭha* chief · *manomayā* < *mano* + *maya* made of
- Mind precedes mental states; mind is their chief, they are made by mind.[^dhamma-gloss]

[^dhamma-gloss]: *Dhammā* here is the contested word, and the gloss above is DPD's, which files this verse under the sense 'mental states' specifically. The Dhammapada commentary reads it narrowly, as the three immaterial aggregates (feeling, perception and formations) with mind as their forerunner (Dhp-a I 22), and Bodhi reads it more widely still: translating the prose parallel at AN 1.56 he has 'qualities', and takes the statement, "in line with Dhp 1 and 2", to be ethical, not psychological: one decides to act before one acts. Nothing in this chapter turns on the choice; it is flagged because a grammar has no business settling it in passing.

Three compounds in six words, and in every one the *-o* of *mano* stands unchanged in front of the following consonant. No rule applies here, and none did.

The practical consequence is the mistake this chapter most wants to prevent, and it is best stated flatly. Meeting two vowels side by side is not evidence that a rule has failed to apply. In *ko imaṁ*, *atha kho āyasmā* and *kassapa etaṁ* the vowels sit next to one another and stay there. Learn the patterns below as things that *may* happen, not as things that must.

### The four junctions

Between two words there are four possible joins, and the count is not arbitrary. A Pāli word may **begin** with either a vowel or a consonant, but it may **end** only in a vowel or in the niggahīta; no Pāli word ends in a consonant. Four combinations, therefore, and no fifth:[^four-cases]

| | followed by a vowel | followed by a consonant |
|---|---|---|
| **ends in a vowel** | *tena* + *upasaṅkami* → *tenupasaṅkami* | *na* + *pajānāti* → *nappajānāti* |
| **ends in the niggahīta** | *ahaṁ* + *api* → *ahampi* | *taṁ* + *ca* → *tañca* |

[^four-cases]: Perniola §230, p. 285, who derives the enumeration this way, alone among the sources consulted. Thānuttamo's four traditional types, p. 26, map onto it without being derived from it: *sarasandhi* is the first cell, *byañjanasandhi* (which he defines as a final vowel meeting an initial consonant) is the second, and *niggahītasandhi* covers both of the third row. His fourth type is *pakatisandhi*, the outcome treated above, which has no cell because it is what happens in any of them.

#### A vowel meets a vowel

Any of four things can happen, and the first two account for most of what a reader will meet.

**One vowel is elided.** Usually the first goes, and the surviving vowel may lengthen if its syllable will take it:[^elision-kacc]

- *tena* + *upasaṅkami* → *tenupasaṅkami* 'he approached by that [way]'
- *cakkhu* + *indriyaṁ* → *cakkhundriyaṁ* 'the faculty of the eye'
- *so* + *ahaṁ* → *sohaṁ* 'so I'
- *puriso* + *asi* → *purisosi* 'you are a man'

[^elision-kacc]: Kaccāyana 12 and 13 (Thānuttamo p. 28). Which of the two vowels goes is not fixed by rule; Perniola §231(b), p. 286, offers the working guide that the vowel of the more important word is retained. *Cakkhundriyaṁ* has 1,262 occurrences in the root texts, so the elision is not a rarity.

**Two vowels contract into one.** Identical vowels give the corresponding long vowel; and a following *iti* regularly lengthens the vowel before it and loses its own *i*:

- *na* + *ahaṁ* → *nāhaṁ* 'not I'
- *tatra* + *ayaṁ* → *tatrāyaṁ* 'there, this'
- *passati* + *iti* → *passatīti* 'thus he sees'
- *sato* + *eva* → *satova* 'mindful indeed'

**Where *a* meets *i* the result is *e*, and where *a* meets *u* it is *o***, which is the same merger that produced those two vowels in the first place:[^ao-merger]

- *upa* + *ikkhati* → *upekkhati* 'he looks on'
- *kaṭṭha* + *udaka* → *-kaṭṭhodaka-*, as in *satiṇakaṭṭhodakaṁ* 'with grass, wood and water'

[^ao-merger]: Kaccāyana 14 (Thānuttamo p. 28); Perniola §6(b), p. 8. The chapter on script and pronunciation gives the historical side: *e* is guttural-palatal because it is *a* plus *i*, and *o* guttural-labial because it is *a* plus *u*.

**A sonant vowel becomes its semivowel.** *I* becomes *y* and *u* becomes *v* when a different vowel follows; the tradition heads this separately as ***ādeso***, 'substitution', and it is not the same thing as a consonant appearing from nowhere, though it looks like one on the page:[^adeso]

- *vi* + *ākaroti* → *byākaroti* 'he explains'
- *su* + *akkhāto* → *svākkhāto* 'well proclaimed'
- *yo* + *ayaṁ* → *yvāyaṁ* 'this [one] who'
- *so* + *eva* → *sveva* 'that very one'

[^adeso]: Kaccāyana 21 and 17 (Thānuttamo pp. 29–30); Perniola §8, pp. 9–10, who derives it from the division of the vowels into pure (*a*, *ā*), sonant (*i*, *ī*, *u*, *ū*) and diphthong (*e*, *o*). A sonant vowel can serve as either a vowel or a consonant, and before another vowel it takes the consonantal role. Where the vowel is *e* or *o* the diphthong is first resolved into its parts, so *so* + *eva* passes through *sa-u* + *eva* to *sveva*. On *byākaroti*: Perniola and most grammars print *vyākaroti*, the Sanskritising spelling, which the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana carries once, in the grammatical literature. The canon has *byākaroti*, 122 times in the root texts, and *byākāsi* 88 times. Pāli turns this initial *v* into *b*, and the *vy-* spelling is imported from Sanskrit, not read off a Pāli text.

#### A vowel meets a consonant

The commonest case is that a consonant simplified in Pāli reappears doubled when a vowel comes before it. Sanskrit *kṣ* is Pāli *kkh*, reduced to *kh* at the start of a word, but put a word in front and the double consonant comes back:[^doubling]

- *na* + *khamati* → *nakkhamati* 'he does not approve'
- *na* + *pajānāti* → *nappajānāti* 'he does not understand'
- *api* + *sudaṁ* → *apissudaṁ* 'moreover'
- *puna* + *puna* → *punappuna* 'again and again'

[^doubling]: Perniola §232, p. 287, who explains the doubling historically: *na khamati* is *na* + *kṣamate*, and the *kkh* that the word lost at its beginning survives in the middle of the joined form. *Punappuna* he derives through *punar puna*, the older *-r* being what the doubling replaces.

A long vowel may also **shorten before a consonant**, particularly in the particles: *vā* becomes *va* where the metre or the phrase wants it lighter.

#### The niggahīta meets a vowel

The niggahīta may stand, may become *m*, may be lost, or may be replaced by *d*.

- *evaṁ* + *eva* → *evameva* 'just so'
- *pāsādaṁ* + *āruyha* → *pāsādamāruyha* 'having climbed the palace'
- *labheyyaṁ* + *ahaṁ* → *labheyyāhaṁ* 'might I obtain'
- *etaṁ* + *avoca* → *etadavoca* 'said this'
- *yaṁ* + *eva* → *yadeva* 'whatever'

The last two are the interesting pair. The *d* is inherited, not inserted: *etad* and *yad* are what these pronouns looked like in Old Indic, and before a vowel the old final consonant surfaces again.[^etad] The same happens with *sammā*, *bahu* and *sakiṁ* before *eva*: *sammadeva*, *bahudeva*, *sakideva*.

[^etad]: Perniola §231(d), p. 287: "The pronouns *ya*, *ta*, followed by a word beginning with a vowel, retain the Sanskrit neuter forms." He lists the *-d* of *sammad eva*, *bahud eva*, *sakid eva* with them.

Where the following word begins with a consonant after all, because the niggahīta was written across a join that the pronunciation closed, the niggahīta takes the nasal of that consonant's own group, as it does inside a word:

- *ahaṁ* + *api* → *ahampi* 'I too'
- *dutiyaṁ* + *api* → *dutiyampi* 'a second time'
- *dukkhaṁ* + *iti* → *dukkhanti* 'thus, suffering'
- *sukhaṁ* + *eva* → *sukhameva* 'happiness indeed'

The canon's definition of the word *pātimokkha* puts both outcomes into one sentence:

- **Pātimokkhanti** ādimetaṁ mukhametaṁ pamukhametaṁ kusalānaṁ dhammānaṁ. Tena vuccati **pātimokkhanti**. (Kd 2 — Vin i 103)
- *pātimokkhanti* < *pātimokkhaṁ* the Monastic Code · *iti* (the quotative) · *ādimetaṁ* < *ādiṁ* first · *etaṁ* this · *mukhametaṁ* < *mukhaṁ* front · *etaṁ* this · *pamukhametaṁ* < *pamukhaṁ* foremost · *etaṁ* this · *kusalānaṁ* of wholesome · *dhammānaṁ* qualities · *tena* therefore · *vuccati* it is called
- "'**Monastic Code**': this is the first, this the front, this the forefront of wholesome qualities. Therefore it is called '**Monastic Code**'."

Three times over, the niggahīta before *e-* becomes *m*; and four words later *pātimokkhaṁ* meets the *t-* of *iti* and takes the dental nasal of that consonant's own group instead.[^adimetam-dpd]

[^adimetam-dpd]: DPD resolves *ādimetaṁ* as *ādiṁ* + *etaṁ*, and glosses the three words as 'first, initial', 'front, foremost, facing' and 'at the head' respectively. *Ādimetaṁ* has 1 root-text occurrence, and so have *mukhametaṁ* and *pamukhametaṁ*: the three stand together in the Uposatha chapter and nowhere else in the canon.

#### The niggahīta meets a consonant

Two cases, and both are the assimilation rule of the previous chapter applied across a word boundary. Before a nasal the niggahīta is absorbed into it; before any other consonant it becomes the nasal of that consonant's group:[^nigg-cons]

- *taṁ* + *ca* → *tañca* 'and that'
- *taṁ* + *eva* → *taññeva* 'that very thing'
- *evaṁ* + *hi* → *evañhi* 'for thus'
- *paṭi* + *saṁ* + *līna* → *paṭisallīno* 'gone into seclusion'
- *saṁ* + *lekha* → *sallekho* 'self-effacement'

[^nigg-cons]: Perniola §234, p. 288; Kaccāyana 31–33 and 39–41 (Thānuttamo pp. 31–32). *Evañhi* and *taññeva* show *h* and *y* taking the palatal nasal, the two consonants that belong to no group. The *y* of *taññeva* is the hiatus glide of the next section, and the editions print the join at both stages: *taṁyeva* 31 times in the root texts, *taññeva* 61.

But the writing does not always follow. *Yaṁ* + *nūna* is analysed as *yannūna*, and the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *yaṁnūna* — 94 times in the root texts, niggahīta intact. The rule describes what a reciter does; whether an editor writes it is a separate question, and the answer varies by edition. Expect the spelling to record the pronunciation and you will be surprised twice: once by the joins that are written and once by the ones that are not.

### What bridges a hiatus

When two vowels meet and a consonant turns up between them, the consonant has one of two quite different origins, and the sources that treat them as one make the language look more arbitrary than it is.

Sometimes it is a **glide**: a consonant with no history, inserted to close the gap because a gap is awkward to say. *Y* and *v* are the commonest, and *m* occurs too:

- *añña* + *añña* → *aññamañña* 'one another'[^annamanna-m]
- *dvi* > *di* + *aḍḍha* → *diyaḍḍha* 'one and a half'
- *na* + *idaṁ* → *nayidaṁ* 'this is not'

Sometimes it is a **relic**: an Old Indic final consonant that had been lost at the end of the word and reappears when the word is no longer at the end of anything:

- *yāva* + *eva* → *yāvadeva* 'just as far as', from *yāvad*
- *puna* + *eti* → *punareti* 'comes back again', from *punar*

Oberlies puts both under one heading and keeps them apart inside it: a hiatus "can be bridged by (a) a glide or (b) a (so-called) *sandhi* consonant, which may be a relic from OIA or analogically inserted".[^oberlies-hiatus] That last qualification matters most. Once a language has a stock of words whose *d* or *r* comes back before a vowel, it can extend the habit to words that never had one, so the class is mixed, and no rule shows which member is in front of you. What you can do is stop expecting the consonant to belong to either word, for that expectation makes such forms impossible to look up.

[^oberlies-hiatus]: Oberlies §25, p. 124. His examples of the glide inside words include *aññamañña*, *diyaḍḍha* ~ *duvaḍḍha* 'one and a half' and *piṇḍiyālopa* 'a morsel of food'. Perniola §231(d), p. 287, had already noticed half the distinction, listing *dhīratthu*, *pāturahosi*, *tasmātiva*, *yāvadeva* and *punarahosi* under the observation that "often there is no new consonant inserted, but the original Sanskrit final consonant is restored".

[^annamanna-m]: The classification is Oberlies's (§25, p. 124), and DPD does not share it: its Sanskrit column gives *anyam* + *anya*, which makes the *m* the old accusative ending of the first member, not a consonant inserted to fill a gap. *Lahumessati* (Dhp 369) goes the same way: DPD reads *lahuṁ* + *eti*, again an accusative. Both are in the mixed class this section is about, and neither reading can be read off the form of the word.

> **A wrong division invents a rule**
>
> Geiger cites *ādicco-v-udayaṁ* (It 85,4) as an instance of a *v*-glide. Oberlies: "But this is, of course, *ādicco v' udayaṁ* 'like the rising sun'." The *v* is what is left of the particle *iva*, not a glide, and once the phrase is divided correctly there is nothing to explain. He reports the same about *ubhaya-v-okiṇṇa-*, which is *ubhaya-vokiṇṇa-*.
>
> The warning generalises, and it applies directly to what this chapter teaches. Undoing a sandhi wrongly does not produce a wrong word; it produces a rule that does not exist, and the rule then looks like evidence for itself. Where a division is uncertain, the test is whether both of the resulting pieces are words that occur elsewhere, not whether the division is possible.

### Consonants meeting consonants

Inside a word, two consonants that come together usually do not stay as they are. Which of them gives way is not a matter of chance: Perniola ranks the consonants by strength (the mutes strongest, then the sibilant *s*, then the nasals, then *l*, *v*, *y* and *r*), and the rule follows in two clauses.[^strength] If the two are of **equal** strength, the first assimilates to the second. If they are of **unequal** strength, the weaker assimilates to the stronger.

[^strength]: Perniola §12, p. 15. He notes that *h* enters only a few combinations, and that *ḷ* and *ḷh* enter none.

- *sat* + *kāra* → *sakkāra* 'honour': two mutes, the first gives way
- *sak* + *noti* → *sakkoti* 'he is able': mute against nasal, the nasal gives way
- *vac* + *ssati* → *vakkhati* 'he will speak': palatal to guttural, then assimilation
- *labh* + *ta* → *laddha* 'obtained': both change: the second is voiced by the first, then the first assimilates to it
- *rāj* + *nā* → *raññā* 'by the king': the second is palatalised by the first, then the first assimilates to it

The direction has names, and the grammars use them. Assimilation is **progressive** when the first consonant makes the second like itself, as in *sakkoti*; **regressive** when the second makes the first like itself, as in *sakkāra*; and **reciprocal** when both move, as in *laddha* and *raññā*.[^directions] The nasal of a group behaves slightly differently and has its own name, **adaptation**: a nasal before a mute simply shifts to the nasal of that mute's group, the niggahīta rule seen once more: *saṁ* + *tiṭṭhati* gives *santiṭṭhati*, *saṁ* + *carati* gives *sañcarati*, *saṁ* + *ṭhāna* gives *saṇṭhāna*.

[^directions]: Perniola §11(a)–(b), pp. 13–15. He lists four further processes under the same section which belong to word formation, not to reading, and are treated in this book under compounds and word formation: metathesis (*gah-ṇāti* → *gaṇhāti*), epenthesis (*rāj-no* → *rājino*), aspiration (*as-ti* → *atthi*) and simplification (*bhed-ssati* → *bhecchati*).

The practical value of all this to a reader is narrow but real. A doubled consonant in the middle of a word very often marks a join, and knowing which single consonants collapse into which double one lets the join be found.

> **Assimilation across manuscript traditions**
>
> Since there are no strict rules, different manuscript traditions have resolved the same junction differently. *Kho ahaṁ* appears as *khvāhaṁ* in Burmese manuscripts, as *khohaṁ* in others, and unassimilated as *kho ahaṁ* elsewhere. It is therefore common to find several spellings of one junction, internal or external, with none of them more correct than the rest.
>
> Older grammars tend to treat Pāli as a derivative of Sanskrit and to explain its sandhi accordingly. Pāli is now generally understood as a sister of Sanskrit, not a daughter, influenced by Sanskrit rules later, at the period of writing. So a Sanskritising spelling like *vyākaroti* can stand in a grammar for a form the canon does not have.

#### Sandhi in verse

Verse takes more liberties than prose, because the words have to fit the metre. Junctions appear there that would be unusual otherwise: *yaṁ taṁ santaṁ* → *yantasantaṁ*, *navaṁ na atthi* → *navanatthi*.

The pressure also runs the other way. The Metta Sutta's *puttamāyusā* is written unassimilated as *puttaṁ āyusā* in several editions, and the unassimilated reading breaks the metre, a useful reminder that an editor's word division is an editorial act. It also explains how a form can be canonical and yet hard to find in a digital text: a search for *āyusā* as a word finds nothing in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, because the line is printed joined. Chapter 18 takes up what the metre does to these lines; here it is enough that a junction which looks irregular in verse is often the metre's work, not the language's.

### The canon undoing a join

Undoing a join is not only a modern reader's problem. The Vinaya does it on its own pages: each rule of the Pātimokkha is followed by a word-analysis, the *padabhājanīyaṁ*, 'the analysis of the rule', literally 'the sentence to be divided', which takes the rule apart word by word and states what each word covers.[^padabhajaniya] Every entry opens by quoting its word as the rule prints it, and the rule prints it joined.

- Yo **panāti** yo yādiso … Eso vuccati '**yo panā**'ti. (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 23)
- *yo panāti* < *yo* whoever · *pana* moreover · *iti* (the quotative) · *yo* whoever · *yādiso* of whatever kind · *eso* this · *vuccati* is called · *yo panā'ti* the same three elements again, the join now marked, not written
- "'**Whoever**': whoever, of whatever kind … This is what is called '**whoever**'."

[^padabhajaniya]: DPD, s.v. *padabhājanīya*: '(vinaya) analysis of the rule', literally 'sentence to be divided'. The texts have the neuter *padabhājanīyaṁ*, and more often the shorter *padabhājanaṁ*, almost entirely in the commentaries. The analysis has a name in the tradition, not in the canon. Brahmāli, in the general introduction to his Vinaya translation ("The Sutta-vibhaṅga"), renders the section this belongs to as 'the Analysis of the Sutta', noting that the *sutta* meant is the Pātimokkha and not a discourse.

The entry opens with the lemma joined (*pana* has swallowed the *i* of *iti* and lengthened its own *a* in compensation) and closes by giving the lemma back with the join marked instead of written. This is the *-ti* that gave *passatīti* above, acting on a short *a* instead of a short *i*. The closing form is instructive. The *ā* of *panā* belongs to neither *pana* nor *iti* but to their meeting, and an editor who divides the phrase has to leave it standing somewhere.[^pana-leftover] A division does not restore the words.

[^pana-leftover]: A leftover piece can look like a word and still not be one. *Panā* has 6 root-text occurrences, every one of them in the Mahāniddesa and every one in a single verse line, *Saddhammapūjāpi panā tatheva*; DPD carries no headword for it. That qualifies the test given below, which checks a division by asking whether both resulting pieces occur elsewhere. The test rules a division out. It does not rule one in.

The other entries do the same on other vowels. The next lemma is *bhikkhūti*, from *bhikkhu* + *iti* (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 24); and the *sekhiya* rules, 'the rules of training', are built on a first-person future welded the same way:

- “Parimaṇḍalaṁ **nivāsessāmīti** sikkhā karaṇīyā”ti. (Bu Sk 1 — Vin iv 185)
- *parimaṇḍalaṁ* evenly all round · *nivāsessāmīti* < *nivāsessāmi* I shall dress · *iti* (the quotative) · *sikkhā* the training · *karaṇīyā* to be done
- "'**I shall dress** evenly all round': this is the training to be done."

Three vowels and one junction: whatever short vowel stands before the quotative lengthens, and *iti* loses its *i*.

For a reader searching a text this is worse than the case of *āyusā* above, where the word was hidden inside a joined form but every letter of it was still there to be found. Here the word's own last vowel has changed, so the form a dictionary files it under is not even contained in the printed text. *Nivāsessāmi* does not occur in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all, while *nivāsessāmīti* has 2 root-text occurrences, one in each Vibhaṅga's sekhiya section. The sekhiya rules are built this way almost without exception, so a search for any of them under its dictionary form finds nothing.

One rule breaks the pattern, and it shows where the fault lies. At Bu Sk 36 the future is not the last word before the quotative, and it stands there in the form a dictionary would give it.[^sekhiya-position] The position hides the form, not the verb.

[^sekhiya-position]: *Na sūpaṁ vā byañjanaṁ vā odanena paṭicchādessāmi bhiyyokamyataṁ upādāyāti sikkhā karaṇīyā* (Bu Sk 36 — Vin iv 192), 'I shall not cover curry or condiment with rice out of a desire for more'. The quotative here attaches to the absolutive *upādāya* and not to the verb, so the verb is left bare, and *paṭicchādessāmi* has 1 root-text occurrence, which is this one. DPD glosses *bhiyyokamyatā* as 'desire for more'.

### Where a sandhi gets resolved

One rule of this book's own follows from everything above, and it is stated here because this is the chapter that earns it.

A resolved sandhi belongs in the gloss, never in the quotation. When a passage is cited, the first line gives the text of the edition, *Esevanto dukkhassā* (SN 12.51 — S ii 84), and the resolution goes on the line beneath, where it is true: *esevanto* < *eso* + *eva* + *anto*. The impulse to print the unpacked form on the quotation line is a teacher's impulse and a sound one, but *eso eva anto* is a sequence no manuscript contains, and a book that puts it inside quotation marks has composed a Pāli example, not cited one.

That is why this book gives every example in three layers, not two. The middle layer is where each join this chapter teaches is undone.

### Two habits

Two habits carry the whole of this chapter, and they pull against each other. When a word will not parse, suspect a junction before you suspect the dictionary: take off a *ti*, a *pi* or an *eva*, put back the vowel the junction ate, and look up what is left. And when two vowels stand side by side, do not go looking for the rule that failed. *Ko imaṁ* is two words that met and did nothing. With both habits you undo the joins the canon made and leave alone the ones it did not.
