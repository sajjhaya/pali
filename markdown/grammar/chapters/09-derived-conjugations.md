## Passive, Causative and Denominative

Everything in Chapters 7 and 8 was a matter of *endings*: the same root, dressed for a person, a number, a tense, a mood. This chapter leaves the endings alone and changes the stem underneath them.

Pāli can take a root and build from it a stem that means something the root did not, *is done* beside *does* and *causes to do* beside *does*, and then conjugate that stem as Chapter 7 conjugates any other. So there is no new paradigm to learn here, and that needs saying at the outset, because the traditional name for these formations, "the secondary conjugations", suggests a second set of tables. There is one set of endings, and there are several ways of building the thing they attach to.

### Five stems, and only three of them are alive

The inherited categories are five: passive, causative, desiderative, intensive, denominative. Of the grammars consulted, only Oberlies says that they are not five equal things.

> Of derived present stems only the causative, the passive and the denominative are productive categories, while the desiderative and the intensive have been preserved only in a few historical forms.[^oberlies-44]

[^oberlies-44]: Oberlies §44 (pp. 199–200). "Productive" is the word that matters. A productive category is one a speaker can extend to a new verb, and an unproductive one survives only in the words that already carried it. His examples of the surviving desideratives and intensives are *jigucchati*, *tikicchati*, *sussūsati*, *lālappati* and *jaṅgamati*: five words for two whole categories, against the hundreds of causatives in the canon.

*Productive* means a reader can meet a causative of a verb no grammar lists, work it out, and be right. It also means the reverse: a desiderative of a verb no grammar lists is almost certainly not a desiderative, because Pāli stopped making them long before the canon was composed. The practical consequence is a division of labour. Three of these formations are rules to be learnt and applied; two are short lists of words to be recognised.

| Stem | Marker | Sense | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passive | *-ya-* | is done, is undergone | productive |
| Causative | *-e-* / *-aya-*, *-āpe-* / *-āpaya-* | causes to do | productive |
| Denominative | *-ya-*, *-āya-*, *-īya-*, *-e-* | acts as, treats as, makes into | productive |
| Desiderative | reduplication + *-sa-* | wishes to do | a few relics |
| Intensive | reduplication | does repeatedly, does intensely | a few relics |

Two things in that table look like mistakes. The passive and the denominative share a marker, *-ya-*; so do the causative and the denominative, *-e-*. The overlap is real, and it explains why the grammars disagree about how to file individual words. The section on the denominative returns to it.

### The passive

The passive is a stem in *-ya-*, and it takes ordinary active endings. Chapter 7 made the point in advance: the personal endings cannot show whether a verb is active or passive, because a passive verb has the same ones.[^stem-not-endings] *Vuccati* looks like *pacati* and means 'is called'.

[^stem-not-endings]: Warder p. 51: "The inflections are the same as in the active. It is the stem which shows whether a verb is active or passive." Oberlies §53, p. 254, opens with the same observation in the same words: the opposition "is shown not by the endings … but by the stems". A coursebook and a historical grammar seldom agree so closely, and their agreement is a sign that the point is no simplification for beginners.

The affix reaches the stem by three routes, and a reader needs to recognise all three because the first two disguise it completely.

| Route | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ya-* straight onto a consonant-final root, assimilated | the *y* is swallowed by the root's final consonant, leaving a double consonant | *vac* + *ya* > *vuccati* 'is called'; *han* + *ya* > *haññati* 'is killed'; *labh* + *ya* > *labbhati* 'is obtained'; *chid* + *ya* > *chijjati* 'is cut'; *dis* + *ya* > *dissati* 'is seen' |
| *-ī-* + *-ya-* onto a vowel-final root | a long *ī* appears before the *y*, and the root's own vowel is often lost | *dā* > *dīyati* 'is given'; *hā* > *pahīyati* 'is abandoned'; *ñā* > *ñāyati* 'is known'; *nī* > *nīyati* 'is led' |
| *-iya-* onto the present stem | the stem's final vowel goes and *-iya-* is added | *ukkhipati* > *ukkhipiyati* 'is raised up'; *deseti* > *desiyati* 'is taught'; *karoti* > *karīyati* 'is done' |

The first route hides the affix. *Vuccati* has no visible *y* at all, and an unwarned reader will parse it as an ordinary first-conjugation present of some root *vucc-*.[^vuccati-derivation] The clue is the double consonant where the root has a single one: *labhati* 'gets' beside *labbhati* 'is got'; *chindati* 'cuts' beside *chijjati* 'is cut'.

The clue is useful but cannot be trusted: a doubled consonant is only a reason to look. Pāli produces the same doubling by half a dozen other routes (*gacchati* from *gam-*, *passati* from *dis-*, *tiṭṭhati* from *ṭhā-*), and by count a doubled consonant is no more often a passive than not.[^passive-tell] Two further tests decide the matter. A passive has an active counterpart with the simple consonant standing beside it in the language, *labhati* against *labbhati*, and its sense is one of undergoing. Neither test alone is enough, and the doubling on its own is the weakest of the three.

[^passive-tell]: Counted over DPD's early-texts export, which carries a morphological construction for 54,038 headwords. Of the present-tense verbs in the early texts showing a true doubled consonant before *-ati*, 179 are built with the *-ya-* suffix and 336 are not, so the doubling test applied alone would be wrong about two in three. Nor are the 179 all passives, because the *-ya-* of the passive is the same morph as the third conjugation's class sign (Appendix A.4), so *nirujjhati* 'ceases' and *maññati* 'thinks' are *-ya-* stems and no one would call them passive.

[^vuccati-derivation]: Perniola §89a(4), p. 99, derives it in steps: *vac-ya-ti* > *vc-ya-ti* > *uc-ya-ti* > *uccati*, with a *v-* prefixed to the initial *u-* to give *vuccati*. The same treatment yields *vussati* 'is dwelt in' from *vas-*, which has forty-two occurrences in the root texts, so the sequence is a rule and not a one-off. Perniola's third example of it, *vuppati* 'is sown' from *vap-*, occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, neither as a word nor inside one. That is a fact about this edition, and not about the language. The form belongs to one line of the Theragāthā, on hope as what makes a farmer sow, and the editions divide over it: the Pali Text Society prints *vuppati* in its running text, the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints *vappati*, and the Buddha Jayanti records the first as the PTS reading. The PTS apparatus divides its own manuscripts two against three. Geiger §175.3 takes the form from that verse. So *vuppati* is one editor's reading of one line, which is a different thing from a form nobody ever wrote.

Sakka's names, in a sutta given over entirely to explaining them, are a page of passives:

- *Sakko, bhikkhave, devānamindo pubbe manussabhūto samāno sakkaccaṁ dānaṁ adāsi, tasmā sakkoti vuccati.* (SN 11.12 — S i 229)
- *sakko* Sakka (m. nom. sg.) · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *devānam* of the gods (m. gen. pl.) · *indo* lord (m. nom. sg.) · *pubbe* formerly · *manussabhūto* being human (m. nom. sg.) · *samāno* while (m. nom. sg.) · *sakkaccaṁ* respectfully · *dānaṁ* a gift (n. acc. sg.) · *adāsi* gave (3 sg. aor.) · *tasmā* therefore · *sakko* 'Sakka' (m. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation) · *vuccati* he is called (3 sg. pres. pass.)
- "Monks, in a former life, when Sakka lord of gods was a human being, he gave a gift respectfully; therefore **he is called** Sakka."

The thing acted on stands in the nominative and the verb agrees with it; the agent, if it is named at all, stands in the instrumental. That is the whole syntax of the passive, and its second half needs stressing, because so often the agent is not named. Pāli reaches for a passive when there is no one to name:

- *yoniso ca kho, bhikkhave, manasikaroto anuppannā ceva āsavā na uppajjanti, uppannā ca āsavā pahīyanti.* (MN 2 — M i 7)
- *yoniso* wisely · *ca kho* but · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *manasikaroto* for one attending (m. gen. sg. prp.) · *anuppannā* unarisen (m. nom. pl.) · *ceva* and · *āsavā* the taints (m. nom. pl.) · *na* not · *uppajjanti* arise (3 pl. pres.) · *uppannā* arisen (m. nom. pl.) · *ca* and · *āsavā* the taints (m. nom. pl.) · *pahīyanti* are abandoned (3 pl. pres. pass.)
- "But for one who attends wisely, monks, unarisen taints do not arise and arisen taints **are abandoned**."

Nobody abandons them, and the passive lets the sentence say so.

The Vinaya's word-analyses reach for it a third way. Every rule in the Vibhaṅga is followed by an analysis of the rule's own wording, phrase by phrase, and an entry that has finished defining a term closes by naming that term again, in the passive, with *vuccati*:

- So tena saddhiṁ natthi. **Tena vuccati** 'asaṁvāso'ti. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 47)
- *so* he (m. nom. sg.) · *tena* with that (m. inst. sg.) · *saddhiṁ* together with · *natthi* there is not · *tena* for that reason (inst. sg.) · *vuccati* is called (3 sg. pres. pass.) · *asaṁvāso* 'excluded from the community' (m. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation)
- "He has no part in that; **therefore** he **is called** 'excluded from the community'."

The term being closed off there is *saṁvāsa*, 'community', which the preceding line defines as joint legal procedures, a joint recitation, and the same training. Look at the case of the two pronouns. Both are instrumentals of *ta*, with two words between them, and neither of them is the agent. The first is governed by *saddhiṁ* 'together with' and marks accompaniment; the second marks cause, where the parallel formula treated in Chapter 12 has the ablative *tasmā* instead.[^tena-vuccati] So an instrumental beside a passive verb raises a question about the sentence and does not answer it. The rule above does not run backwards.

[^tena-vuccati]: Both senses are in Chapter 15's list for the instrumental — accompaniment, reinforced by *saddhiṁ*, and cause. Wijesekera §62 is the authority for holding the agent apart from the rest of that list, where modern grammarians tend to file it under means. The ablative parallel is the Buddha's derivation of *rūpa* from *ruppati* at SN 22.79 — S iii 86, quoted in Chapter 12, where *tasmā* stands where *tena* stands here. And the same *tena vuccati* closes the analysis of the rule's other defined term shortly before, at Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 47, so the formula is the section's habit, not a feature of this one entry.

In the canon the passive is almost entirely third person. Neither *vuccati* nor *pahīyati* (between them nearly five thousand occurrences in the root texts) is attested in the first or second person at all, anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana.[^passive-third-person] The reason is practical: the subject of a passive verb is the thing acted on, and the things acted on in the suttas are taints, fetters, feelings and doctrines, none of which speak. So the forms a reader needs are *-ti* and *-nti*, and the rest of the paradigm is possible but seldom used.

[^passive-third-person]: *Vuccati* has 4,693 occurrences in the root texts and *vuccanti* 527; *vuccasi*, *vuccāmi*, *vuccatha* and *vuccāma* have none anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. *Pahīyati* has 177 and *pahīyanti* 135; *pahīyasi*, *pahīyāmi*, *pahīyatha* and *pahīyāma* likewise none. This is a statement about two verbs, and a first person passive is perfectly well formed; but the two are the commonest passives in the canon, and their distribution is what a reader will meet.

The passive stem is a stem like any other, and takes the whole system. Once *pahīya-* exists it inflects for tense and mood as freely as *gaccha-* does: *pahīyissanti* 'they will be abandoned' at DN 9, *vimucciṁsu* 'they were liberated' as an aorist, *bhaññamāne* 'while being spoken' as a present participle. The last two stand in one sentence, the sentence that closes the second discourse:

- *Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne pañcavaggiyānaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ anupādāya āsavehi cittāni vimucciṁsūti.* (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)
- *imasmiṁ* this (n. loc. sg.) · *ca pana* and · *veyyākaraṇasmiṁ* exposition (n. loc. sg.) · *bhaññamāne* being spoken (n. loc. sg. prp. pass.) · *pañcavaggiyānaṁ* of the group of five (m. gen. pl.) · *bhikkhūnaṁ* of the monks (m. gen. pl.) · *anupādāya* without grasping (absol.) · *āsavehi* from the taints (m. abl. pl.) · *cittāni* the minds (n. nom. pl.) · *vimucciṁsu* were liberated (3 pl. aor. pass.) · *ti* (closing the narrator's report)
- "And while this exposition **was being spoken**, the minds of the group of five monks **were liberated** from the taints by not grasping."

*Bhaññamāne* is a locative absolute, which belongs to Chapter 16, but its stem belongs here: the passive *bhañña-*, from *bhaṇati* 'speaks', with the *y* assimilated as in *haññati*.[^bhannati-note]

[^bhannati-note]: The finite present *bhaññati* that the grammars give as the headword of this passive occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. The canon has the participle instead, *bhaññamāne* forty times in the root texts alone, so a reader will meet the stem without ever meeting the form the dictionary files it under. The pattern is common enough with passives to deserve a name.

A last warning, and it catches many readers: a stem in *-iya-* is not always a passive. A small group of verbs wear the passive shape and mean something active:

- *attā me vediyati* (DN 15 — D ii 66)
- *attā* my self (m. nom. sg.) · *me* my (gen. sg.) · *vediyati* feels (3 sg. pres.)
- "my self **feels**"

*Vediyati* has the form of a passive of *vedeti* and the sense of an active verb, 'feels, experiences'. So do *ādiyati* 'takes up', *sādiyati* 'consents to', *samādiyati* 'undertakes' and *ruccati* 'is pleased with'. Oberlies's explanation is that these are the middle voice's inheritance: the middle died, its work of marking an action done for oneself had to go somewhere, and some of it went to this small class of *-iya-* stems.[^passive-as-active] For a reader the rule is that the shape raises a question, and the sense of the sentence answers it.

[^passive-as-active]: Oberlies §44, p. 199, where the observation is made in the same breath as the loss of the middle: its function "has been partly taken over by the causative, and passives in *°iyati* are sporadically used as such". The list he gives is *ādiyati*, *vediyati* and *sādiyati*, with *uttariyati* and *ruccati* compared. All are canonical and common (*sādiyati* has two hundred occurrences in the root texts), so this is a class a reader will meet early.

Last, a matter of proportion: most passive sentences in Pāli have no finite passive verb. The commonest way of saying "the mind is liberated" is *cittaṁ vimuttaṁ*, with a past participle and no finite verb at all, and *cittaṁ vimuccati* is the rarer. Warder teaches passive *sentences* at his lesson 7 and the passive *conjugation* only at lesson 9, and in that order on purpose. So this section has taught the rarer of two roads to the same English. Chapter 10 takes the other one.

### The causative

The causative is a stem in *-e-* or *-aya-*, built on the root, usually with the root vowel strengthened. The strengthening makes a causative visible: *pat-* 'fall' gives *pāteti* 'fells', *jan-* 'be born' gives *janeti* 'produces', *dis-* 'see' gives *deseti* 'points out, teaches'.

There is a second suffix, *-āpe-* / *-āpaya-*, with the same function, and it can be added to almost anything, including a causative that already exists. *Kāreti* 'has (something) made' becomes *kārāpeti*; *gaṇhāti* 'seizes' becomes *gāhāpeti* 'causes to be seized'.[^double-causative]

[^double-causative]: Oberlies §52, p. 253, calls the *-āpe-* suffix "abstracted and added to any verb stem", which is what productivity looks like from the inside. He notes that where the base is itself a causative the result has a double-causative sense, *ṭhapāpeti* 'orders to be erected' from *ṭhapeti* 'erects', but also that *chedāpeti* and *gāhāpeti* are blends, and that in them "derivation and base seem to have identical meanings". Warder p. 79 reports the same thing from the other end, calling *ropāpeti* a "triple" form. The practical upshot is that a second *-āp-* does not reliably add a second causation, and a reader should not count the layers.

Since the causative stem ends in *-e-*, it conjugates like *deseti* in Chapter 7, and that is no coincidence: *deseti* is itself a causative that has stopped being one.

| Person | Active |
|---|---|
| 1 sg. | *bhāvemi* |
| 2 sg. | *bhāvesi* |
| 3 sg. | *bhāveti* |
| 1 pl. | *bhāvema* |
| 2 pl. | *bhāvetha* |
| 3 pl. | *bhāventi* |

*Bhāveti* is the causative of *bhavati* 'is', so it means literally 'causes to be'. It is also the ordinary Pāli word for developing or cultivating a meditation, a good early lesson in how far a causative can travel from its root.[^bhaveti-attested]

[^bhaveti-attested]: Every cell above is canonical except *bhāvema*, which appears only in the commentaries; the ending is ordinary and the verb simply happens not to take it in this edition, so the cell is a specimen like any other in a paradigm. *Bhāveti* itself has 1,574 occurrences in the root texts, which makes it one of the commonest verbs in the canon and the causative a reader will meet first.

Only the syntax is new here, and it comes down to counting. The agent who causes stands in the nominative, as any agent does; the tradition calls this agent the *hetukattā*, 'the causal agent'. The person or thing *made* to act, which would have been the subject if the verb were not causative, is displaced into the accusative, or sometimes the instrumental. So a causative verb governs one more object than its base verb did.[^warder-counting]

[^warder-counting]: Warder pp. 79–80, who works the arithmetic through: an intransitive verb takes no patient and its causative takes one, a verb with one patient gives a causative with two, and a verb with two gives one with three — adding that with three "in practice the extravagance seems to be avoided". His illustrative sentence for the three-patient case comes from Kaccāyana, not from the canon, and for that reason it is not reproduced here.

The Vinaya, which has to legislate for what a monk does and for what he gets someone else to do, gives the minimal pair in a single rule:

- *Yo pana bhikkhu pathaviṁ khaṇeyya vā khaṇāpeyya vā, pācittiyan'ti.* (Bu Pc 10 — Vin iv 33)
- *yo pana* whatever · *bhikkhu* monk (m. nom. sg.) · *pathaviṁ* the earth (f. acc. sg.) · *khaṇeyya* should dig (3 sg. opt.) · *vā* or · *khaṇāpeyya* should have (it) dug (3 sg. opt. caus.) · *vā* or · *pācittiyaṁ* an offence entailing confession (n. nom. sg.) · *ti* (quotation)
- "Whatever monk **should dig** the earth or **should have** it **dug**: an offence entailing confession."

*Khaṇeyya* and *khaṇāpeyya* are the same optative ending on two stems, one plain and one causative, and the whole legal weight of the rule rests on the syllable *-āp-*. The analysis that follows the rule then runs the pair three times over, on three different verbs, in a single line:

- Pathaviyā pathavisaññī **khaṇati** vā **khaṇāpeti** vā, **bhindati** vā **bhedāpeti** vā, **dahati** vā **dahāpeti** vā, āpatti pācittiyassa. (Bu Pc 10 — Vin iv 33)
- *pathaviyā* in earth (f. loc. sg.) · *pathavisaññī* perceiving it as earth (m. nom. sg.) · *khaṇati* digs · *vā* or · *khaṇāpeti* has it dug (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *bhindati* breaks · *bhedāpeti* has it broken (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *dahati* burns · *dahāpeti* has it burned (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *āpatti* there is an offence (f. nom. sg.) · *pācittiyassa* of confession (n. gen. sg.)
- "If it is earth and he perceives it as earth, and he **digs** it or **has** it **dug**, **breaks** it or **has** it **broken**, **burns** it or **has** it **burned**: an offence entailing confession."

Look at *bhedāpeti*: its base is *bhindati* 'breaks', whose root is *bhid-*, and the causative shows *bhed-*. This is the strengthening the section opened with, here under the *-āpe-* suffix instead of a bare *-e-*. The other two roots have *a* in them, which does not strengthen, so in those two only the suffix shows.[^apeti-graded]

[^apeti-graded]: DPD gives all three as causatives of the expected base (*khaṇāpeti* "caus of *khaṇati*", √*khaṇ*; *bhedāpeti* "caus of *bhindati*", √*bhid*; *dahāpeti* "caus of *dahati*", √*ḍah*) and cites this rule for the first two. *Dahati* here is the verb of burning, not its homonym 'places', which is √*dhā*.

This pairing is a habit of the Vinaya and of almost nothing else. A rule has to reach the monk who acts and the monk who arranges for the act, so the Vibhaṅga writes both verbs out and coordinates them. As a result the canon's cleanest evidence for the productivity of *-āpe-*, a verb and its own causative side by side in one breath, is concentrated in a single basket. Counted as the exact coordination, a verb, *vā*, its own *-āpe-* causative, *vā*, there are twenty-three distinct verb pairs and a hundred and five occurrences, and every one of them is in the Vinaya.[^apeti-vinaya] Oberlies's claim that the suffix can be "added to any verb stem" describes the language, and the Vinaya puts it to use.

[^apeti-vinaya]: Counted over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana root texts by shelf: 23 pairs and 105 occurrences in the five Vinaya books, none in the 41 files of the Sutta Piṭaka and none in the Abhidhamma. The narrowness of that search accounts for some of the contrast, since *vā … vā* is itself legal style, so the count was loosened as a control: any *-āpe-* causative standing within sixty characters of its own base verb, in any coordination at all, gives 124 in the Vinaya against 8 in the Sutta Piṭaka and still none in the Abhidhamma. The construction is therefore not unknown outside the Vinaya, but the rest of the canon seldom uses it.

One more example shows the extra object arriving:

- *Tena kho pana samayena aññataro māṇavako mātaraṁ jīvitā voropesi.* (Kd 1 — Vin i 88)
- *tena samayena* at that time · *kho pana* now · *aññataro* a certain (m. nom. sg.) · *māṇavako* young man (m. nom. sg.) · *mātaraṁ* his mother (f. acc. sg.) · *jīvitā* from life (n. abl. sg.) · *voropesi* deprived (3 sg. aor. caus.)
- "Now at that time a certain young man **deprived** his mother **of** life."

*Voropeti* is a causative of *orohati* 'descends', literally 'causes to come down from', and it governs the accusative of the person with the ablative of what they are brought down from.[^voropeti] Neither the object pattern nor the sense could be predicted from *orohati*; both belong to the causative as a separate word.

[^voropeti]: DPD, s.v. *voropeti*: "causes to ascend down", *vi* + *ava* + *rope* + *ti*, from √*ruh*. The idiom *jīvitā voropeti* is the canon's standard expression for killing, and it is best learnt as an idiom, not derived afresh each time.

Many causatives have stopped meaning 'cause to'. This is the most important practical fact about the category, because to parse every causative as a causation is to mistranslate steadily. *Deseti*, from *dis-* 'point out', means 'teaches'. *Sāveti*, from *su-* 'hear', means 'announces'. *Ropeti*, from *ruh-* 'grow', means 'plants'. *Kāreti*, from *kar-* 'do', means 'has made', and of a king it means 'rules':

- *Sakko, bhikkhave, devānamindo devānaṁ tāvatiṁsānaṁ issariyādhipaccaṁ rajjaṁ kāreti, tasmā devānamindoti vuccati.* (SN 11.12 — S i 229)
- *sakko* Sakka (m. nom. sg.) · *devānam indo* lord of gods (m. nom. sg.) · *devānaṁ tāvatiṁsānaṁ* of the gods of the Thirty-Three (m. gen. pl.) · *issariyādhipaccaṁ* lordship and sovereignty (n. acc. sg.) · *rajjaṁ* kingship (n. acc. sg.) · *kāreti* exercises (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *tasmā* therefore · *devānam indo* 'lord of gods' · *ti* (quotation) · *vuccati* he is called (3 sg. pres. pass.)
- "Monks, Sakka lord of gods **exercises** lordship, sovereignty and kingship over the gods of the Thirty-Three; therefore he is called lord of gods."

That sentence has a causative and a passive in eleven words, a fair measure of how ordinary both are.

One causative deserves separate mention because it stands at the head of a doctrinal formula a reader will meet dozens of times. *Janeti*, the causative of *jan-* 'be born', is what a monk does to his intention in right effort:

- *Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu anuppannānaṁ pāpakānaṁ akusalānaṁ dhammānaṁ anuppādāya chandaṁ janeti vāyamati vīriyaṁ ārabhati …* (SN 45.8 — S v 9)
- *idha* here · *bhikkhave* monks (m. voc. pl.) · *bhikkhu* a monk (m. nom. sg.) · *anuppannānaṁ* unarisen (m. gen. pl.) · *pāpakānaṁ* bad (m. gen. pl.) · *akusalānaṁ* unwholesome (m. gen. pl.) · *dhammānaṁ* of states (m. gen. pl.) · *anuppādāya* for the non-arising (m. dat. sg.) · *chandaṁ* desire (m. acc. sg.) · *janeti* generates (3 sg. pres. caus.) · *vāyamati* strives (3 sg. pres.) · *vīriyaṁ* energy (n. acc. sg.) · *ārabhati* arouses (3 sg. pres.)
- "Here, monks, a monk **generates** desire for the non-arising of unarisen bad, unwholesome states, strives and arouses energy."

Literally *janeti* is 'causes to be born'; in use it means 'generates'. The literal sense still shows through, since one does not simply have the intention, one brings it into being. But the word no longer says so.

### The denominative

A **denominative** is a verb built from a noun instead of a root. English does it constantly and without ceremony: to *hammer*, to *shelter*, to *father*. Pāli does it with a suffix (*-ya-*, *-āya-*, *-īya-* or the same *-e-* the causative uses), and the tradition's name for the result is exact: *dhāturūpakasadda*, 'a word used as though it were a root'.[^denominative-term]

[^denominative-term]: Warder p. 316 and Thānuttamo p. 101, both giving the Kaccāyana term. The literal rendering is kept because it describes the formation: it takes something that is not a root and puts it in the position a root occupies, and the ordinary verbal machinery then works on it unchanged.

The senses are four, and they overlap: to act as, or be like, the noun; to want the noun; to make something into the noun; to treat something as the noun.[^denominative-senses] So *namas-* 'homage' gives *namassati* 'pays homage';[^namassati] *hiri* 'shame' gives *harāyati* 'is ashamed'; *mettā* 'loving-kindness' gives *mettāyati* 'has loving-kindness towards'; *dhūma* 'smoke' gives *dhūmāyati* 'smokes'; *mama* 'mine', the genitive of the first person pronoun, gives *mamāyati* 'treats as mine, cherishes', as neat a piece of doctrinal vocabulary as the language contains.

[^namassati]: Perniola §93a, p. 106, and Oberlies §51b1, p. 251, both derive it from the noun, and the Sanskrit *namasyati* is a denominative of the same shape. DPD gives it a root √*namass* of its own and no denominative label. That is the lexicographer's decision recorded for *sikkhati* below, made for the same reason: a denominative old enough to have been a plain verb throughout the canon's history has, from a dictionary's point of view, become one.

[^denominative-senses]: Thānuttamo p. 102, following Duroiselle (§499, p. 213 in the 1921 edition) and Oberlies (2019) p. 504. Perniola §93, pp. 106–108, organises the same material by suffix and does not give the senses at all. A chapter's structure should not be borrowed from a source, and his shows why: it answers "how is this formed", and a reader needs "what does this mean" first.

The cleanest of those in the canon is *mettāyati*, and it is clean in two senses. DPD files it as a denominative without hesitation, *mettāya* + *ti*, from *mettā*, and the verse it stands in is about nothing else.[^mettayati]

- Ekampi ce pāṇamaduṭṭhacitto, **Mettāyati** kusalo tena hoti; (Iti 27 — It p. 21)
- *ekaṁ* one (m. acc. sg.) · *pi* even · *ce* if · *pāṇamaduṭṭhacitto* < *pāṇaṁ* a living being (m. acc. sg.) + *aduṭṭhacitto* with an uncorrupted mind (m. nom. sg.) · *mettāyati* has goodwill towards (3 sg. pres. denom.) · *kusalo* skilful, good (m. nom. sg.) · *tena* by that (n. inst. sg.) · *hoti* becomes (3 sg. pres.)
- "If, with an uncorrupted mind, one **has goodwill towards** even a single living being, one becomes good by that."

*Mettā* is a noun, and there is no verb 'to love' standing behind it that *mettāyati* could be built from. The verb is the noun, put where a root goes and conjugated. The verb names *mettā* as something done, not something had or felt, and that grammatical fact has a good deal of doctrine folded into it.

[^mettayati]: DPD, s.v. *mettāyati*: "pr, deno of *mettā*", root √*mitt*, construction *mettāya* + *ti*, Sanskrit *maitrāyati*. The finite verb is rare: two occurrences in the root texts, and they are the same verse, at Iti 27 and at AN 8.1. The two witnesses differ in the following word, the Itivuttaka printing *kusalo* and the Aṅguttara *kusalī*, and DPD's own citation of the verse follows the Aṅguttara. The stem is commoner than the finite verb, as it usually is with these: *mettāyanā* 'the having of goodwill' has sixteen root-text occurrences against the verb's two.

*Harāyati* is the same formation on the same kind of base, an abstract noun of feeling made into a verb, and it stands in a formula a reader meets constantly:

- *assutavā kho puthujjano attanā jarādhammo samāno jaraṁ anatīto paraṁ jiṇṇaṁ disvā aṭṭīyati harāyati jigucchati …* (AN 3.39 — A i 145)
- *assutavā* unlearned (m. nom. sg.) · *kho* indeed · *puthujjano* ordinary person (m. nom. sg.) · *attanā* himself (m. inst. sg.) · *jarādhammo* subject to old age (m. nom. sg.) · *samāno* being (m. nom. sg.) · *jaraṁ* old age (f. acc. sg.) · *anatīto* not gone beyond (m. nom. sg.) · *paraṁ* another (m. acc. sg.) · *jiṇṇaṁ* aged (m. acc. sg.) · *disvā* having seen (absol.) · *aṭṭīyati* is troubled (3 sg. pres.) · *harāyati* is ashamed (3 sg. pres. denom.) · *jigucchati* is disgusted (3 sg. pres. desid.)
- "An unlearned ordinary person, though himself subject to old age and not gone beyond it, on seeing another who is aged **is troubled, ashamed and disgusted**."

*Harāyati* is built on *hiri* 'moral shame', one of the two states the canon calls the guardians of the world.[^harayati] The line describes the wrong use of it: shame turned outward at someone else's ageing, when it belongs inward, at one's own conduct. The verb's derivation from *hiri* sharpens the observation.

[^harayati]: DPD, s.v. *harāyati*: "pr, deno of *hiri*", root √*har*, "is ashamed (of); is humiliated (by); is worried (by)". Perniola §93d, p. 107, files it with *hiriyati* and *hirīyati* as a special case of the *-īya-* denominative, the three forms differing only in the treatment of the base's vowel.

> **Variant spellings: *aṭṭīyati* and *attīyati*.** The stock phrase for revulsion is *aṭṭīyati harāyati jigucchati*, and its first word is printed two ways. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's running text has *aṭṭīyati*, and so, in the main, do the Buddha Jayanti and the PTS editions; Perniola §93c and Oberlies §51c both print *attīyati*, which as a free-standing word occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all. This is the ordinary *ṭ*/*t* alternation of Chapter 1, not a disagreement about the word, and both spellings answer to the same entry in the dictionaries.

Siṅgāla, worshipping the six directions because his father told him to, supplies the commonest one:

- *Tena kho pana samayena siṅgālako gahapatiputto kālasseva uṭṭhāya rājagahā nikkhamitvā allavattho allakeso pañjaliko puthudisā namassati …* (DN 31 — D iii 180)
- *tena samayena* at that time · *kho pana* now · *siṅgālako* Siṅgālaka (m. nom. sg.) · *gahapatiputto* the householder's son (m. nom. sg.) · *kālasseva* early · *uṭṭhāya* having risen (absol.) · *rājagahā* from Rājagaha (n. abl. sg.) · *nikkhamitvā* having gone out (absol.) · *allavattho* with wet clothes (m. nom. sg.) · *allakeso* with wet hair (m. nom. sg.) · *pañjaliko* with palms joined (m. nom. sg.) · *puthudisā* the various directions (f. acc. pl.) · *namassati* pays homage to (3 sg. pres. denom.)
- "Now at that time Siṅgālaka the householder's son, having risen early and gone out from Rājagaha with wet clothes and wet hair and palms joined, **was paying homage to** the various directions."

*Namassati* takes an accusative object, and that is the general pattern: a denominative behaves as an ordinary verb in every respect once it exists. A second example, from the list of what a layman does with his wealth, shows the *-e-* type:

- *te tena attānaṁ sukhenti pīṇenti, mātāpitaro sukhenti pīṇenti …* (DN 2 — D i 51)
- *te* they (m. nom. pl.) · *tena* by that (n. inst. sg.) · *attānaṁ* themselves (m. acc. sg.) · *sukhenti* make happy (3 pl. pres.) · *pīṇenti* satisfy (3 pl. pres.) · *mātāpitaro* mother and father (m. acc. pl.) · *sukhenti* make happy · *pīṇenti* satisfy
- "With that they **make** themselves **happy** and satisfied, and **make** their mother and father **happy** and satisfied."

Whether *sukheti* is a denominative or a causative is a question the grammars answer differently, and the disagreement has something to teach. Perniola files it under the denominatives, from the noun *sukha* 'happiness'; DPD calls it a causative of a verb *sukhati*, from a root √*sukh*.[^sukheti-classification] Both accounts produce the same stem *sukhe-* and the same meaning, 'makes happy'. The formal marker is identical, and the two analyses differ only over whether there is a root behind the noun. Nothing a reader does with the sentence depends on the answer, and it is useful to learn early that some of the tidiness in a grammar's classification belongs to the grammarian and not to the language.

The other overlap this chapter opened with resolves the same way, and it needs saying, because this one will confuse a reader mid-sentence. The passive and the denominative both build stems in *-ya-*, and no formal test separates them: *haññati* 'is killed' and *mettāyati* 'has goodwill towards' are the same suffix on different kinds of base. They differ in what the suffix was added to. A passive is built on a root and means undergoing; a denominative is built on a noun and means acting as, wanting, making into or treating as. So ask of an unfamiliar *-ya-* stem what lies underneath it; if a noun lies underneath, the answer follows. The third conjugation's class sign is the same morph again, so *nirujjhati* 'ceases' is neither.

[^sukheti-classification]: Perniola §93f, pp. 107–108, listing *sukheti* among the denominatives "usually classified as verbs of the tenth class", against DPD s.v. *sukheti*: "pr, caus of *sukhati*", root √*sukh*, literally "causes to please". His own data cuts against his classification: he prints *sukhāyati* 'he is pleased' as intransitive beside *sukheti* 'he makes happy' as transitive, and an intransitive paired with a transitive of the same base is the causative relation exactly. Warder p. 316 gives *sukheti* as his first denominative example. The book follows the majority and treats it as a denominative; the point holds either way.

### The two fossils

The desiderative and the intensive cannot be applied as rules. They are word lists, and short ones, so this section says what has survived and stops.

**The desiderative** was formed by reduplicating the root and adding *-sa-*, and it meant 'wishes to do'. These survive in the canon:

| Form | Built on | Literally | What it means in the canon |
|---|---|---|---|
| *sikkhati* | *sak-* 'be able' | desires to be able | trains, practises |
| *jigucchati* | *gup-* | desires to avoid | is disgusted by |
| *titikkhati* | *tij-* | desires to endure | endures, is patient |
| *vīmaṁsati* | *maññati* 'thinks' | desires to know | investigates, tests |
| *sussūsati* | *suṇāti* 'hears' | desires to listen | wants to hear, is attentive |

Only the last of those still means what a desiderative is supposed to mean.[^desid-analysis] *Sikkhati* means 'trains', not 'wishes to be able'; *jigucchati* 'is disgusted', not 'wishes to keep clear of'; *titikkhati* 'endures', not 'wishes to endure'. The formation has been absorbed into the vocabulary, as happens to a category that stops being productive.

[^desid-analysis]: The literal column is DPD's, which marks all of these as desideratives except the first: *jigucchati* "desid of √gup", *titikkhati* "desid of √tij", *vīmaṁsati* "desid of *maññati*", *sussūsati* "desid of *suṇāti*". For *sikkhati* it gives a root √*sikkh* of its own and no desiderative analysis, and Warder agrees with it: *sikkhati* is absent from his desiderative conjugation at pp. 352–353, and his vocabulary enters *sikkh* at p. 413 as a first-conjugation root, "train, study, learn", with none of the "desid." he puts on *(s)su* and *har* a page later. Perniola §91, p. 105, deriving it *si-sk-sa-ti* from *sak-*, stands alone of the three. The disagreement is over how far back a dictionary should look, and the form itself is not in doubt: a word that has been an independent verb since before the canon may reasonably be given its own root by a lexicographer and still be a desiderative to a historical grammarian. The chapter follows the historical analysis because the point being made here is historical, and it is Perniola's alone among the sources consulted.

- *Yāni ca kho tāni sikkhāpadāni ādibrahmacariyakāni brahmacariyasāruppāni tattha dhuvasīlo ca hoti ṭhitasīlo ca, samādāya sikkhati sikkhāpadesu.* (AN 3.88 — A i 234)
- *yāni ca kho tāni* whichever · *sikkhāpadāni* training rules (n. nom. pl.) · *ādibrahmacariyakāni* fundamental to the spiritual life (n. nom. pl.) · *brahmacariyasāruppāni* befitting the spiritual life (n. nom. pl.) · *tattha* therein · *dhuvasīlo* of constant virtue (m. nom. sg.) · *ca hoti* he is · *ṭhitasīlo* of steady virtue (m. nom. sg.) · *ca* and · *samādāya* having undertaken (absol.) · *sikkhati* he trains (3 sg. pres. desid.) · *sikkhāpadesu* in the training rules (n. loc. pl.)
- "As for the training rules that are fundamental to the spiritual life and befit it, he is of constant and steady virtue in them; having undertaken them, **he trains** in the training rules."

The verse traditions keep the older sense a little longer:

- *Akkosaṁ vadhabandhañca, aduṭṭho yo titikkhati.* (MN 98 — M ii 196)
- *akkosaṁ* abuse (m. acc. sg.) · *vadhabandhaṁ* execution and imprisonment (n. acc. sg.) · *ca* and · *aduṭṭho* without ill will (m. nom. sg.) · *yo* who (m. nom. sg.) · *titikkhati* endures (3 sg. pres. desid.)
- "Who, without ill will, **endures** abuse, execution and imprisonment."

The desideratives the grammars print are largely absent from the canon. Every grammar consulted for this chapter gives *bubhukkhati* 'wishes to eat', *jighacchati* 'wishes to eat', *pipāsati* 'wishes to drink' and *cikicchati* 'cures' as its standard examples. Not one of the four occurs anywhere in the four nikāyas or the Vinaya; three of them occur nowhere outside the grammarians' own texts.[^desiderative-unattested] The roots are alive in the canon, but as nouns and participles (*jighacchā* 'hunger' twenty-one times in the root texts, *vīmaṁsā* 'investigation' fifty-four), and a reader will meet them in those.

[^desiderative-unattested]: Counted against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. *Bubhukkhati*: nothing in the root texts, nothing in the commentaries, fourteen occurrences in the grammatical and chronicle texts. *Jighacchati*: nine, all in the same works. *Cikicchati*: seven, likewise, with *tikicchati* managing one commentarial occurrence. *Pipāsati*: one commentarial occurrence and five in the grammarians. These are correctly derived forms which nobody wrote: Kaccāyana's demonstrations of a rule, reprinted by every grammar since. *Vīmaṁsā* is one of the four bases of spiritual power, so a reader has met the desiderative's nominal offspring long before meeting the verb.

**The intensive** reduplicated the root and meant 'does repeatedly' or 'does intensely'. Even less of it survives, and almost all of that is one word, which every meditator knows:

- *Tena kho pana samayena bhagavā rattiyā paccūsasamayaṁ paccuṭṭhāya abbhokāse caṅkamati.* (SN 10.8 — S i 212)
- *tena samayena* at that time · *kho pana* now · *bhagavā* the Blessed One (m. nom. sg.) · *rattiyā* of the night (f. gen. sg.) · *paccūsasamayaṁ* at the time before dawn (m. acc. sg.) · *paccuṭṭhāya* having risen (absol.) · *abbhokāse* in the open air (m. loc. sg.) · *caṅkamati* walks up and down (3 sg. pres. intens.)
- "Now at that time the Blessed One, having risen at the time before dawn, **was walking up and down** in the open air."

*Caṅkamati* is *kamati* 'walks' with its root reduplicated, and the intensive sense is still alive in it. It means walking back and forth, which is what walking meditation is.[^cankamati] Beside it the canon has *jagghati* 'laughs' once. The remaining intensives the grammars list (*cañcalati* 'moves to and fro', *lālappati* 'talks much', *jāgarati* 'is awake', *jaṅgamati* 'goes about', *daddaḷhati* 'blazes') are not canonical at all as finite verbs. The canon has, again, only the nouns and adjectives, *jāgaro* 'wakeful', *lālappaṁ* 'lamentation'.[^intensive-unattested]

[^cankamati]: DPD, s.v. *caṅkamati*: "pr, intens of *kamati*", root √*kam*, "walks up and down; walks in meditation". Perniola §92, p. 106, derives it *caṅ-kam-a-ti* from *kram-*, and the *caṅkama* 'walking path' of every monastery is the same word as a noun.

[^intensive-unattested]: Root-text counts, in order: *cañcalati* none (eight in the grammarians' texts), *lālappati* none anywhere, *jāgarati* none in the root texts and two in the commentaries, *jaṅgamati* none (two in the grammarians'), *daddaḷhati* none anywhere. Against that, *jāgaro* has twenty-seven root-text occurrences and *lālappaṁ* one. Oberlies §44, p. 200, lists *lālappati* and *jaṅgamati* among the surviving intensives; on the evidence of this edition, the verbs are gone and what was made from them survives.

### Recognising a derived stem

The five formations of this chapter reduce, for a reader with a text in front of them, to four questions asked in order.

Is there a doubled consonant where the root has one, or a long *-ī-* before *-ya-*, or *-iya-* after the present stem? Then a **passive** is possible, but only possible, since the doubling has other causes and misleads more often than not. Look for the simple-consonant active beside it, and check that the sense is one of undergoing; a small class of *-iya-* stems is active.

Is the root vowel strengthened before *-e-* or *-aya-*, or is there an *-āpe-*? Then a **causative**. Do not assume it still means 'cause to', because the commonest ones do not.

Is the base a noun, not a root? Then a **denominative**, and its sense will be one of acting as, wanting, making into, or treating as.

Is the first syllable a reduplication of the second? Then a **desiderative** or an **intensive**, and the word should be looked up, not derived, because there are perhaps a dozen of them and each has gone its own way.

What none of these formations changes is the ending. *Vuccati*, *bhāveti*, *namassati*, *caṅkamati*: four different stems, one *-ti*, and Chapter 7's table still governs all of them.
