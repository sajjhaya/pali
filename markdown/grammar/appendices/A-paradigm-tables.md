## Appendix A: Paradigm Tables

The complete declensions and conjugations, gathered here for reference. Chapters carry only the working subset a reader needs at that point; the full paradigm, in every attested variant, belongs here.

**Source.** Transcribed from Bhikkhu Ñāṇatusita's inflection tables (© 2005, Forest Hermitage, Kandy, Sri Lanka; freely distributable non-commercially), themselves a synthesis compiled against Ānanda Maitreyya, Buddhadatta, Duroiselle, Fahs, Geiger, Perniola, Warder and Wijesekera. This appendix reproduces his tables, not a union with any other grammar: where his tables and Oberlies (the first authority for morphology) diverge, Ñāṇatusita's own content stands, checked but not amended. The one exception is three places where his own tables contradict themselves — a mislabelled row, a doubled letter, and a form set in the wrong column, each plainly typographical rather than something he intended — corrected with a note where they occur below.

**Notation.** Ordinary endings are given plain; a rare, dialectal, poetic or post-canonical variant is marked with parentheses, following Ñāṇatusita's own italic marking and Oberlies's own bracket convention for the same forms. Two other uses of parentheses are Ñāṇatusita's own and are reproduced as printed rather than read as rarity markings: his explanatory shorthand — `(= Dat.)`, `(= nt.)` — and his bracketing of an optional letter *within* a form, as in `(n)ehi`, `(i)ssāmi`, `(a)nti`, `(ṭ)ṭha`. The two are told apart by position: a rarity marking follows the form it qualifies and is separated from it by a space, an optional letter sits flush inside the form.

**On the verb tables' Middle Voice column specifically:** Ñāṇatusita sets his entire Middle Voice block in italics, evidently to mark the voice as a whole rather than to flag individual rare forms within it — unlike the noun and pronoun tables, where italic marks a rare variant *of the form given beside it*. Wrapping every middle-voice ending in parentheses would misread that convention, so the Middle column below is set plain, in its own column, exactly as the Active column is. Parentheses are still used *within* either column wherever Ñāṇatusita's own italic marks one alternative as rarer than its neighbour (for instance the a-Aorist's rare active alternates `amha`, `atha`).

[^amu-unattested]: Oberlies rejects both of these outright, and the distinction has to be kept because this appendix cannot otherwise draw it. Of *amuyo*: "GEIGER (§ 109) gives also *amuyo*. But this is only a grammarian's form" (§42(6), p. 190 n.2). Of *amūsānaṁ*: "The gen. *amūsānaṁ* which GEIGER cites is not attested" (n.3). Neither appears in his own paradigm. **Rare and unattested are different claims**: a rare form is Pāli that a reader may meet, while these are forms the indigenous grammatical tradition constructed by analogy and no text prints. The cells are left exactly as Ñāṇatusita set them, since this appendix is his tables verified rather than a new paradigm — but a reader who takes a parenthesised form here as something to use should know that for these two, on the best current authority, there is nothing to meet.

    **Geiger says less than Oberlies's quotation of him makes it sound, and the difference is typographic.** Read off the page (§109, p. 103), his paradigm gives the feminine plural nominative and accusative as *amū, (amuyo)* and the genitive plural as *amūsaṁ, (-sānaṁ)* — both disputed forms **in brackets**, and he declares at §105 note 1 what the brackets mean: "the rarer or more archaic forms are again given in brackets." So he is not asserting them as ordinary Pāli; he is flagging them, in his own notation, much as this appendix flags them in parentheses. On the substance he and Oberlies still differ — rare is not unattested, and Oberlies has looked — but they differ by one step, not two.

    Both forms have travelled further than either. Perniola prints *amuyo* in his *asu* plural and lists *amūsānaṁ* as an alternative genitive plural for both masculine and feminine (*Pali Grammar* §41, p. 55), **with no mark of rarity at all**. That is the mechanism this note exists to name: Geiger brackets a form, the next grammar drops the bracket, and a grammarian's construction has become a standard paradigm in two moves.

### A.4 Derivational suffixes and prefixes

Ñāṇatusita's tables carry this apparatus alongside the conjugation proper. It is not a paradigm in the same sense as the tables above — not a closed set of inflections for one form, but an inventory of what Pāli word-formation draws on.

**A note on notation in this section.** Parentheses here are part of the printed forms themselves — `(i)tvā`, `(ṭ)ṭha`, `(an)āya` — marking an optional segment, not rarity. So rarity cannot also be shown with parentheses without ambiguity. Rare and post-canonical suffixes therefore get their own column, on the same reasoning as the Middle Voice column above: where a distinction can't share a glyph, it gets its own space. Numbered groups (1., 2., 3.) are Ñāṇatusita's own, marking alternative formations of the same category.
