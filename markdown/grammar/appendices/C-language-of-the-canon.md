## Appendix C: The Language of the Canon

The introduction states a conclusion: that Pāli is the closest surviving witness to the language the Buddha spoke, and is not that language itself. This appendix is the argument behind it.

It sits here, not there, for two reasons. If you have come to learn declensions you should not have to work through a philological dispute to reach Chapter 1; and the dispute deserves having properly or not at all, which takes more room than an introduction can spare. Nothing in the grammar depends on how it comes out. What does depend on it is the reader's ability to tell which of this book's statements are facts about the language and which are inferences about its history, and that distinction deserves the pages.

### The name is not the name of a language

Begin with the word itself, which in the texts does not mean a language at all. *Pāḷi* means the text, the canonical wording as against the commentary that explains it, and the dictionary's first sense is "canonical text; text of the suttas".[^pali-gloss] A commentator writing *pārājikapāḷiyaṁ* means "in the text of the Pārājikas", not "in the Pārājikas' language".

The distribution says the same thing more sharply, because in the root texts the word occurs about forty times all told and nearly every one of them is in the Visuddhimagga, which is Buddhaghosa in the fifth century and not the canon at all; the few that are not are the Paṭṭhāna's own directions to its reciter, telling him which questions are *pāḷiyaṁ*, "in the text", and which he must work out for himself. In the commentaries, where there is a commentary to be distinguished from a canon, the count runs into the hundreds. It is a word the tradition needed only once it had two things to tell apart.

The name of the language, then, is a shortening of *pāḷibhāsā*, "the language of the text".[^palibhasa] That longer phrase is printed nowhere in the canon and nowhere in the aṭṭhakathā; the eleven places the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana does print it are ṭīkā or later throughout, and one of them, a Burmese sub-commentary of the last two centuries, lists it among the names of the Magadha language.[^palibhasa-count] The name is late. Using it for a particular language appears to be no older than the eighteenth century.[^pali-eighteenth]

What the tradition calls the language instead is *māgadhabhāsā*, "the Magadha language", or *māgadhiko vohāro*, "the Magadhan idiom". Be exact here, because the modern literature routinely reports the tradition as saying *Māgadhī*, and it does not. The word *Māgadhī* occurs nowhere in the canon, nowhere in the aṭṭhakathā and nowhere in the ṭīkā; it appears ten times in the later literature and not once before it. Four of those ten are a flower and a pepper, not the language at all. Norman noticed the same absence from the other end, observing that nowhere does Buddhaghosa state that the language of the canon in his day was Māgadhī.[^no-magadhi] The distinction matters. *Māgadhī* is the name of one eastern Prakrit, and *māgadhabhāsā* need not mean it.

The canon does have a word for the language, and it is neither of those. A monk renouncing the training has to be understood when he does it, and the Vinaya rules on what happens when he is not:

- **Ariyakena** milakkhassa santike sikkhaṁ paccakkhāti, so ca na paṭivijānāti, apaccakkhātā hoti sikkhā. (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 27)
- *ariyakena* in Aryan (inst.) · *milakkhassa* of a barbarian, a non-Aryan speaker (gen.) · *santike* in the presence of · *sikkhaṁ* the training · *paccakkhāti* he renounces, disavows · *so* he · *ca* and · *na* not · *paṭivijānāti* understands, recognises · *apaccakkhātā* not renounced · *hoti* is · *sikkhā* the training
- He renounces the training **in Aryan** in the presence of a non-Aryan speaker, and that one does not understand: the training is not renounced.

*Ariyaka* is simply "the Indo-Aryan language", and *milakkha* (Sanskrit *mleccha*) is a speaker of something else.[^ariyaka-gloss] The commentary spells the contrast out: *ariyaka* is the Aryan idiom, the Magadha language; *milakkhaka* is any non-Aryan one, Andhan or Tamil or the like.[^ariyaka-comm] So for the tradition the opposite of *māgadhabhāsā* is Dravidian, not a rival Indo-Aryan dialect. And there the difficulty starts.

### What the tradition says

The tradition's claim is bold, and it is clear. Buddhaghosa states it in the Visuddhimagga, discussing the analytical knowledge of language: the speech in question is *sabhāvanirutti*, expression that matches the nature of things, and it is *māgadhikāya sabbasattānaṁ mūlabhāsāya*, the Magadhan tongue, the root language of all beings.[^mulabhasa] On this account every other language is a departure from the Magadha language.

The Vibhaṅga commentary works the claim out at length, and it is given whole here, because it is the strongest form the traditional position ever takes. A child of a Tamil mother and an Andhaka father will speak whichever of them it hears first; hearing neither, it will speak *māgadhabhāsā*. So will a child born in a forest with no village and nobody in it to hear, "raising up speech by its own nature". In every realm (hell, the animal realm, the ghost realm, the human world, the world of the gods) the Magadha language prevails. The other eighteen languages change; this one alone, "reckoned the true speech of Brahmā and the speech of the noble ones", does not change. And the Fully Awakened One, setting the Buddha-word of the three piṭakas into a canonical text, set it up in the Magadha language and no other.[^vibhanga-mulabhasa]

The reason the commentary gives is, interestingly, a practical one: the meaning comes out more easily. For one who has reached the analytical knowledges, the Buddha-word set down in that language needs only to arrive at the ear, and the moment it strikes the ear the sense stands up in a hundred ways and a thousand; set down in some other language it must be *pothetvā pothetvā uggahetabbaṁ*, beaten out and beaten out before it can be learned. The claim underneath the cosmology, then, is a claim about wording and meaning: that the one carries the other. The Buddha himself says as much about badly-transmitted phrasing at AN 4.160 — A ii 147.

The same claim is attached to a particular Vinaya episode, and the argument has been fought over that episode ever since. Two monks, brothers, brahmins by birth and, as the text is careful to say, fine speakers, come to the Buddha with a proposal:

- Te sakāya niruttiyā buddhavacanaṁ dūsenti. Handa mayaṁ, bhante, buddhavacanaṁ **chandaso** āropemā ti. (Kd 15 — Vin ii 139)
- *te* they · *sakāya* (in) one's own (fem. instr. sg., agreeing with *niruttiyā*) · *niruttiyā* (in) expression · *buddhavacanaṁ* the word of the Buddha · *dūsenti* they corrupt · *handa* come now · *mayaṁ* we · *bhante* venerable sir · *chandaso* see below · *āropemā* let us put up, let us set
- They corrupt the word of the Buddha by their own manner of speaking. Come, venerable sir, let us put the word of the Buddha into **chandas**.

The Buddha refuses, and rules:

- Na, bhikkhave, buddhavacanaṁ chandaso āropetabbaṁ. Yo āropeyya, āpatti dukkaṭassa. Anujānāmi, bhikkhave, **sakāya niruttiyā** buddhavacanaṁ pariyāpuṇitun ti. (Kd 15 — Vin ii 139)
- *na* not · *āropetabbaṁ* to be put (gerundive) · *yo* whoever · *āropeyya* should put (opt.) · *āpatti* offence · *dukkaṭassa* of wrong-doing · *anujānāmi* I allow · *pariyāpuṇituṁ* to learn thoroughly, to master
- The word of the Buddha is not to be put into *chandas*, monks. Whoever does so commits an offence of wrong-doing. I allow you, monks, to learn the word of the Buddha **in its own manner of speaking**.

Buddhaghosa glosses both halves of the exchange. *Chandaso āropema*, he says, means "let us put it into a mode of recitation in the Sanskrit language, as one does the Veda"; and as for *sakāya niruttiyā*, "here 'one's own expression' means the Magadhan idiom of the kind spoken by the Fully Awakened One."[^samanta] A ṭīkā spells out the grammar he leaves implicit: *sakā* is "that which is his — the meaning is, the Blessed One's speech."[^vinayalankara] On this reading the ruling is not about translation at all. It forbids Sanskritising the canon and requires it to be learned in the Buddha's own Magadhan.

Another ṭīkā glosses the complaint, and shows what the tradition took the two brahmins to be objecting to: because the Magadhan language is easy for anyone to speak, even the lowborn take it up, and learning it they corrupt it.[^vimativinodani] On that account the canon is being recited badly by people the commentator thinks cannot speak properly, and is not being rendered into many dialects at all. The gloss is a single sentence and does not say what the spoiling consists of (bad pronunciation, bad grammar and the wrong register are all available), so it will not bear being pressed further than this. Even unpressed, it is a complaint about how the words are being said, which is a different thing from a complaint about translation.

### What the language itself says

Set the commentary aside and read the language. Three features define Māgadhī as the Prakrit grammarians and the eastern inscriptions describe it: *l* for *r*, palatal *ś* for every sibilant, and *-e* as the nominative singular ending.[^magadhi-features] Pāli does none of them as a rule: it keeps its *r*, so that the *r* of *rāja* and *rūpa* stays an *r*; it has no *ś* at all but only *s*; and its nominative singular is *-o*. Burnouf and Lassen raised that objection in 1826, and Norman, setting the three features out again, states the conclusion flatly. Whatever Pāli is, it is not Māgadhī.

The evidence underneath that should be seen, not taken on trust, because it is dated and localised, and has nothing to do with Buddhism. Aśoka's edicts survive in versions cut at both ends of his empire, and the same imperial text comes out differently at each end. At Girnar in the west the verb is *karoti* and the king is *rāja*; at Kālsī in the east they are *kaleti* and *lāja*. Girnar keeps the three nasals apart, has *-cch-* where Kālsī has *-kkh-*, assimilates its consonant clusters where Kālsī splits them with a helping vowel, and makes its nominative singular *-o* and *-aṁ* where Kālsī makes it *-e*.[^asoka] Set Pāli beside those two columns and it goes with Girnar nearly throughout. That is a fact established from stone, and so the traditional account cannot stand in the form the commentaries give it.

What complicates the picture is that the eastern forms have not vanished; they are scattered through the texts in ones and twos, and one of them is the word for a monk. *Bhikkhu* carries eastern *kkh* where the western pattern would give *cch*.[^norman-eastern] The vocative is stranger still: the Buddha addresses the monks as *bhikkhave*, with the eastern *-e*, 26,718 times in the root texts, and as *bhikkhavo*, with the ending the rest of Pāli morphology would predict, 183 times. The prefix *pari-* appears as *pali-* in a small closed group of words: *palipatha*, "an obstruction", which the dictionary derives from *pari* + √*path*, and *palibodha*, "an impediment", from *pari* + √*rudh*; *paribodha* occurs nowhere in the root texts. *Nibbāna* has a dental *n* where Sanskrit *nirvāṇa* has a retroflex. And the ordinary way one monk addresses another, *āvuso*, stands where the vocative of *āyasmant-* should have given *āyasmanto*, with a *v* in place of an expected *y*.[^avuso]

That ratio is usually quoted as it stands, and as it stands it is misleading. Sort the same occurrences by whether they sit in prose or in verse and the proportion turns over. In the root texts, taking the edition's own marking of which paragraphs are verse, *bhikkhavo* occurs fifty times in verse and *bhikkhave* ten. Of those ten, six are a prose formula sitting inside a verse-marked paragraph, one is a variant reading whose running text is *bhikkhavo*, and only two are metrical, both in the Apadāna, which is among the latest strata in the canon. In prose *bhikkhave* outnumbers *bhikkhavo* by more than two hundred to one. In verse it is effectively absent. Karpik has argued from that distribution that *bhikkhavo* is the older form and *bhikkhave* a prosodic shortening of it, which would take the flagship eastern residue off the list altogether.[^bhikkhave-verse] The same distribution is equally consistent with an eastern form frozen into a prose formula and kept out of verse by the metre. It does not decide between them. It does show that the bare ratio proves nothing, and this book will not quote it as though it did.

None of these is an error, and calling them anomalies is a convenience, not a description. Norman's own warning is that Pāli is by definition the language of the *pāḷi*, so every form in the texts, however odd, is Pāli.[^norman-prisoner] He adds a caution that is easy to skip: "western" here is a linguistic description and not a map reference, since eastern forms turn up in the version of the edicts cut at Sopārā, on the west coast.

What the odd forms are is residue. A language recast westward from an eastern original would look like this: predominantly western in its phonology and morphology, with eastern forms surviving where they were too frequent, too formulaic, or too little trouble to change. The recasting has a counter-intuitive signature. The eastern dialect was phonologically further along than the western ones (voiced stops lost between vowels, voiceless ones voiced, gender distinctions going), so carrying the texts west made them look *older*, not newer, and the process overshot in places. Oberlies points to the hyper-forms it left behind, of which *Isipatana* is the standard example.[^oberlies-recast] Lüders gave the layer underneath a name, *Alt-Ardhamāgadhī*; Lévi had already argued in 1912 for *une langue précanonique* beneath the texts we have.[^precanonical] That is the standard reconstruction.

Von Hinüber states the conclusion: it is possible to infer from the peculiarities of the language that it was "created as some kind of *lingua franca* presumably used in a large area at a time considerably later than the Buddha", and that the canon, though "the oldest Buddhist texts surviving, are the result of a lengthy and complicated development".[^vonhinuber] Oberlies reaches the same verdict by the same route.

It is not unanimous, and it never has been. Geiger reviewed the alternatives (Ujjayinī, the Vindhya region, Kaliṅga), found that no consensus had been reached about any of them, and fell back on the old tradition, holding that Pāli should be regarded as a form of Māgadhī, the cultivated speech of the educated classes and not a popular dialect, already in use before the Buddha.[^geiger] He is the author of the grammar this book leans on, and he took the other side. Nor is he the last to: the position has living defenders, and the last section of this appendix names them. That is one reason to state the modern account as an inference, not as a result.

### Two words that will not settle

Return to the Vinaya passage, because the case for the traditional view rests on it and it cannot carry the weight.

*Chandaso* has been read four ways: "into Vedic", "into Sanskrit", "metrically", and "as one pleases". The dictionary itself gives two of them without deciding: an ablative of *chandas*, "metre, prosody", and an indeclinable adverb from *chanda*, "willingly".[^chandaso] Buddhaghosa read it as Sanskrit, and if he was right the passage shows only that the Buddha's words were not already in Sanskrit, which nobody doubted; if the reading is "metrically" or "as one pleases", it says nothing whatever about the language he used.

Two further readings are on offer, and both are deflating in the same way. Norman points out that Buddhaghosa's gloss (put it into a mode of recitation in the Sanskrit language, as one does the Veda) may describe brahmins *glossing* difficult words as they recited, in the way Sanskrit commentaries were written on the Vedas, so that what was being asked for was permission to explain the Buddha's words, not to translate them. Karpik reads the same gloss as a periphrasis for verse: what the two brahmins wanted was to put Buddhist prose into *gāthā* form.[^chandaso-readings] That last reading has something in its favour that the others do not, which is that the Buddha prohibits reciting the Dhamma *āyatakena gītassarena*, "in a drawn-out singing tone", thirty sections earlier in the same chapter of the Vinaya (Kd 15 — Vin ii 108), and gives five drawbacks for it. Five readings of one word, then, and the history of a language is expected to rest on it.

*Sakāya niruttiyā* is harder, and the difficulty is grammatical, not lexical. *Saka* means simply "one's own", from *sa* + *ka*, and Pāli does not mark whose. In the monks' complaint the possessor must be the monks: they corrupt the word of the Buddha *by their own* manner of speaking. In the Buddha's ruling the commentary takes the possessor to be the Buddha: learn it *in its own* manner of speaking. Both look available, in both places.

But there is an argument that they are not, and it deserves stating. Geiger made it in 1922, answering Weller, with an example from modern Europe: if a German says to a Frenchman *speak in your own language, I shall answer in my own language*, "own language" means French in the first clause and German in the second, because the possessor is fixed by the subject. On that rule the two occurrences here *must* differ. The subject of the complaint is the monks, so the idiom is theirs; the subject of the ruling is *I*, the Buddha, or, taking the second clause on its own, *buddhavacanaṁ*, so the idiom is his or its. Brahmāli's translation follows that pattern, rendering "their own expressions" in the complaint and "its own expressions" in the ruling, and there is a rule behind it and not only an ear. Norman at one point took the sharper option and read even the *first* occurrence as "its own", so that the monks are ruining the Buddha-word in the Buddha-word's own idiom.[^sakaya-camps]

Underneath the grammatical argument sits a lexical one that unsettles more. Levman has gone through the canon's uses of *nirutti* and concludes that there it does not mean "dialect" and does not mean "language" at all: it belongs with naming, designation and terminology, and stands as a synonym of *vohāra* — the very word Buddhaghosa reaches for in the gloss. On that reading the Samantapāsādikā is not saying "the Magadhan dialect" but something closer to "the Magadhan way of putting things", and the ruling is about terminology rather than about which tongue to use.[^nirutti-sense] This book glosses *nirutti* as "expression" for that reason, and a reader who renders it "dialect" has taken a side.

There is a second passage in the canon about language, and it is far less contested than the first. In the Araṇavibhaṅga Sutta the Buddha lays down a rule of two clauses:

- **Janapadaniruttiṁ** nābhiniveseyya, **samaññaṁ** nātidhāveyyā ti. (MN 139 — M iii 234)
- *janapadaniruttiṁ* local expression, the idiom of a district (acc.) · *na* not · *abhiniveseyya* should insist on, adhere to (opt.) · *samaññaṁ* common usage, the ordinary way of speaking (acc.) · *na* not · *atidhāveyya* should overshoot, should run past (opt.)
- One should not insist on **local expression**, and should not override **common usage**.

He then illustrates it. In one district a vessel is called *pātī*, in another *patta*, in others *vitta*, *sarāva*, *dhāropa*, *poṇa*, *pisīlava*; and the fault is to seize on whichever one is current where you happen to be and to insist, "only this is true, the rest is nonsense" (MN 139 — M iii 234). The right practice is to use the local word without gripping it, thinking "this is evidently what these venerables mean by it". It is a ruling against making a doctrine out of vocabulary, not about which language to preach in. And the fact that the Buddha could list seven regional words and expect an audience to recognise all seven is itself a small piece of evidence about how far apart the districts' speech was. Karpik makes this point, and it is a fair one.[^mn139]

Because the phrase is open, the passage became famous in the West as the Buddha's licence to translate. Read *sakāya niruttiyā* as "each in his own dialect", and the ruling authorises what the tradition says it forbids. The grammar permits it. The commentary rejects it. Norman's advice, after setting out the difficulties in both words, is that we should not put too much weight on so problematic a passage and should instead work the matter out another way.[^norman-weight] That is sound counsel, and for that reason this appendix has spent longer on the endings of the language than on the story about them.

### Where that leaves a reader

So what should a reader take Pāli to be?

Start by noticing that "closest" is a comparison, and that a comparison needs something to compare against. Pāli is not the only surviving witness to what the early Buddhist texts said. There is Gāndhārī, the language of the north-western kingdom, in which fragments survive dated to the first century BCE, the oldest Buddhist manuscripts in existence. There is Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit and the fully Sanskritised recensions after it. And there are the Chinese and Tibetan translations, made from Indic originals now lost, which carry in another language what those originals said. Against that field, Pāli cannot claim that its manuscripts are old. They are not: the oldest Pāli manuscript is roughly ninth-century, a thousand years younger than the Gāndhārī fragments.[^gandhari] The claim rests on the language and on what it carries: one school's canon, complete, in a Middle Indo-Aryan form that never underwent the wholesale move toward Sanskrit that overtook the other Indic recensions, though it shows traces of the process itself. Age of witness and age of language are two different measurements, and only the second is in Pāli's favour.

Some things are not in doubt. It is a Middle Indo-Aryan language, not Sanskrit and not derived from the Sanskrit we have. The canon it carries is, on von Hinüber's judgement, the oldest body of Buddhist text surviving; and it was nevertheless the mother tongue of no community anyone has been able to locate, in Magadha or anywhere else, so that the Buddha did not speak it in the form in which we have it.

Beyond that, the ladder shortens. It seems well established that the language is predominantly western in its phonology and that its eastern forms are residue from an earlier transmission. It seems reasonable to assume that this earlier transmission was in something close to the language of Magadha, since the events the texts describe took place there. It is hard to know what to make of the further question, what the Buddha actually said, in what dialect, and how far the recasting altered it; any answer to it is a conjecture, some of them good ones.

And the modern position is less settled than a reference book usually lets on. Gombrich has argued that Pāli is a lingua franca the Buddha himself developed, in order to teach consistently across dialects. Levman argues that the language behind the canon is a koine already in use before the Buddha, and that the pre-canonical layer Lévi identified is that koine showing through. Karpik goes furthest, and argues that the traditional account has not been disproved at all: that the Buddha taught in a standard language of which Pāli is the continuation, that the commentaries never say Māgadhī (which, as recorded above, is true), and that translating the teaching was never contemplated in the early texts.[^living-debate] These are not fringe positions.

What they disagree about is chiefly dating. Von Hinüber's *lingua franca* comes later than the Buddha, and Pāli is its product; Gombrich's and Levman's common language is contemporary with him or older, so that he could have used it. Oberlies, whose account this appendix has broadly followed, says something that sits between the two: the proto-canonical language was based on an artistic Middle Indo-Aryan poets' language "in use long before the time of the Buddha". The gap is narrower than the summaries make it, and it is real.

What holds is this: between the Buddha's speech and the Pāli of the editions there stands a period of oral transmission, a translation westward, and an editorial tradition that reached its present shape centuries later. Every one of those is a distance. But no other body of Buddhist text stands closer, and the eastern forms scattered through it are the marks of the older layer showing through, not of a later composition covering it over. Pāli is the closest surviving witness to the language the Buddha spoke. Not the thing itself, and near enough to be listened to.

[^no-magadhi]: Counted across the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. *Māgadhī* occurs **zero** times in the root texts, zero in the aṭṭhakathā and zero in the ṭīkā; the ten occurrences are all in the remaining works, which are later literature and the grammarians'. Read them and the absence is sharper still, because four of the ten are a different word. Two are in the Abhidhānappadīpikā, which is a thesaurus in verse, and in both the neighbours settle it: *yūthikā māgadhī cātha, sattalā navamallikā* is a line of flower names, and *apāmaggo sekhariko, pippalī māgadhī matā* one of plants used as medicine. Its ṭīkā glosses each in the same words — *magadhe bhavā māgadhī*, "the Magadhan one". Of the six that do name a language, five are one verse, *sā māgadhī mūlabhāsā*, "that Māgadhī is the root language", quoted in the Padarūpasiddhi, the Payogasiddhi, a Dīgha ṭīkā, the Vinayālaṅkāra-ṭīkā and the Khuddasikkhā-Mūlasikkhā-ṭīkā; the sixth is a nīti anthology listing six Prakrits. So the tradition's entire use of *Māgadhī* as the name of a language, across ten million words, is one late verse and one list. *Māgadhabhāsā* is likewise absent from the root texts and appears only in the commentaries: eight in the aṭṭhakathā, twelve in the ṭīkā. Of the five canonical occurrences of *māgadhika*, two are the Visuddhimagga passage quoted above and three are ethnic, not linguistic, being Bimbisāra's Magadhan brahmins and householders. Norman's observation is quoted by Karpik, *The Buddha taught in Pali* (2019: 26), from Norman 1980: 63, which I have not seen directly: "Nowhere, to my knowledge, does Buddhaghosa state that the language of the canon in his day was Māgadhī."
[^ariyaka-gloss]: DPD, s.v. *ariyaka* 1 "Indo-Aryan language"; s.v. *milakkha* "barbarian; non-Indo-Aryan speaker", Sanskrit *mleccha*. *Paṭivijānāti* "to recognise", PED s.v., which cites this very passage. The rule stands at ¶54 of the Pārājika in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana. The following clause makes the reverse case: renouncing in a non-Aryan language before an Aryan speaker who does not understand is equally invalid.
[^ariyaka-comm]: Samantapāsādikā, *Sikkhāpaccakkhānavibhaṅgavaṇṇanā* (Sp I 255): *Tattha ariyakaṁ nāma ariyavohāro, māgadhabhāsā. Milakkhakaṁ nāma yo koci anariyako andhadamiḷādi.* The inference drawn here, that the tradition contrasts *māgadhabhāsā* with Dravidian and not with a rival Indo-Aryan dialect, is Karpik's (2019 §3.2), built on von Hinüber's 1977 translation of this passage and the next. The canonical compound *ariyavohāra* is a different word and must not be counted as evidence here: *ariyavohārā* stands eleven times in the root texts and *anariyavohārā* twenty-three, and every one of them belongs to the four ways of speaking: saying one has seen what one has seen, or has not, and the same for the heard, the sensed and the cognised. The Aṅguttara's four Vohāra suttas set the two lists out in full (¶¶250–253), the Vibhaṅga doubles them to eight (¶¶839, 955), and the Parivāra lists both. Nothing to do with language.
[^vibhanga-mulabhasa]: Sammohavinodanī, *Paṭisambhidāvibhaṅgavaṇṇanā* (Vibh-a 387–388; the passage recurs at Paṭis-a I 6): *Ubhinnampi pana kathaṁ assuṇanto māgadhabhāsaṁ bhāsissati. Yopi agāmake mahāraññe nibbatto, tattha añño kathento nāma natthi, sopi attano dhammatāya vacanaṁ samuṭṭhāpento māgadhabhāsameva bhāsissati … sabbattha māgadhabhāsāva ussannā. Tattha sesā oṭṭakirātaandhakayonakadamiḷabhāsādikā aṭṭhārasa bhāsā parivattanti. Ayamevekā yathābhuccabrahmavohāraariyavohārasaṅkhātā māgadhabhāsā na parivattati. Sammāsambuddhopi tepiṭakaṁ buddhavacanaṁ tantiṁ āropento māgadhabhāsāya eva āropesi.* Two details are worth noticing. The verb in that last clause is *āropeti*, the same one the two brahmins use in *chandaso āropema*; and *tanti* here is "canonical text" (DPD, s.v. *tanti* 3, literal "string"), so what the Buddha is said to do is set the teaching into a fixed text, not merely to speak it.
[^asoka]: Oberlies §1, pp. 1–2, setting the two recensions side by side. Girnar in the west: *karoti*, *rāja(n)-*, *likhita-*, *sila-*, the three nasals distinguished, *-cch-* < *-kṣ-*, clusters assimilated (*rañño*), nom. sg. masc. *-o* and ntr. *-aṁ* (*°piyo*, *dānaṁ*, *mūlaṁ*). Kālsī, Dhauli and the pillars in the east: *kaleti*, *lāja(n)-*, the nasals merged, *-kkh-* < *-kṣ-* (*lukkha-*), clusters split by an anaptyctic vowel (*lājine*), nom. sg. masc. and ntr. *-e* (*°piye*, *dāne*, *mūle*), loc. in *-asi* (*vijitasi*).
[^norman-eastern]: Norman, *A Philological Approach to Buddhism*, p. 53, whose list is fuller than the one this section carries: besides the items given here he names the voicing of consonants (*yādeti*), intervocalic *y* for *k* or *t*, *j* where *y* is expected (*jantāghara*), and a group of words in which a cluster containing a nasal has developed unexpectedly (*nt* > *nd*, *nd* > *nn*, *mb* > *mm*).
[^bhikkhave-verse]: The verse/prose count is my own, made over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's root-text files by asking, for each occurrence, whether the paragraph carrying it is marked as verse in the edition's own markup. Totals: *bhikkhavo* 50 in verse and 130 in prose; *bhikkhave* 10 in verse and 26,773 in prose. Of the ten, six are the *adhivacana* prose formula of the Bhayabherava similes sitting inside verse-marked paragraphs, one is a variant reading whose running text is *bhikkhavo*, and two are verse, both in the Apadāna (`s0510m1`, `s0510m2`). Karpik (2019: 38 n. 43) argues the same conclusion from a hand list of verse occurrences and from the observation that the Khuddaka Nikāya has no *bhikkhave* in verse.
[^oberlies-recast]: Oberlies §1, p. 4: the eastern proto-canonical language had lost internal voiced occlusives, voiced the surds, and was losing the gender distinction, so that "the 'texts' were transformed into a more archaic language … as Buddhism spread westward. And that process over-reached itself in not a few instances, i.e. hyper-forms like *Isipatana* were created", from a reconstructed *Ṛṣyavṛjana*.
[^precanonical]: *Alt-Ardhamāgadhī* is Lüders's term, reported by Oberlies §1, p. 4, who describes the language it names as akin to Aśoka's administrative language and based on an artistic Middle Indo-Aryan poets' language "which was in use long before the time of the Buddha". Lévi's *langue précanonique* (1912) is reported by Levman, *Pāli and Buddhism* (2023), ch. 1. I have read neither Lüders nor Lévi directly.
[^chandaso-readings]: Norman, *A Philological Approach to Buddhism*, p. 51: Buddhaghosa "seems to have understood the bhikkhus' suggestions as meaning: 'Let us translate, as we translate the Veda into Sanskrit', presumably referring to a situation where brahmans would be able to explain difficult words in the Vedas, when they recited them … This, then, might perhaps mean that the ex-brahmans were asking permission to gloss, i.e. explain, the Buddha's words as they recited them." [^nirutti-sense]: Levman, *Sakāya niruttiyā revisited*, pp. 39–40, after going through the canonical occurrences: "*nirutti*, in its use in the canon does not mean 'dialect', nor does it mean 'language'." He proposes rendering Buddhaghosa's gloss "the Magadhian name/designation in the manner spoken by the Completely Enlightened One", on the ground that *vohāra* is used in the canon as a synonym of *nirutti* in the sense "name, designation, definition, explanation". Norman had already declined "dialect" for it at 1980: 61.
[^mn139]: The seven words are at MN 139 — M iii 234 and the two wrong and right practices at 12.5–12.11. Karpik (2019 §2.3) reads the list as evidence that the varieties of Indo-Aryan were mutually comprehensible, on the ground that a dispute about which word is correct can only arise between people who understand each other. *Samaññā* here is DPD's sense 2, "common language; normal usage"; *atidhāvati* is "overshoots the mark, goes beyond the limit".
