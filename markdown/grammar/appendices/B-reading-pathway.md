## Appendix B: A Graded Reading Pathway

A reference grammar teaches the system, and only volume of reading builds a reader. This appendix is the exit ramp.

Start it at the end of Part IV, when the participles, absolutives and indeclinables are in place, and run it alongside the remaining chapters, not after them. Everything from Part V onward (compounds, word formation, the syntax chapters, metre) is easier to learn against text you are already struggling with, and much harder to learn in the abstract. Waiting until the grammar is finished is the reliable way to never begin.

### What "graded" means here, and how it was measured

A pathway is graded if each step is mostly built out of what the last step taught. That is a measurable property, and it has not previously been measured for Pāli, so the ordering below is measurement, not taste.

Two numbers decide it. **Coverage** is the share of a text's running words that fall among the commonest words of the four Nikāyas: a text with high coverage is built from vocabulary a reader will already have met many times. **The type/token ratio** is the count of distinct words divided by the count of running words: a low ratio means the text repeats itself, and that turns the canon's formulaic habit into free graded input.

The curve for the four Nikāyas (956,218 running words, 55,602 distinct forms) runs like this:

| Coverage of running words | Commonest forms needed |
|---|---:|
| 25% | 43 |
| 50% | 502 |
| 60% | 1,153 |
| 70% | 2,530 |
| 80% | 5,637 |
| 90% | 13,601 |
| 95% | 23,762 |
| 98% | 36,578 |

A quarter of everything the Buddha is recorded as saying is forty-three word-forms, and half of it is five hundred. That is the number to hold, and it is the reason a beginner's progress feels so steep at first and then so sudden.

Two cautions attach to the other end of that table, and the first matters more than it looks. These are surface forms, not word families. Pāli inflects heavily, writes its compounds solid, and welds its particles onto the word in front of them, so a single known word turns up on the page under a great many shapes: *dhamma* is also *dhammaṁ*, *dhammena*, *dhammassa*, and it is also *dhammañca* and *dhammampi* and *dhammaṁyeva*, and it is inside every compound that contains it. Nation's threshold (98% of running words, which for general English implies a vocabulary of eight to nine thousand word *families*) therefore cannot be set beside the 36,578 in the last row. They are counting different things.

So the comparison was made properly. Resolving each form to a dictionary headword and then decomposing the headwords through the *Digital Pāli Dictionary*'s own analysis of what each is built from, the vocabulary behind the resolvable seven-tenths of the four Nikāyas comes to roughly **eight thousand distinct words**.[^lemmas] That is the same order of magnitude as the English figure, not four times it, and so the intimidating number in the last row of the table is a fact about Pāli morphology, not about how much a reader has to hold in mind. The forms are many. The words are not.

The curve itself is good for ranking texts against each other, and the rest of this appendix uses it so.

The four collections rank like this:

| | Running words | Distinct forms | Type/token | Covered by the top 1,000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Dīgha | 143,464 | 17,360 | 0.121 | 52.8% |
| Majjhima | 247,885 | 21,613 | 0.087 | 57.4% |
| Saṁyutta | 263,867 | 23,045 | 0.087 | **62.1%** |
| Aṅguttara | 301,002 | 25,468 | 0.085 | 58.1% |

Both measures agree, though they were not designed to. The Saṁyutta is the most accessible of the four collections and the **Dīgha the least**, and the Dīgha also introduces new vocabulary fastest: its type/token ratio is nearly half again the others'. A collection of long framed narratives, thick with proper names and place names, looks like that from underneath. Begin with the Saṁyutta.

### The one place the measurement is misleading

Verse. The Metta Sutta draws only 29.6% of its words from the commonest thousand; the Dhammapada's opening chapter, 37.5%; and the first verse of the Theragāthā has a type/token ratio of 0.927, which means that of fifty-five words, fifty-one are different. By this measurement the texts a Buddhist is most likely to know by heart are among the hardest in the canon, and by this measurement that is correct: metre forces rare words, compressed compounds and unusual word order, and a verse line will not tell you what it means from context because it has no context to spare.

It does not follow that you should not start there. It follows that you start there for a different reason, which is the subject of the first stage.

### Stage 0 · What you already know by heart

Take a text you can already recite (the Maṅgala Sutta, Snp 2.4; the Metta Sutta, Snp 1.8; the Ratana Sutta, Snp 2.1; the Dhammacakkappavattana, SN 56.11) and work through it word by word with the grammar open.

The reason is not that these are easy. The table above says they are not. The reason is that you already know what the text means, so none of your attention is going on the lexicon and all of it can go on the morphology. Every case ending you identify lands on a sentence whose sense you can already check. That is the cheapest grammar practice available to anyone who chants, and it is available on day one.

Expect it to be slow and expect some of it to surprise you. A phrase recited a thousand times turns out to be an absolutive plus a locative absolute, and the recitation had smoothed over a joint that the grammar makes visible.

### Stage 1 · The short Saṁyutta suttas

Now prose, and what follows is the shortest and most repetitive prose in the canon. The three suttas at **SN 22.15–17** are the place to start: *Yadanicca*, *Yaṁdukkha*, *Yadanattā*, seventy-seven, fifty-two and forty-six words long, and drawing 85.7%, 88.5% and 91.3% of their vocabulary from the commonest thousand forms. The last of those is among the most accessible texts in the four Nikāyas by this measure, and far from a curiosity: it is the *anicca–dukkha–anattā* argument in its barest form. The first of the three is read through in full at the close of Chapter 12, together with the two editorial abbreviations that open and close it, *sāvatthinidānaṁ* and *…pe…*, which all three use and which no rule of grammar explains.

Then **SN 22.14** and **SN 35.1**, and then work outward through the Khandhasaṁyutta (SN 22) and the Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN 35). These *saṁyuttas* are built by permutation: one frame, filled with a different member of a short list each time. Having read four of them you have read the vocabulary of forty, and the fifth is where you start noticing the grammar instead of decoding it.

The best-known short suttas measure lower than the texts that open this stage. The *Sabba* (SN 35.23) draws 75.4% of its words from the commonest thousand and the *Kaccānagotta* (SN 12.15) 69.6%; the *Sīsapāvana* (SN 56.31), at 68.0%, would belong to the next stage. The first two are listed here anyway, at the foot of the stage, on the reasoning of Stage 0: a text whose teaching you already know costs less to read than its figure says. That is worth a few points of coverage.

Grammar assumed: Parts I to III, and the participles from Chapter 10. You will not need compounds yet, and so this stage sits where it does.

### Stage 2 · Where the formula gets long

The same repetitive structure carries on at greater length, and with the texts of this stage a reader first covers real ground in a sitting. **SN 22.59**, the *Anattalakkhaṇa*, is 474 words at 81.6% coverage and is the argument of Stage 1 worked out in full. **SN 45.8** analyses the eightfold path in 303 words: its coverage jumps from 78.9% at the commonest thousand to 93.4% at the commonest two thousand, the signature of a text that defines a technical vocabulary and then reuses it: learn the terms once and the rest is free.

Then **MN 63**, the simile of the arrow, which is 1,653 words long and has one of the lowest type/token ratios in this pathway, 0.159. It is the clearest demonstration in the canon that long and difficult are different properties.

Grammar assumed: add compounds, Chapter 13. From here on you will meet them constantly.

### Stage 3 · The Majjhima

The Majjhima's suttas are longer, and they have a structure and a narrative frame that reward reading them as wholes, not mining them. **MN 10**, the Satipaṭṭhāna, at 65.4% coverage and a type/token ratio of 0.211: long and formulaic, and the text you will most want to have read closely. **AN 3.65**, the Kesamutti (the "Kālāma Sutta"), at 62.1%. Then **MN 2**, at 61.4%, and **MN 8**, just under fifty per cent from the commonest thousand and worth the work; then **MN 21**, the simile of the saw, at 53.7%, whose difficulty is almost entirely simile vocabulary, not grammar.

The Vinaya enters here. Its narratives measure with the Majjhima: the first sermons in the Mahākhandhaka (Kd 1) at 65.0%, the going forth of Sāriputta and Moggallāna at 59.1%, the opening of the Vibhaṅga at Verañjā at 58.1%. The ranking behind those figures is drawn from the four Nikāyas alone, so the Vinaya's own vocabulary of robes, bowls, dwellings and procedure counts as rare even where the Vinaya uses it constantly. If anything, then, the figures are a little severe on it.

Grammar assumed: the whole of Parts V and VI. The case-syntax and clause-syntax chapters were written for this stage, and reading them against these suttas, not before them, is the better order.

### Stage 4 · Narrative, and then verse

The Dīgha last of the prose, because its difficulty is real and specific: **DN 2**, the Sāmaññaphala, is 7,090 words at 48.1% coverage, and **DN 16**, the Mahāparinibbāna, is 15,115 at 51.7%. Neither is grammatically harder than the Majjhima. Both are lexically much harder, and long enough that a reader still decoding will not finish. The longer Vinaya narratives belong beside them: Sudinna's story, which leads to the first rule of the Pātimokkha; Devadatta's, through to the schism; and the First Council.

Verse last of all, and by then it will be the second time you have met it. Go back to the chanting texts of Stage 0 and read them again with the metre chapter beside you; then the **Dhammapada**, then the **Theragāthā** and **Therīgāthā**, then the **Sutta Nipāta**, whose oldest strata are the hardest Pāli in the canon and the most rewarding to have earned.

### How to read, once you are reading

Four rules, and people break the first.

**Do not look up every word.** To stop at every unknown form is not to read, and reading builds the reader. Read the sentence, take what you get, and look up only what blocks it.

Read the same text more than once. The second pass through a sutta costs a fraction of the first and teaches more, because the vocabulary is no longer in the way. Here the low type/token texts pay off: reread SN 22.59 instead of moving to a new sutta of the same length.

**Read aloud.** Vowel length and consonant doubling are half the morphology, and they are invisible until pronounced. This is the same instruction as in the front matter and it matters more here, not less.

**Keep a translation shut but nearby.** Use it to check after you have committed to a reading, not to arrive at one. An answer read cold teaches a fact; an answer read after an attempt teaches the reason the fact was needed.

The page is where each text opens in the Pali Text Society edition, for a reader who wants the book, not the screen, and for a passage of the Vinaya it is where the passage opens. Where a collection is printed in a single volume the page is marked as one (Sn p. 46), so that it cannot be read as a reference to a second text. The Khuddakapāṭha's three rows carry no page; it carries none anywhere in this book. Every text is counted whole, as the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana bounds it; a Vinaya passage runs from one of the edition's section headings to another. All are measured against the four-Nikāya frequency ranking described above. Any other text can be ranked the same way, to build a different route: count its distinct words, and see how far down the frequency list one must go before the text is covered.

[^lemmas]: Computed by a lemmatiser that resolves 70.3% of the running words of the four Nikāyas and reduces 55,602 surface forms to 19,097 dictionary headwords and 8,249 base words once compounds are decomposed. The figure is an order of magnitude and not a count. Sampling twenty splits at a time and checking them by hand, about four in twenty are wrong, always where the language has fused something the method cannot see: *nāhaṁ* is *na* + *ahaṁ*, not *nāha* + *ṁ*. An error rate of that size in both directions does not move a figure by a factor of four, which is the only claim being made on it. The unresolved three-tenths are mostly rare compounds, which would decompose into words already counted, so the true figure is unlikely to be much larger.
