    Front matter

    How to Use This Book

    This is a reference grammar of the Pāli of the Theravāda canon, and it is also a course. Two kinds of reader are expected, and they want opposite things. One is beginning, and wants to be taken through the language in an order that builds: sounds, then the noun phrase, then the verb, then how a sentence is put together. The other already reads Pāli, has met a form on the page, and wants one place where that form is set out with its variants and with some indication of whether the texts actually use it. A book arranged for the first reader is tedious for the second, and a book arranged for the second is unusable by the first. This one is arranged for the first and indexed for the second, and the sections below say how.

    One promise runs underneath all of it. Every Pāli sentence in this book is canonical, and every one is cited to the text it stands in. None has been composed to make a point come out neatly. That constraint has a cost, because a real sentence rarely isolates one grammatical feature as cleanly as an invented one would, and it is why the examples reward a second reading: a sentence parsed here is a sentence of the Buddha’s word that you have read, not a demonstration of one.

    Two volumes

    The grammar and the exercises are separate books: this volume carries the explanation, the paradigms and the worked examples, and the companion volume, Pāli: A Workbook, carries the drills and their keys.

    The worked examples are here and not there on purpose, beside the rule each one illustrates, because a reader can check a rule that is shown and cannot check one merely stated. The workbook holds the drilling, and it will be of no use until the chapter beside it has been read.

    The two volumes are numbered alike. The exercises for Chapter 6 are “Exercises: Numerals” in the workbook, and their key is “Answers: Numerals” at the back of it. No cross-reference in either book gives a page or a number, only a name, because names survive repagination.

    The workbook also carries four cumulative reviews, gathered after the exercise sets, not interleaved with them. These are a different instrument from the chapter drills: each one tests what only becomes visible when several chapters are in place, and no item in them can be answered out of a single chapter. R1 covers Part I, R2 Part II, R3 Part III, and R4 the whole of Parts IV to VII.

    How the grammar is arranged

    Eighteen chapters in seven parts, in the order a reader can actually build on:

    Part I, sounds and script, covers the alphabet, the pronunciation and the rules of junction. It comes first because sandhi disguises words, and if you cannot undo a junction you will look up forms that are not there.

    Part II, the noun phrase (nouns, gender and adjectives, pronouns, numerals), and Part III, the verb, are the two halves of the morphology. Between them they account for most of what a page of Pāli is made of.

    Part IV, the non-finite forms, takes the participles, absolutives, infinitives and indeclinables. With these Pāli does what English does with subordinate clauses, and here a reader begins to stop translating word by word.

    Part V, word formation, covers compounds and the prefixes and suffixes. Part VI, syntax, returns to the cases and asks what they do in a sentence, not what their endings are, and then takes the clause and the relative construction. Part VII is a single chapter on metre.

    The order is a teaching order and not a strict dependency chain, and it strains in places. Compounds cannot be discussed without nouns, so Chapter 13 assumes Part II throughout. Case syntax revisits declensions first met in Chapter 3, twelve chapters later, on purpose: the endings are learnable early and what the cases mean is not.

    Four appendices follow. Appendix A gathers the paradigms in full. Chapters carry only the working subset a reader needs at that point, and the complete tables, with every attested variant, are here. Appendix B is a graded reading pathway: which texts to read first, and in what order, once the grammar is in place. Appendix C is the argument behind the introduction: where Pāli came from, what the tradition claims, and why the famous Vinaya ruling on language will not settle it. Appendix D is the bibliography, which names every grammar, dictionary and coursebook the synthesis draws on.

    How to read a page

    A handful of typographic conventions run through both volumes, and they mean one thing each.

    Italic marks a cited Pāli form, anywhere: in prose, in a footnote, in a paradigm cell. Bold has two uses that never collide in practice: it marks an English grammatical term at the point where it is being defined, and it marks whichever element a worked example is drawing attention to. Where an example needs to point at three things at once, the third is bold italic.

    Parentheses mark a rare form. In a paradigm, -o (-e) means that the nominative singular is -o and that -e also occurs but is rare, dialectal, poetic or post-canonical. This is Oberlies’s convention, not a new one, and it frees italic to mean a single consistent thing everywhere. Appendix A’s notation paragraph sets out the two further uses its source makes of the same brackets, which are not rarity markings and are reproduced as he printed them.

    A boxed passage, set off as a block quotation, means one of two things: real variation in the language itself, such as a manuscript spelling or a ṭ where another edition prints t, or a point on which reputable grammars give different and defensible analyses. It never means an argument. Ordinary variation between textbooks is settled silently and stated as fact, which is what any grammar does; the box is reserved for the places where the disagreement is real. A box is always headed by a bold line saying what it is; a block quotation with no such heading is a passage quoted at length, and carries no claim about disagreement at all.

    Footnotes carry the working. A contested dictionary sense, an etymology, a note that a form is found only in the commentaries and not in the canon: these go below instead of interrupting the argument.

    A citation names a text, and it carries a page. MN 10 — M i 56 means the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, standing on page 56 of the first Pali Text Society volume of the Majjhima. The citation promises the page, and the page finds the passage in a book a reader can hold open. Where a text has no volumes the page is marked as one, Dhp p. 19, so that it cannot be read as a second verse. The Vinaya is cited in Brahmāli’s own style, Kd 15 — Vin ii 139, because that is what a reader of his translation will recognise. A few citations carry no page at all. In those the passage could not be placed on one page beyond doubt, and a page that might be wrong is worse than no page. One consequence follows, and it should be said: where a long text is quoted twice from the same page, or from a text that carries no page, the two citations read alike. The Maṅgalasutta is short and is cited eleven times as Kp 5.

    The niggahīta is written ṁ throughout. Other books print ṃ or ŋ for the same sound, and the editions themselves differ; Chapter 1 sets out what the notations are and why this one was chosen.

    The three-layer example

    Every substantial example is given three ways, following Bhikkhu Bodhi’s practice in Reading the Buddha’s Discourses in Pāli: the Pāli as the edition prints it, with its citation; then a word-by-word gloss; then an idiomatic rendering. The middle layer is literal on purpose and is not English. It lets a reader see what each Pāli word contributes, and it will often be ugly for that reason. Bold in the first layer and bold in the third mark the same element, so that the eye can find in the translation what it has just been shown in the original.

    In print the middle layer is set interlinear: each Pāli word stands above its English, and under the English, where the word inflects, sits a short tag in small capitals. The tags use one spelling throughout. M, f and n are the genders; nom, acc, ins, dat, abl, gen, loc and voc the cases; sg and pl the numbers. So the eye reads down a column for one word and across a row for the sentence.

    Where an example is about agreement, a small upright letter after a Pāli word links it to the other words that refer to the same thing and agree with it. In the Vinaya’s ordination sentence in Chapter 4, chāyāa ‘the shadow’ is linked to metabbāa ‘is to be measured’, and saṅgītid ‘the recitation’ to its own ācikkhitabbād. Words that share a letter agree in gender, number and case, with one exception: a relative and its correlative, which Chapter 17 sets out, refer to one thing and agree in gender and number while differing in case. Read the letters first, and the structure of the sentence is visible before a word of it has been translated.

    The recommendation is to cover the second and third layers and try the Pāli first. Reading a gloss you have not attempted teaches a fact; reading it after an attempt teaches the reason the fact is needed.

    Two ways through

    For the reader learning the language: work forward, one chapter at a time, and do the drills for a chapter before moving to the next. Take each cumulative review when its part is finished, instead of saving them all for the end. They are diagnostic, and a diagnosis is useful only while there is still time to act on it. When Part IV is done, start Appendix B’s reading pathway in parallel with the remaining chapters. The reading-volume evidence below warns against waiting for the grammar to be finished before reading anything.

    For the reader consulting: Appendix A is the fastest route to a form, and each chapter’s paradigm tables are the same material in the subset that chapter teaches. The Principal sources line that closes every chapter names the one to three works that chapter leans on most, and what each was leant on for, so that a claim can be traced to the grammar it came from without reading the chapter around it.

    Why the wording matters

    Learning a classical language by its morphology is slow, and it is fair to ask what the exactness buys. The canon asks the same question and answers it, in a passage about how the teaching is lost. The first of four causes of the decline of the true Dhamma is bad memorisation, neither persecution nor heresy. The words and phrases in bold:

    Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhū duggahitaṁ suttantaṁ pariyāpuṇanti dunnikkhittehi padabyañjanehi. Dunnikkhittassa, bhikkhave, padabyañjanassa atthopi dunnayo hoti. (AN 4.160 — A ii 147)

    idhaherebhikkhavemonksvoc plbhikkhūmonksnom plduggahitaṁbadly grasped, poorly learnedsuttantaṁdiscoursepariyāpuṇantithey learn thoroughly, they masterdunnikkhittehibadly set downins plpadabyañjanehiwith words and phrasesdunnikkhittassaof what is badly set downgen sgpadabyañjanassaof the words and phrasesatthothe meaningpitoodunnayohard to draw outhotiis

    Here, monks, the monks master a discourse badly grasped, with its words and phrases badly set down. And of words and phrases badly set down, the meaning too is hard to draw out.

    The commentary reads the second sentence in the narrowest and most practical way available: when the wording has been set down out of order, the explanation cannot be drawn out of it and stated.1 That is a claim about transmission, not scholarship, and it makes a grammar of this language more than a pedantic exercise. The meaning is carried in the wording, in a language where one vowel of an ending decides who is doing what to whom, and if you cannot see the endings you cannot find out that they are wrong.

    The modern evidence points the same way, which is less obvious than it sounds, because a generation of popular advice about language learning says the opposite. Norris and Ortega’s meta-analysis of forty-nine studies found that focused instruction produces large gains, that explicit teaching outperforms implicit, and that the effects held at delayed testing.2 Those studies are of modern languages learned partly for conversation, and no comparable meta-analysis of classical-language teaching exists, so carrying the finding across to Pāli is an inference, not a result. It seems a safe one. Every condition under which explicit instruction pays best is present here at full strength: adult literate learners, a heavily inflected morphology, and a goal that is reading, not speech. A grammar-forward text is not a concession to how these things have always been done.

    How to study a classical language

    The canon also describes the method, and it is more specific than one might expect. A monk masters the teaching, and then four things are said of what he has mastered. The four stages are in bold:

    Tassa te dhammā sotānugatā honti, vacasā paricitā, manasānupekkhitā, diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā. (AN 4.191 — A ii 185)

    tassaof him, for himgen sgtethosedhammāteachingssotānugatāfollowed by ear, listened tohontiarevacasāby speech, verballyparicitārehearsed, made familiarmanasānupekkhitāmentally reconsidered, gone over in the minddiṭṭhiyāby viewsuppaṭividdhāwell penetrated, thoroughly understood

    His teachings are followed by ear, rehearsed aloud, gone over in the mind, well penetrated by view.

    Four stages, and they are four operations, not four intensities of one. Hearing is exposure. Vacasā paricita, made familiar by speech, is recitation; paricita is literally “piled up”, built by accumulation. Manasānupekkhita, gone over in the mind, is review without the text in front of one. And diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdha, penetrated by view, is understanding, which the sequence puts fourth and not first.

    The formula recurs across the canon, and it describes sajjhāya, “recitation, rehearsal”: the tradition’s technique for holding a text without writing, and the technique that preserved this canon through the centuries before it was written down. It was more than a devotional flourish laid on top of study. For most of this literature’s history it was study.

    That matters here because the modern evidence points at the same two things. Of the ten study techniques Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed in 2013, two came out rated high utility, and they were practice testing and distributed practice: testing yourself, and spreading the sessions out. A meta-analysis of the same ten techniques across 242 studies and 169,179 participants ranked those two first again.3 Recitation from memory is both at once, which is my own observation and not theirs: the recall is the test, and daily recitation sustained over years is distribution in an extreme form. I would not press the parallel further than that. The tradition was not doing cognitive psychology, and that its practice turns out to be well designed is a happy accident, not an argument.

    A reference grammar teaches the system; only volume of reading builds a reader. This is the standing complaint about grammar-first training in every classical language, and it is fair. Two meta-analyses of extensive reading, one of 34 studies and one of 71 samples and nearly six thousand learners, find medium to large gains in comprehension, reading rate and vocabulary, and the second reports that adults gain more from it than children do.4 So the grammar is not the destination, and finishing it is not the point at which reading begins.

    There is a threshold to respect, though, and so the pathway in Appendix B is graded, not a list of good suttas. Nation’s work on vocabulary coverage puts unassisted comprehension of ordinary written text at about 98% of the running words known; at 95% a reader meets an unknown word every twenty, which is decoding, not reading.5 Sutta Pāli is far more formulaic and repetitive than ordinary prose, so the effective threshold is almost certainly lower here, and by how much nobody has measured. The practical consequence stands either way. Reading text far above your coverage does not build fluency, and reading text a little above it does.

    What follows for a reader of this book is concrete enough:

    • Recite the Pāli aloud. Not silently. Vowel length and the doubled consonants are half the morphology, and they are invisible until they are pronounced.
    • Test before you look. Cover the gloss, cover the answer key, and attempt the form first. The attempt teaches, and a failed attempt teaches more than a fluent read-through.
    • Space the repetitions. A paradigm met on four separate days will hold; the same total time in one sitting will not.
    • Start reading before you feel ready, and read within your range. Appendix B exists for both halves of that, and beginning it at the end of Part IV, not at the end of the book, turns the grammar into reading.
    • Do the cumulative reviews on time. They are the only place in either volume where two chapters are made to interfere with each other, and the real errors appear there.

    One more finding bears on why anyone finishes. In the largest meta-analysis of second-language motivation, a learner’s vision of the reader they want to become correlated strongly with intended effort and only weakly with measured achievement.6 Read that carefully: motivation does not multiply ability. It predicts whether someone keeps turning up, which over an undertaking measured in years is most of what there is to predict. For a reader whose reason for learning Pāli is to meet the Buddha’s words without an intermediary, that reason will still be there in year three.

    And one caution, which the canon supplies too. The Buddha describes a monk who masters the discourses, spends his days on them, neglects seclusion and does not commit himself to inner calm; the verdict on him is pariyattibahulo, no dhammavihārī: “one keen on learning, not one who lives by the Dhamma” (AN 5.73 — A iii 86). The same is said in the same terms of one who spends the day teaching, and of one who spends it reciting.7 Grammar is a means. It is a good means, it is not the point, and the tradition that produced these texts said so first.

    What this book owes

    The chapters began as Bhikkhu Sunyo’s course handouts, the lessons of a Pāli course, still marked draft, which map onto the first fourteen chapters here and supplied their structure, much of their sequencing and a good deal of their explanation. Little of his wording survives the redrafting, and the debt is to something else: the decision about what a beginner needs to meet first, which is the hardest part of a grammar to get right and the part a reader never notices when it has been done well.

The Pāli text is the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition of the Vipassana Research Institute, with the Buddha Jayanti and Pali Text Society editions consulted wherever a spelling is in question.
