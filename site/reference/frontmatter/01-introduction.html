    Front matter

    Introduction: The Language of the Canon

    Pāli is the language in which the Theravāda canon has come down to us. That much is agreed. Almost everything else that is commonly said about it is inference, not record: where the language came from, whose everyday speech it once was, how close it stands to the words the Buddha himself used.

    The distinction matters from the first page, because the two kinds of statement look alike once they are set in print. A statement about the grammar can be checked by anyone who has the texts: it says that a particular ending does a particular job, and the texts either bear it out or they do not. A statement about the language’s history cannot be checked in that way. It is reconstructed from inscriptions, from comparison with related languages and from the internal evidence of the texts themselves, and competent scholars reconstruct it differently. This book states the first kind as fact, because it is fact, and marks the second kind as inference wherever it has occasion to mention it. The grammar is settled. Where the language came from is not.

    What is settled

    Pāli is a Middle Indo-Aryan language. It descends, along with the other Prakrits, from the same Old Indo-Aryan stock as Sanskrit, but it does not descend from the Sanskrit we have.

    In its sounds and its endings it belongs with the languages of western India, not with those of the east, and this can be shown from evidence that is dated, localised, and has nothing to do with Buddhism. The emperor Aśoka had his edicts cut in stone in the third century BCE at both ends of his empire, and the same imperial text comes out differently at each end: at Girnar in the west the king is rāja, at Kālsī in the east he is lāja. Set Pāli beside those two columns and it goes with the western one nearly throughout. Appendix C sets the columns out.

    Scattered through it, however, are forms that do not fit that pattern and belong with the eastern column instead. The clearest of them needs no grammar at all to see: the canon writes nibbāna where Sanskrit writes nirvāṇa, and the difference in the final consonant is an eastern trait, not a western one. There are not many of them, and Appendix C lists what they are. What they are evidence of is the harder question, and Appendix C takes that up too.

    The word pāḷi is not, in the texts, the name of a language. It means the text: the canonical wording, as against the commentary written to explain it. A commentator who writes pārājikapāḷiyaṁ means “in the text of the Pārājikas”, not “in the Pārājikas’ language”. The name we use is a shortening of pāḷibhāsā, “the language of the text”, a phrase neither the canon nor the commentaries ever use, and which turns up only in the sub-commentaries and later. Using Pāli as the name of a particular language appears to be no older than the eighteenth century.1 The tradition’s name for the language is māgadhabhāsā, “the Magadha language”. The canon’s own word, in the one passage where it needs one, is ariyaka, “Aryan”, set against milakkha, a speaker of something else.

    And it was nobody’s mother tongue. Von Hinüber puts it without qualification: Pāli “has never been a spoken language neither in Magadha nor elsewhere”,2 and the reason is the western character just described. A language with the features of a region hundreds of miles from where the events it narrates took place was not the daily speech of either region. Whatever the Buddha spoke, he did not speak Pāli in the form in which we have it.

    What is not

    Almost everything else: where the language came from, how far the westward recasting altered it, and what the Buddha’s own speech was like. These are inferences. Some of them are good ones, and none is checkable in the way a declension is checkable.

    The dispute has one famous text at its centre. Two brahmin monks complain to the Buddha that the word of the Buddha is being spoiled, and propose a remedy; he refuses the remedy, and rules:

    Anujānāmi, bhikkhave, sakāya niruttiyā buddhavacanaṁ pariyāpuṇitun ti. (Kd 15 — Vin ii 139)

    anujānāmiI allowbhikkhavemonksvoc plsakāyaone’s ownf ins sg, agreeing with niruttiyāniruttiyā(in) expressionbuddhavacanaṁthe word of the Buddhapariyāpuṇituṁto learn thoroughly, to master

    I allow you, monks, to learn the word of the Buddha in its own expression.

    Read sakāya niruttiyā as “each in his own dialect” and the ruling licenses translating the canon into whatever a listener speaks. Read it as “in its own expression” and it forbids that very thing, and requires the Buddha’s wording to be kept. The commentary takes the second; the grammar permits both; and a century and a half of philology has not closed the question. Appendix C sets that argument out: what the tradition claims and where, what the Aśokan inscriptions show, what the two disputed words can and cannot be made to mean, and which scholars currently hold what. If you want only the grammar, you can pass it by.

    The closest surviving witness

    If Pāli is not the Buddha’s own speech, the question is what it is worth, and the answer is a comparison, not a claim. Closest needs something to be closest to.

    Pāli is neither the only surviving witness to what the early Buddhist texts said nor the oldest. Fragments survive in Gāndhārī, the language of the north-western kingdom of Gandhāra, dated as early as the first century BCE: they are the oldest Buddhist manuscripts in existence, and roughly a thousand years older than the oldest Pāli manuscript we possess.3 Other schools’ versions of the same discourses survive in Sanskrit and in the mixed language conventionally called Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit. The Chinese and Tibetan canons preserve, in translation, a great deal of Indian material whose originals are lost. Against that field, any claim resting on the age of the surviving copy fails at once.

    Its claim rests on two other things. It carries one school’s canon entire, neither fragments nor a selection, in a single consistent language. And that language stayed Middle Indo-Aryan. The other Indian recensions were progressively reworked in the direction of Sanskrit, the prestige language of learned India, and the reworking reached the wording itself; the Theravāda tradition, though it shows traces of the same pull, never carried it through. So a Pāli page stands nearer in its actual forms to what an Indian speaker of the Buddha’s era would have said than the corresponding Sanskrit page does, whatever the dates on the two manuscripts. Age of witness and age of language are different measurements, and only the second favours Pāli.

    Between the Buddha’s speech and the Pāli of the printed editions there stand a period of oral transmission, a recasting of the language westward, and an editorial tradition that reached its present shape centuries later. Each of those is a distance, and none of them is small. But no surviving body of Buddhist text stands closer. Pāli is the closest surviving witness to the language the Buddha spoke. Not the thing itself, and near enough to be listened to.

    What follows for this book

    The grammar taught here is the grammar of the received Theravāda canon as the printed editions actually have it. It is not a reconstruction of an earlier stage of the language, and it does not tidy the text into what a historical linguist might expect the text to have been. Three consequences follow, and they will show on almost every page.

    Where the editions disagree, this book says so. The canon survives in several printed recensions, among them the Burmese Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, the Sinhalese Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society’s roman-script edition, each prepared by scholars working from manuscripts that do not always agree with one another. Where such an editor has had to choose between two spellings, the edition records the rejected alternatives in its apparatus: the register of variant readings printed under or beside the running text, with a mark against each to show which manuscript tradition it came from. A grammar that reads only the running text will present one editor’s choice as though it were the language. This book goes to the apparatus, and where the choice is a real one it is reported, not settled in silence.

    Forms the grammarians derive but the texts never use are marked as such. The Pāli grammatical tradition completes its paradigms by rule, so a full table will contain cells that no surviving text ever fills. Those cells belong there, because they show how the system works. But you are entitled to know which forms have been read somewhere and which have only been calculated, and the two are not distinguished by looking at them.

    Forms that look irregular because they are eastern are explained as eastern, not filed under exceptions. An exception is something a reader has to memorise. A survival from an earlier version of the text is something a reader can understand, and understanding it costs a sentence.

    That is a narrower object than “the Buddha’s language”. It is also the one a reader can check, and it is enough for the purpose. Someone who learns this system can open the Saṁyutta at any page and follow the argument in the words it was made in, which is in the end the only reason to learn it.
