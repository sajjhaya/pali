    Chapter 16

    Clause and Sentence Syntax

    One finite verb, four sentences

    The last chapter was about what a single ending does. This one is about what happens when a sentence gets long, and it is best begun by looking at one.

    Sīho, bhikkhave, migarājā sāyanhasamayaṁ āsayā nikkhamati. Āsayā nikkhamitvā vijambhati. Vijambhitvā samantā catuddisā anuviloketi. Samantā catuddisā anuviloketvā tikkhattuṁ sīhanādaṁ nadati. (AN 4.33 — A ii 33)

    sīhothe lionbhikkhavemonksmigarājāking of beastssāyanhasamayaṁin the eveningm acc sg, adverbialāsayāfrom his lairm abl sgnikkhamaticomes outnikkhamitvāhaving come outvijambhatihe stretchesvijambhitvāhaving stretchedsamantā catuddisāin all four directionsanuviloketihe surveysanuviloketvāhaving surveyedtikkhattuṁthree timessīhanādaṁa lion’s roarm acc sgnadatihe roars

    The lion, monks, the king of beasts, comes out from his lair in the evening. Having come out from his lair, he stretches. Having stretched, he surveys the four directions all around. Having surveyed the four directions all around, he roars a lion’s roar three times.

    Four sentences, four finite verbs, and every one of them is anticipated by a non-finite form that repeats the sentence before. The pattern is how the passage is built, and missing it, you will read four disconnected statements where the Pāli gives one continuous action.

    That is this chapter’s subject. Pāli subordinates by case and by participle, not by conjunction, and the finite verb is the scarce resource that a long sentence is organised around. English marks its subordinate clauses at the front, with ‘when’, ‘although’, ‘because’, ‘if’; Pāli marks them, when it marks them at all, on the ending of a participle in the middle. The whole of what follows is an inventory of how it does that, and of what to do when it has not done it at all.

    What holds a sentence together

    Start from Perniola’s definition, because it is the narrowest: a sentence or clause is a group of words with a finite verb and a complete meaning, and “the finite verb is sometimes understood.”1 Both halves matter. A finite verb makes a clause a clause, and Pāli will often decline to supply one.

    Duroiselle states the ordinary order in four rules, and they hold well enough to be useful even though every one of them has exceptions.2 The predicate comes last. In a simple sentence with an object the order is subject, object, predicate: dāso kammaṁ karoti, ‘the slave does the work’. Words qualifying the subject or the object come before it, and adverbs come before the verb. Adverbs of time come first of all, and so many suttas open ekaṁ samayaṁ or tena kho pana samayena before anything else has happened.

    His first rule is too strong, and his own grammar contains the evidence against it. Perniola records that a question can be marked by putting the verb at the front: atthi nu kho, bhante, añño koci mayā upaṭṭhākataro, ‘is there, sir, anyone who is a better attendant to you?’ (MN 81 — M ii 51)3 Word order in Pāli is not free, but it is a rhetorical instrument, not a grammatical one: the endings carry the grammar, and that leaves the order available for emphasis. Fronting is how a Pāli sentence shouts.

    The sentence with no verb

    Where English requires is, Pāli commonly requires nothing. The forms of hoti and atthi are understood, not written, and this is at its most frequent where the predicate is a past participle or an agent noun.4 Wait for the verb in such a sentence and you will wait for ever.

    Appamādo amatapadaṁ, pamādo maccuno padaṁ. (Dhp 21 — Dhp p. 4)

    appamādodiligencem nom sgamatapadaṁthe state of the deathlessn nom sgpamādonegligencem nom sgmaccunoof deathm gen sgpadaṁthe state, the footingn nom sg

    Diligence is the state of the deathless; negligence is the state of death.

    There are four words in the first half and no verb among them, and the sentence is complete. Notice also what does not happen: appamādo is masculine and amatapadaṁ is neuter, and they sit in apposition without either changing to match the other. When a substantive stands where the verb would be, the concord of gender and number lapses: the two nouns are identified with each other, not made to agree.5 Where an adjective or a past participle is the predicate, concord holds, and it is gender and number that are at stake: so gato, sā gatā, taṁ gataṁ.

    Agreement, and what it will not tell you

    Three agreements operate, and they are best stated flat before the exceptions crowd in.6 A finite verb agrees with its subject in number and person. An adjective or participle qualifying a noun agrees with it in gender, number and case. A relative agrees with its antecedent in gender, number and person, the next chapter’s subject. When several subjects of different persons share one verb, the verb goes into the first person plural if a first person is among them, and otherwise into the second person plural.

    The commonest exception is not marginal at all. Sati, the locative singular of the present participle of atthi, is the workhorse of the whole absolute construction, and it stands beside the feminine jāti at jātiyā sati and beside a plural at saṅkhāresu … sati, masculine singular in both.7 The Digital Pali Dictionary files two entries for those three letters, one locative singular and one locative plural. Wijesekera reports that the habit is shared with other Indo-European languages, and English does the same thing in ‘there’s two of them’. The form does not agree, and a reader should stop waiting for it to.

    The absolutive chain

    Return to the lion. The construction there is the commonest way Pāli builds a long sentence, and it has one governing rule: when a run of actions shares a subject, every verb but the last goes into the absolutive.8 The absolutive is indeclinable and carries no person, no number and no tense; what it carries is priority. The one finite verb at the end supplies everything the others have gone without.

    Atha kho bhagavā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya sāvatthiṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi. (MN 26 — M i 160)

    atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgpubbaṇhasamayaṁin the morningm acc sg, adverbialnivāsetvāhaving dressedpattacīvaraṁbowl and roben acc sgādāyahaving takensāvatthiṁSāvatthīf acc sgpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sgpāvisihe entered3 sg aor

    Then the Blessed One dressed in the morning, took his bowl and robe, and entered Sāvatthī for alms.

    The English needs three finite verbs and a conjunction. The Pāli has one finite verb and two absolutives, and it is a single grammatical unit, not three joined ones.

    The chain has three shapes, and Bodhi distinguishes them.9 The plain sequence is above. The second is rhetorical repetition, where the same absolutive is chained across a list (jātisaṁvattanikepi saṅkhāre abhisaṅkharitvā jarāsaṁvattanikepi saṅkhāre abhisaṅkharitvā, and so on for death and for sorrow, SN 56.42 — S v 449), and the effect is cumulative, not narrative. The third is the lion’s, and nothing in Pāli prose is more characteristic: the finite verb of one sentence becomes the absolutive that opens the next. Each sentence is joined on at the last action of the sentence before. Whether one prints such a passage as four sentences or as one is only a decision about punctuation, since the Pāli has no punctuation to consult.

    And the chain has a limit, on which this chapter turns. It works because the subject does not change. The moment the prior action belongs to somebody else, the absolutive cannot be used, because it cannot say whose action it is. Pāli then reaches for a different instrument.

    What an absolute construction is

    Take a noun or a pronoun, put it in an oblique case, and agree a participle with it. The result has a subject and a predicate of its own, and it is therefore a clause; it stands outside the grammar of the main sentence, and it is therefore called absolute, from Latin absolutus, ‘freed, detached’. Wijesekera’s formulation is exact, and every word of it counts: the construction “differs from the simple temporal or modal use of a case only in the predicative character of the participle. Since the latter’s presence makes it virtually a clause separate from the main sentence having a predicate of its own, it is regarded as freed or detached (absolutus) from the construction of the rest of the sentence. But there is always a logical connection between the two.”10

    Two practical consequences follow from that definition. Because the absolute has its own subject, its subject is normally different from the main clause’s; that is its purpose. And because the case is what detaches the phrase, the construction is named after the case it uses: locative absolute, genitive absolute, and the two rarer ones.

    Which gives the rule a reader most needs, and Bodhi is the only source in the collection to state it as a contrast:

    If the prior action is done by the subject of the main verb, the absolutive of that action is used; if the prior action is done by someone or something else, the locative absolute with the past participle is used.11

    What the tradition calls it

    The indigenous grammarians have no category ‘absolute construction’ at all. What they have is a case-substitution rule, and it sits in the kāraka section beside the substitutions the last chapter taught. Kaccāyana gives two suttas, neither of which mentions a participle:

    Sutta Text The vutti’s gloss
    Kacc 305 Anādare ca anādare chaṭṭhīvibhatti hoti, sattamī ca
    Kacc 313 Kālabhāvesu ca kālabhāvesu ca kattari payujjamāne sattamīvibhatti hoti

    The first says that in the sense of anādara (conventionally ‘disregard’) the sixth case-ending occurs, and the seventh. The second says that in expressions of kāla, ‘time’, and bhāva, ‘state’, the seventh occurs when the agent is being employed.12 Between them they cover the locative and genitive absolutes exactly, without ever naming a construction. That is no oversight: the tradition is describing the uses of a vibhatti, and an absolute is one use of the seventh vibhatti.

    Aggavaṁsa’s Saddanīti sharpens both. His rule for the locative is Yambhāvo bhāvalakkhaṇaṁ, and he unpacks the compound in the sentence after it: bhāvantarassa lakkhaṇaṁ bhāvalakkhaṇaṁ, “bhāvalakkhaṇa is the marking of one state by another”; and then, “when that state is understood, the seventh occurs, and the sixth is also seen.”13 Moggallāna and the Payogasiddhi put the same rule in nearly the same words: yassa bhāvo bhāvantarassa lakkhaṇaṁ bhavati, tato sattamī, ‘the state by which another state is marked goes into the seventh’.

    And his gloss on anādara is better than any Western rendering of it. Anādaro’ti dvinnaṁ lakkhaṇa-lakkhitabbakriyānaṁ ekappahārena pavattiyā adhivacanaṁ: “anādara is a term for the simultaneous occurrence of the two actions, the marking one and the one to be marked.”14 That means ‘at the same time as’, not ‘disregard’, and it explains at a stroke why one construction covers both ‘while’ and ‘in spite of’, which every grammar in the collection reports as two senses and none of them accounts for. Something done in spite of something else is something done while it is going on.

    Which participles may stand in one

    Wijesekera puts a restriction on it, and the restriction is specific enough to be tested. The participle in an absolute construction is either a present participle or a past passive one. The future passive participle is never so employed, nor is the past active participle, “since these cannot agree with the substantive put in the oblique case.”15

    Half of that holds and half does not. No future passive participle stands in an absolute construction anywhere I have looked, and the reason he gives is sound. But the past active participle in -āvin does, and the case is canonical:

    Seyyathāpi, ānanda, gahapatissa vā gahapatiputtassa vā manuññaṁ bhojanaṁ bhuttāvissa bhattasammado hoti. (DN 17 — D ii 195)

    seyyathāpijust asānandaĀnandagahapatissaof a householderm gen sggahapatiputtassaof a householder’s sonm gen sgmanuññaṁdeliciousn acc sgbhojanaṁfoodn acc sgbhuttāvissaof one who has eatenm gen sg, past active participle of bhuñjatibhattasammadodrowsiness after a mealm nom sghotithere is

    Just as, Ānanda, when a householder or a householder’s son has eaten delicious food there is drowsiness after the meal.

    Bhuttāvī is a past active participle (the Digital Pali Dictionary resolves it √bhuj + tāvī and files it as one), and it stands here in the genitive with its own object bhojanaṁ, marking a state that belongs to somebody other than the subject of hoti.16 So the rule is right about the future passive participle and too strong about the past active one. Weakened, it still holds: a reader will meet the present participle and the past passive one in the great majority of cases, and bhuttāvissa is rare enough that Thānuttamo had to go looking for it.

    The locative absolute

    This is the ordinary one. It occurs everywhere, it is the construction the tradition’s bhāvalakkhaṇa rule is written for, and the tense of the participle tells the reader almost everything: a past participle where the subordinate action precedes the main one, a present participle where the two run together.17

    Parinibbute bhagavati saha parinibbānā mahābhūmicālo ahosi bhiṁsanako salomahaṁso. (DN 16 — D ii 156)

    parinibbuteafully extinguishedm loc sg, past participlebhagavatiathe Blessed Onem loc sgsaha parinibbānātogether with the full extinguishmentmahābhūmicāloa great earthquakem nom sgahosithere was3 sg aorbhiṁsanakoterrifyingsalomahaṁsohair-raising

    When the Blessed One had attained final Nibbāna, there was, together with the final Nibbāna, a great earthquake, terrifying and hair-raising.

    Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    imasmiṁathisn loc sgveyyākaraṇasmiṁaexpositionn loc sgbhaññamāneabeing spokenn loc sg, present participle of the passive bhaññati, from bhaṇatiāyasmato koṇḍaññassaof the venerable Koṇḍaññam gen sgvirajaṁdust-freevītamalaṁstainlessdhammacakkhuṁthe eye of the Dhamman nom sgudapādiarose3 sg aor

    And while this exposition was being spoken, the dust-free, stainless eye of the Dhamma arose in the venerable Koṇḍañña.

    In the first the Buddha’s parinibbāna is over before the earth moves, and the participle is past. In the second the discourse is still being delivered when Koṇḍañña’s insight breaks, and the participle is present. Neither sentence contains a word meaning ‘when’.

    The six senses

    The temporal sense is the base, but it is not the only one. Wijesekera lists six, and four of them are invisible to anyone taught only ‘when’.18

    Sense Marked by Example
    Time nothing; the default parinibbute bhagavati (DN 16 — D ii 156)
    Attendant circumstance a present participle ukkāsu dhāriyamānāsu … niyyāsi (DN 3 — D i 108)
    Cause context; often a verb of feeling dibbe cakkaratane antarahite anattamano (DN 26)
    Condition sati or asati, usually jātiyā sati jarāmaraṇaṁ hoti (SN 12.49 — S ii 78)
    Hypothesis an interrogative main clause dassane, bhagavā, sati kathaṁ paṭipajjitabbaṁ (DN 16 — D ii 141)
    Concession eva, evaṁ pi, or a negative main clause deve vassante … neva passeyya (DN 16)

    A reader of the suttas meets the conditional use first and most often, because the entire formula of dependent origination is built on it:

    Jātiyā sati jarāmaraṇaṁ hotī ti. (SN 12.49 — S ii 78)

    jātiyābirthf loc sgsatithere beingm loc sg, present participle of atthijarāmaraṇaṁold-age-and-deathn nom sghotithere istiend of quotation

    ‘When there is birth, there is old-age-and-death.’

    No word in that sentence says ‘if’ or ‘because’. The locative absolute says only that one state is the mark of another, as the doctrine says too, and so Aggavaṁsa, illustrating his rule for the locative absolute, reaches past all the examples a grammarian might have invented and quotes imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imasmiṁ asati idaṁ na hoti.19 The grammar and the doctrine are the same sentence.

    The concessive sense reverses the reader’s expectation, and ‘when’ produces nonsense in it:

    Deve vassante deve gaḷagaḷāyante vijjullatāsu niccharantīsu asaniyā phalantiyā neva passeyya, na pana saddaṁ suṇeyya. (DN 16 — D ii 131)

    deveathe skym loc sgvassantearainingm loc sg, pres partgaḷagaḷāyanteathunderingm loc sg, pres part of the onomatopoeic gaḷagaḷāyativijjullatāsubthe lightning-streaksf loc plniccharantīsubflashing outf loc pl, pres partasaniyācthe thunderboltf loc sgphalantiyācsplittingf loc sg, pres partneva passeyyahe would not seena pana saddaṁ suṇeyyanor would he hear a sound

    Even with the rain falling, the sky thundering, lightning flashing and thunderbolts splitting, he would neither see nor hear a sound.

    Five nouns and four participles, all in the locative, all in a single absolute clause, and the concessive force comes from the negative in the main clause, not from any of them. Wijesekera’s observation is that the concessive reading is available to the locative absolute where the genitive absolute would carry anādara, which, on Aggavaṁsa’s definition of anādara, is the same fact stated twice, not a coincidence.

    The agent, and the phrases with no subject at all

    When the participle is passive, the logical subject can be added in the instrumental while the grammatical subject stays in the locative. That is how the Dhammacakkappavattana’s closing sentences are built, and the instrumental is often the first sign that the locative is absolute, not local.

    Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    pavattiteaset rollingn loc sg, past partbhagavatāby the Blessed Onem ins sgdhammacakkeathe wheel of Dhamman loc sgbhummā devāthe earth-dwelling godsm nom plsaddaṁa crym acc sganussāvesuṁraised3 pl aor

    And when the wheel of Dhamma had been set rolling by the Blessed One, the earth-dwelling gods raised a cry.

    At the other extreme the subject may be missing entirely, and the adverb evaṁ stands in its place.20 Two such phrases are so common that they are effectively single words: evaṁ vutte, ‘when this had been said’, which introduces most of the replies in the canon; and evaṁ sante, ‘this being so’, which introduces most of the traps.

    Evaṁ sante, bho, na mayaṁ jānāma. (MN 93 — M ii 157)

    evaṁthus, sosantethere beingm loc sg, pres part of atthibhosirna mayaṁ jānāmawe do not know

    ‘That being so, sir, we do not know.’

    The genitive absolute

    The same construction in the sixth case, and it is much rarer. Wijesekera calls it restricted “to a few standing phrases”, and reports two conditions inherited from Sanskrit that Pāli mostly keeps: the noun is almost always a person or a personal pronoun, and the predicate has a durative sense: a present participle, or an adjective serving as one.21

    It is the construction that carries the Bodhisatta’s going-forth, which is the passage the grammarians themselves reach for:

    So kho ahaṁ … akāmakānaṁ mātāpitūnaṁ assumukhānaṁ rudantānaṁ kesamassuṁ ohāretvā kāsāyāni vatthāni acchādetvā agārasmā anagāriyaṁ pabbajiṁ. (MN 26 — M i 163)

    so kho ahaṁI, that onem nom sgakāmakānaṁaunwillingm gen plmātāpitūnaṁamother and fatherm gen plassumukhānaṁawith tear-stained facesm gen plrudantānaṁaweepingm gen pl, pres partkesamassuṁhair and beardn acc sgohāretvāhaving shaved offkāsāyāni vatthāniochre robesn acc placchādetvāhaving put onagārasmāfrom homen abl sganagāriyaṁto homelessnessn acc sgpabbajiṁI went forth1 sg aor

    And I, though my mother and father were unwilling and were weeping with tear-stained faces, shaved off my hair and beard, put on ochre robes, and went forth from home into homelessness.

    That is anādara on the traditional analysis and ‘although’ on ours, and the two amount to the same thing: the parents’ weeping and the going-forth are simultaneous, and the sentence declines to subordinate one to the other. Notice what else the sentence does. Four words in the genitive make a clause with its own subject; two absolutives carry the actions that share the main subject; and one aorist at the end carries the whole. Every device in this chapter is in that one sentence, which is one reason the passage is quoted so often by people who are not thinking about grammar at all.

    The genitive absolute is not always concessive. In its plain temporal use it is interchangeable with the locative, and the clearest demonstration of that is a phrase which occurs in both cases:

    Atha kho tesaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ acirapakkantassa bhagavato ayamantarākathā udapādi. (DN 14 — D ii 8)

    tesaṁ bhikkhūnaṁof those monksm gen placirapakkantassaanot long departedm gen sg, past part compounded with acira ‘not long’bhagavatoathe Blessed Onem gen sgayaṁ antarākathāthis conversation in the meantimef nom sgudapādiarose3 sg aor

    Then, not long after the Blessed One had left, this conversation arose among those monks.

    Atha kho māro pāpimā acirapakkante āyasmante ānande yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami. (DN 16 — D ii 104)

    māro pāpimāMāra the Evil Onem nom sgacirapakkanteanot long departedm loc sgāyasmante ānandeathe venerable Ānandam loc sgyena … tenato whereupasaṅkamihe approached3 sg aor

    Then Māra the Evil One, not long after the venerable Ānanda had left, approached the Blessed One.

    Same idiom, same participle, same sense, two cases. And the pair resolves something Wijesekera flagged and could not explain. He noticed that pakkanta- was the only past participle he could find in a genitive absolute, contrary to the durative rule, and left it as an exception.22 Thānuttamo’s refinement accounts for it: the genitive absolute admits a past participle as the final member of a compound, and acira-pakkanta is one. The exception turns out to be a second clause of the rule.

    The accusative absolute

    Rarer again, and confined in practice to one participle. Santaṁ, the accusative singular of the present participle of atthi, appears with a noun in the accusative, and the sense is the ordinary absolute sense of a state obtaining while something else is the case.

    Santaṁ yeva kho pana paraṁ lokaṁ ‘natthi paro loko’ ti saṅkappeti. (MN 60 — M i 402)

    santaṁaexistingm acc sg, pres part of atthiyevaindeedparaṁ lokaṁathe other worldm acc sgnatthi paro lokothere is no other worldtiend of quotationsaṅkappetihe forms the thought

    Although the other world does exist, he forms the thought ‘there is no other world’.

    Two facts support it that no argument from frequency could. The first is that the commentary names it. Buddhaghosa, glossing the Poṭṭhapāda Sutta’s evaṁ santaṁ, writes: evaṁ santan ti evaṁ sante. Bhummatthe hi etaṁ upayogavacanaṁ: “‘evaṁ santaṁ’ means ‘evaṁ sante’; for this is an accusative in the sense of the locative.”23 He does what a grammarian does: he identifies an unexpected ending by naming the ending it stands for. And the second is that the accusative phrase has a locative twin in the same stock formula, evaṁ sante beside evaṁ santaṁ, which is the same demonstration the genitive gave above.

    Grammars differ on the accusative absolute. Wijesekera §56 reads santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ byāpādaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ byāpādo’ ti pajānāti (MN 10 — M i 60) as a probable accusative absolute: ‘there being inward ill-will, he knows that there is inward ill-will’. Bodhi reads the parallel line as an ordinary direct object with a participle agreeing with it: ‘he understands the lust for forms existing internally’. Both readings fit the Pāli, and the commentary supports each in a different place: bhummatthe at DN 9, but at DN 4 it glosses tathā santaṁ yeva as itthambhūtākhyānatthe upayogavacanaṁ, an accusative describing a state of being, and resolves it with a genitive. The safe position is that santaṁ + accusative is sometimes absolute and sometimes not, and that the main verb decides: where the accusative could be its object, it probably is.

    It should also be said that no premodern Pāli grammarian classified the accusative with the locative and the genitive here. Collins is careful about this, and he is right to be: the accusative absolute and the instrumental “semi-absolute” are modern Western categories laid over Pāli, not divisions the tradition made.24 That does not make them wrong. It does mean that if you do not find them in Kaccāyana, you have missed nothing.

    The nominative absolute, which is probably not one

    Three sources in this collection report a nominative absolute in Pāli. Duroiselle asserts one and gives no examples.25 The Niruttidīpanī claims one, and the tradition’s other grammars make the same claim in the same words (Kvaci paṭhamāpi bahulaṁ dissati, ‘sometimes the nominative too is frequently seen’), with two verses from the Jātaka to prove it. Wijesekera and Collins both report a handful of Nikāya instances while hedging about them. It needs settling carefully, because nowhere else in this chapter do the sources and the evidence come apart.

    The tradition’s two verses do not show what they are offered for. They are canonical (Gacchanto so bhāradvājo, addassa accutaṁ isiṁ and Yāyamāno mahārājā, addā sīdantare nage, both in the Jātaka), and the grammarians quote them with small slips of their own.26 But in each of them the nominative participle agrees with the subject of the main verb: it is Bhāradvāja who is going and Bhāradvāja who sees the sage. That is an ordinary participle qualifying a subject, and it is not detached from anything. Wijesekera makes this objection and Collins makes it independently; modern grammar, he says, “would see the participles simply as adjectives qualifying the subject of the main clause.”

    The Nikāya evidence comes down to one word, and the editions disagree about it. The sentence is DN 1’s account of the world contracting, and all three sources rest their case on it. Read the editions side by side:

    Edition DN 1
    Pali Text Society, D I 17 Saṁvaṭṭamāno loko yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvaṭṭanikā honti
    Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana Saṁvaṭṭamāne loke yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvattanikā honti
    Buddha Jayanti Saṁvaṭṭamāne loke yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvattanikā honti

    The nominative is one edition’s reading against two, and saṁvaṭṭamāno occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana in any of its four layers. The Burmese and Sinhalese editions read a perfectly ordinary locative absolute (‘when the world is contracting, beings are for the most part reborn in the world of streaming radiance’), and they read it at DN 24 and DN 27 as well, in the same words.

    And the Pali Text Society reads the locative everywhere else itself. Across its volumes saṁvaṭṭamāne stands eight times (three in Aṅguttara IV, two in Aṅguttara V, two in Dīgha III at the parallels to this passage, one in the Itivuttaka) against the single nominative at Dīgha I 17.27 So this is one word in one volume, against the same edition’s practice everywhere the sentence recurs, not an editorial policy about the construction.

    The second Nikāya instance is not a variant reading at all. Collins offers DN 19, “when Govinda the brahmin died king Disampati grieved”, as a place where some manuscripts have a nominative where others have a locative. Every edition prints the locative (Govinde brāhmaṇe kālaṅkate rājā disampati paridevesi, DN 19 — D ii 231, with kālakate in the PTS and the Buddha Jayanti), and the nominative Govindo brāhmaṇo kālakato is twenty lines away, inside the king’s quoted lament, where it is an ordinary verbless sentence with no absolute about it. Two forms, two functions, and no variation.

    So the summary is short. The nominative absolute is not established for Pāli. Every case offered for it either has a competing reading in another edition or admits an ordinary analysis, and Wijesekera, who wanted it to exist and reached for a plausible explanation in the anacoluthon of long spoken sentences, conceded that “it is not a regular construction in either Pāli or Sanskrit.”28 What a reader meets often, and should not confuse with it, is the semi-absolute nominative with samāna- or santa-: daharo va samāno, ‘while still young’, where the participle does agree with the subject and the temporal sense is real. That is in the Bodhisatta’s going-forth quoted above, four words before the genitive absolute, and telling the two apart in one sentence is as good a test of this chapter as any.

    Conditional sentences

    A conditional has two parts: the clause carrying the condition, called the protasis, and the clause carrying its consequence, the apodosis. Pāli marks the protasis with sace, with the enclitic ce, or with yadi; ce never begins a sentence, because it has no accent of its own.29 The mood of the verbs decides the kind of conditional, not the particle, and there are three kinds.

    A real condition (‘if you listen, you will understand’) puts both verbs in the indicative, present or future, and the apodosis may be an imperative instead.

    Sace te agaru, bhāsassū ti. (DN 21 — D ii 284)

    saceifteto youdat sgagarunot burdensomenom sg, na + garubhāsassuspeak!2 sg imp mid of bhāsatitiend of quotation

    ‘If it is no trouble to you, speak.’

    The protasis there has no verb at all (hoti is understood, as it so often is), and the apodosis is an imperative. That is the ordinary form of a request in the canon.

    A possible condition puts the optative in both clauses. This is much the commonest type in the suttas, and once a reader has met it a few times the pair of optatives becomes the surest signal in the sentence.

    Sace maṁ bhagavā anujāneyya, āgaccheyyāhaṁ imaṁ ambavanaṁ padhānāya. (AN 9.3 — A iv 355)

    saceifmaṁmeacc sgbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sganujāneyyawere to permit3 sg optāgaccheyyaṁI would come1 sg optahaṁIimaṁ ambavanaṁto this mango groven acc sgpadhānāyafor strivingn dat sg

    If the Blessed One were to allow me, I would come to this mango grove to strive.

    Meghiya is floating a request, not reporting a fact, and the two optatives say so. Sace alone would not have: it introduces real conditions too.

    An unreal condition (‘if you had listened, you would have understood’) is the third, and only here does the conditional tense, whose forms the chapter on tenses and moods sets out, have a settled use in the language. The false premise goes into the conditional, usually abhavissa; the consequence goes into the optative. And then, characteristically, the Pāli does not leave the false premise standing: it answers it with yasmā … tasmā, ‘because … therefore’, restoring the true state of affairs.30

    No cetaṁ, bhikkhave, abhavissa ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, nayidha jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyetha. Yasmā ca kho, bhikkhave, atthi ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, tasmā jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyati. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 80)

    no ceif notetaṁthisn nom sgabhavissawere to be3 sg condajātaṁunbornabhūtaṁunbecomeakataṁunmadeasaṅkhataṁunconditionedall n nom sgna idhanot herejātassaof the bornn gen sgnissaraṇaṁescapen nom sgpaññāyethawould be discerned3 sg opt mid of the passive paññāyatiyasmā ca khobut becauseatthithere istasmāthereforepaññāyatiis discerned

    If there were no unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, no escape from the born, become, made and conditioned would be discerned here. But because there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, therefore an escape from the born, become, made and conditioned is discerned.

    Two conditionals, two optatives and two indicatives, and the doctrine rests on the difference between them. The grammar is central to that passage: its whole force is that the first sentence is contrary to fact and the second is not, and only the mood of the verbs says so.

    A relative clause carries a condition too, and most of the Pātimokkha is built that way. A rule states its case as a generic relative with an optative, and its consequence as briefly as the class of offence allows, often in a clause with no verb in it at all. The nuns’ rule about garlic runs to six words:

    Yā pana bhikkhunī lasuṇaṁ khādeyya, pācittiyaṁ. (Bi Pc 1 — Vin iv 259)

    yāwhateverf nom sgpana(filler)bhikkhunīnunf nom sglasuṇaṁgarlicn acc sgkhādeyyashould eat3 sg optpācittiyaṁan offence requiring confessionn nom sg

    “Whatever nun should eat garlic: an offence requiring confession.”

    There is no sace, no ce and no yadi. The optative carries the condition by itself, and the relative carries the generality: yā pana bhikkhunī is any nun there may be, not some particular nun. The consequence then arrives as a single noun, with hoti understood as it was in appamādo amatapadaṁ at the head of this chapter.31 A rule is a conditional with the ‘if’ left to a mood and the ‘then’ left to a case ending.

    A locative absolute can also carry a condition without any particle at all, and Bodhi notes a case where the situation is so unlikely that ‘if’ translates better than ‘when’:

    Āditte, bhikkhave, cele vā sīse vā kimassa karaṇīyan ti? (SN 56.34 — S v 440)

    āditteaablazen loc sg, past partceleacloth, garmentn loc sgvā … vāeither … orsīseaheadn loc sgkiṁwhatassawould be3 sg opt of atthikaraṇīyaṁto be donen nom sg, fpptiend of question

    ‘If one’s turban or one’s head were ablaze, monks, what should be done?’

    The simile, and the sentence that answers it

    The discourses make constant use of similes, and the simile has a fixed shape: seyyathāpi, ‘just as’, opens the first term, and evam eva or evam evaṁ, ‘just so’, opens the second. The verbs in the first term are usually optative, because the case is imagined, not reported.32

    Seyyathāpi, bhante, purisassa agāraṁ ekādasadvāraṁ, so tasmiṁ agāre āditte ekamekenapi dvārena sakkuṇeyya attānaṁ sotthiṁ kātuṁ. (MN 52 — M i 353)

    seyyathāpijust aspurisassaof a manm gen sgagāraṁa housen nom sgekādasadvāraṁwith eleven doorsn nom sg, bahubbīhisohem nom sgtasmiṁ agārethat housen loc sgāditteablazen loc sg, past partekamekena dvārenaby any one doorn ins sgsakkuṇeyyawould be able3 sg optattānaṁhimselfm acc sgsotthiṁ kātuṁto make safe

    Just as, sir, if a man had a house with eleven doors, and that house were ablaze, he would be able to get himself to safety by any one of the doors.

    Three of this chapter’s constructions are in that one sentence: the simile frame, a locative absolute carrying a condition, and an infinitive of purpose. It is also a good measure of how much of the grammar a reader has to have available to follow one ordinary sentence of the canon at reading speed.

    Quotation, and the ti that is not one

    Where English says ‘he knew that the cat was in the house’, Pāli almost always quotes: the thought is given as direct speech and closed with ti.33 The construction belongs with the indeclinables and is treated there; what belongs here are the two things it does at the level of the clause.

    The first is that Pāli does have a real indirect statement, and it is easy to miss because it looks like an ordinary object: a noun or pronoun in the accusative with a participle agreeing with it, the two together forming the object of the main verb. Addasā kho nigrodho paribbājako sandhānaṁ gahapatiṁ dūratova āgacchantaṁ (DN 25 — D iii 37) is not ‘the wanderer Nigrodha saw the householder Sandhāna from afar, coming’, but ‘the wanderer Nigrodha saw that the householder Sandhāna was coming from afar’.34 The participle carries what English would put in a subordinate clause.

    The second is that a ti clause need not be a quotation at all. It can carry purpose: an imperative or optative closed with ti states the aim for the sake of which the main action is done.

    ‘Pasaṁsā me āgacchatū’ ti sīlaṁ rakkheyya paṇḍito. (Iti 76 — It p. 67)

    pasaṁsāpraisef nom sgmeto medat./gen. sgāgacchatulet it come3 sg imptiend of quotationsīlaṁvirtuen acc sgrakkheyyawould guard3 sg optpaṇḍitothe wise onem nom sg

    The wise one would guard his virtue so that praise might come to him.

    Read as a quotation that sentence is nonsense, since nobody is speaking. Read as a purpose clause it is exact, and it is the ordinary Pāli way of saying ‘in order that’.35

    What is left, and where it is

    Four kinds of subordinate clause remain, and all four are built the same way: a relative word in the subordinate clause answered by a demonstrative in the main one. That correlative construction is the next chapter’s subject, and this section is a map, not a treatment.36

    Kind Introduced by Answered by
    Time yadā, yāva, yasmiṁ samaye tadā, tāva, atha, tasmiṁ samaye
    Cause yasmā, yato, yatvādhikaraṇaṁ tasmā, tato
    Result yathā, yena, yatra hi nāma tathā, yāva, sādhu
    Concession api or pi, alone or with ce —

    Three notes on that table, because each hides something a reader will meet.

    Yatvādhikaraṇaṁ looks like a rarity and is not. It occurs eighty-five times in the root texts, and never as a separate word: the canon has yatvādhikaraṇamenaṁ, joined to the pronoun that follows, in the formula of sense restraint. A search for the bare form will suggest it belongs to the grammarians.

    Yatra hi nāma takes a future and carries astonishment, not result. The monks at DN 14, in the conversation this chapter quoted for the genitive absolute, exclaim at the Buddha’s power of recollection: yatra hi nāma tathāgato atīte buddhe parinibbute … jātitopi anussarissati (DN 14 — D ii 8), ‘that the Realised One should recollect, of past Buddhas who have attained final Nibbāna, even their birth’. The future there is not future time, and there is a locative absolute inside it.37

    And the concessive clause has no conjunction at all. It is marked by pi or api attached to a word in a clause whose verb is usually a participle: Chinnopi rukkho punareva rūhati (Dhp 338 — Dhp p. 49), ‘though the tree is cut, it grows again’. The relation is left for the reader to supply, which is the habit this whole chapter has been describing.

    Where clause syntax is treated elsewhere

    The particles (na and mā and the moods they take, ca and vā and pi, the interrogatives nu kho, kacci, nanu and api nu, and iti with its sandhi) are each treated in the chapter on indeclinables, where their forms and their enclitic behaviour belong. This chapter has added only their effect on a whole clause.

    The relative correlatives (the whole ya-/ta- apparatus, and the adjectival clause built with yo … so) are the next chapter’s, and it treats them with paradigms.

    The participles and the absolutive, whose forms every construction here depends on, are in the chapters on participles and on absolutives and infinitives. This chapter uses them and derives none of them; where it names a form as a present participle or a past passive one, that is the claim those chapters establish.

    The cases themselves are in the chapter on case syntax, which left the absolutes here on purpose, on the ground that an absolute construction is a dependent clause and a reader meets it as one. It kept the observation this chapter has spent its middle third unfolding: these constructions are named after their case because only the case marks them off, and when the case will not decide, no other word in the sentence will.

    Where to start in a long sentence

    Begin with the finite verbs. They are few, and each is a place the sentence could stop. Then take every absolutive as an action finished before the next, by the same subject, and every participle standing in an oblique case with its own noun as a clause of its own, joined to the rest by nothing but that case. Where no finite verb is printed, supply hoti and see whether the sentence closes. Every remaining word is read against one of these. The next chapter ends by doing that to a single sentence, word by word.
