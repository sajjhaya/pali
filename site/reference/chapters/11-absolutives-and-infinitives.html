    Chapter 11

    Absolutives and Infinitives

    A brahmin farmer, meeting the Buddha at the edge of his field, tells him to go and earn his food as everyone else does:

    Ahaṁ kho, samaṇa, kasāmi ca vapāmi ca, kasitvā ca vapitvā ca bhuñjāmi. (SN 7.11 — S i 172)

    ahaṁInom sgkhoindeedsamaṇaasceticm voc sgkasāmiI plough1 sg prescaandvapāmiI sow1 sg preskasitvāhaving ploughedabsvapitvāhaving sownabsbhuñjāmiI eat1 sg pres

    “I, ascetic, plough and sow, and having ploughed and sown, I eat.”

    The first half of that sentence is two finite verbs joined by ca, which says something at the outset: Pāli can coordinate finite verbs, and does. The second half is the same two actions said again in the form Pāli reaches for far more readily: two indeclinable forms leading into a third, not two more finite verbs. That form is the absolutive (pubbakiriyā, ‘the prior action’), also called the gerund, and it names an action completed before the finite verb it depends on.1

    The preference is strong enough to shape how a sutta sentence looks on the page. Long stretches of narrative in the canon consist of a chain of absolutives with a single finite verb at the end, and the practical consequence for a reader is a reading strategy, not a rule to memorise: when a sentence will not resolve, find the finite verb first (it is usually last) and read everything in front of it as a sequence of completed actions leading up to it.

    The absolutive

    An absolutive takes no ending for case, gender, number or person. It is an indeclinable, and so it cannot agree with anything; it modifies the action of the clause instead of describing any noun in it. Oberlies counts eight suffixes that form it, of which two account for nearly everything a reader will meet; the remainder are in Appendix A.4.2

    The -tvā type

    The commonest suffix by a wide margin is -tvā, joined to the root or the present stem, often through a connecting -i-:

    Form From Meaning
    katvā / karitvā karoti having done
    gantvā gacchati having gone
    datvā / daditvā dadāti / deti having given
    vatvā vadati having said
    sutvā / suṇitvā suṇāti having heard
    hitvā / jahitvā jahati3 having abandoned

    Many verbs offer two absolutives (katvā beside karitvā, sutvā beside suṇitvā), and the choice between them carries no difference in meaning. The longer variant -tvāna belongs almost entirely to verse, and the formula introducing a sutta’s closing stanzas puts it there:

    Idaṁ vatvāna sugato athāparaṁ etadavoca satthā. (DN 16 — D ii 90)

    idaṁthisn acc sgvatvānahaving saidabssugatothe Well-Gone Onem nom sgathathenaparaṁfurtheretadthisn acc sgavocasaid3 sg aorsatthāthe Teacherm nom sg

    “Having said this, the Well-Gone One, the Teacher, then said this further.”

    The -(i)ya type

    A second suffix, -ya, attaches mostly, though not only, to verbs carrying a prefix. Where the root ends in a long ā, the suffix simply follows it; where it ends in a consonant or a short vowel, the resulting cluster assimilates, and the outcome is a doubled consonant that can look nothing like a suffix at all:

    Form From Meaning
    āgamma āgacchati having come
    upādāya upādiyati having taken up
    pahāya pajahati having abandoned
    paccakkhāya paccakkhāti (paṭi + ā + √khā) having renounced
    samatikkamma samatikkamati having passed beyond
    upasampajja upasampajjati having entered upon
    pecca peti (pa + eti) having died

    The last of these shows the assimilation at its most opaque: pa + i + tya gives pecca, in which nothing of the suffix survives as a recognisable syllable.4 Keep pecca apart from paṭicca, which the footnote names beside it: the two differ only in their prefix and are otherwise the same formation from the same root, so pecca is pa- ‘onward’ (one has gone on, and Sanskrit writes it pretya), while paṭicca is paṭi- ‘back’ and belongs to pacceti, Sanskrit pratītya.5 The reduplication that a present stem may carry does not transfer (pajahati has pahāya, built on the bare root), the behaviour Chapter 10 noted for the gerundive pahātabba.

    Either type negates with a-, becoming an- before a vowel, as the participle does in Chapter 10. The first rule of the Pātimokkha turns on two of them in a row, one of each type:

    Yo pana bhikkhu bhikkhūnaṁ sikkhāsājīvasamāpanno sikkhaṁ apaccakkhāya dubbalyaṁ anāvikatvā methunaṁ dhammaṁ paṭiseveyya … pārājiko hoti asaṁvāso. (Bu Pj 1 — Vin iii 23)

    yo pana bhikkhuwhatever monkm nom sgbhikkhūnaṁof the monksm gen plsikkhāsājīvasamāpannohaving undertaken the training and way of lifem nom sgsikkhaṁthe trainingf acc sgapaccakkhāyanot having renouncedabs, negateddubbalyaṁhis weaknessn acc sganāvikatvānot having disclosedabs, negatedmethunaṁ dhammaṁsexual intercoursem acc sgpaṭiseveyyashould engage in3 sg optpārājikoexpelledm nom sghotiisasaṁvāsoexcluded from the communitym nom sg

    “Whatever monk, having undertaken the monks’ training and way of life, without having renounced the training and without having disclosed his weakness, engages in sexual intercourse … is expelled and excluded from the community.”

    Apaccakkhāya is the negation of the paccakkhāya in the table above, and anāvikatvā of āvikaroti ‘makes clear’.6 The negation gives the rule its whole force: the two absolutives describe two things the monk could have done and did not, not things he did. A negated absolutive names the road not taken, and here it is the road that would have made the act no offence at all, since a monk who renounces the training first is no longer bound by it.

    Whose action is it?

    In most sentences the absolutive’s unexpressed agent is the subject of the main verb, and a chain of them shares that one subject throughout. The formula closing a teaching encounter is the standard case:

    Atha kho bhagavā soṇadaṇḍaṁ brāhmaṇaṁ dhammiyā kathāya sandassetvā samādapetvā samuttejetvā sampahaṁsetvā uṭṭhāyāsanā pakkāmīti. (DN 4 — D i 126)

    atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgsoṇadaṇḍaṁ brāhmaṇaṁthe brahmin Soṇadaṇḍam acc sgdhammiyā kathāyawith a talk on the Dhammaf ins sgsandassetvāhaving instructedabssamādapetvāhaving rousedabssamuttejetvāhaving inspiredabssampahaṁsetvāhaving gladdenedabsuṭṭhāyahaving risenabsāsanāfrom the seatn abl sgpakkāmideparted3 sg aorti(quotation)

    “Then the Blessed One, having instructed, roused, inspired and gladdened the brahmin Soṇadaṇḍa with a talk on the Dhamma, rose from his seat and departed.”

    Five absolutives, one aorist. Every one of the five is the Buddha’s action, and the sentence does not need to say so once.

    The rule that follows from this, that the absolutive’s agent is the main verb’s subject, is the one most pedagogical grammars give, and it is right about the great majority of sentences. It is not, however, exceptionless, and the Pāli commentators noticed the exception and gave it a name. Consider the sentence that closes the description of the liberated person:

    Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sabbaso nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṁ samatikkamma saññāvedayitanirodhaṁ upasampajja viharati. Paññāya cassa disvā āsavā parikkhīṇā honti. (MN 25 — M i 160)

    puna caparaṁfurthermorebhikkhavemonksm voc plbhikkhua monkm nom sgsabbasoentirelyadvnevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṁthe dimension of neither perception nor non-perceptionn acc sgsamatikkammahaving passed beyondabssaññāvedayitanirodhaṁthe cessation of perception and feelingm acc sgupasampajjahaving entered uponabsviharatidwells3 sg prespaññāyawith wisdomf ins sgcaandassafor him, hism gen./dat. sgdisvāhaving seenabsāsavāthe defilementsm nom plparikkhīṇāexhaustedm nom pl pphontiare3 pl pres

    “Furthermore, monks, a monk, having passed entirely beyond the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, having entered the cessation of perception and feeling, dwells there. And having seen with wisdom, his defilements are exhausted.”

    The first sentence is ordinary: samatikkamma and upasampajja both belong to bhikkhu, the subject of viharati. The second is not. The subject of honti is āsavā, ‘the defilements’, and the defilements do not do the seeing. The seer is the monk, present in the sentence only as assa, a form that is genitive and dative alike and cannot be told apart here.7

    The tradition analysed this directly. Dhammapāla, commenting on the Visuddhimagga, sets out the ordinary construction and then turns to this one with the phrase asamāne pana kattari, “but when the agent is not the same”, and gives two illustrations: the sentence above, and a made-up one that the grammatical tradition passes from hand to hand, pisācaṁ disvā bhayaṁ hoti, “having seen a goblin, fear arises.”8 The example is well chosen, because in English the mismatch passes unnoticed. Fear does not see the goblin. A person does, and the fear results.

    A second commentary is blunter still. Glossing the same line, the Pañcapakaraṇa-mūlaṭīkā says outright: na āsavā paññāya passanti, “the defilements do not see with wisdom”, and explains that they are called ‘exhausted having seen’ because their exhaustion depends on the seeing.9 Hold on to that last point, because it shows something the grammar alone would not. Here the absolutive marks why, and not merely what happened first. The commentaries gloss disvā in this construction with dassanahetu, ‘by reason of seeing’, and set beside it a standing pair of illustrations: sappiṁ pivitvā balaṁ hoti, sīhaṁ disvā bhayaṁ hoti, “having drunk ghee, strength arises; having seen a lion, fear arises.”10 Neither strength nor fear performs the action in front of it. Both are its result.

    So the rule has two halves. Ordinarily the absolutive’s agent is the subject of the main verb, and to assume so is right nearly every time. Where the main clause’s subject cannot plausibly have performed the action (where it is an abstract noun like bhayaṁ or āsavā, and the sentence describes a consequence, not a sequence), the agent is someone else, usually named in the genitive or dative, and the absolutive gives a cause.

    The absolutive can also carry the weight of a conditional clause, with no verb in the conditional mood anywhere in the sentence:

    Mañhi, bhante, aññatitthiyā sāvakaṁ labhitvā kevalakappaṁ nāḷandaṁ paṭākaṁ parihareyyuṁ. (MN 56 — M i 379)

    maṁmeacc sghiindeedbhantevenerable sirm voc sgaññatitthiyāfollowers of another sectm nom plsāvakaṁas a disciplem acc sglabhitvāhaving gainedabskevalakappaṁthe whole ofadj, acc sgnāḷandaṁNāḷandāf acc sgpaṭākaṁa bannern acc sgparihareyyuṁthey would carry around3 pl opt

    “For if followers of another sect were to gain me as a disciple, venerable sir, they would carry a banner around the whole of Nāḷandā.”

    Chapter 8 gave the optative as the ordinary vehicle for the apodosis, ‘the “then” half’, of a conditional sentence. Here the protasis, the “if” half, has no verb of its own at all; an absolutive stands in for it.

    Absolutives that have stopped being verbs

    A number of absolutives have hardened into other parts of speech, and are best learned as vocabulary, not parsed each time. The largest group functions as postpositions, governing a case of its own:

    Form From Meaning
    uddissa uddisati for, on account of (+ acc.)
    paṭicca pacceti dependent on, because of (+ acc.)
    ārabbha ārabhati concerning, about (+ acc.)
    nissāya / upanissāya nissayati depending on, close to (+ acc.)
    upādāya upādiyati out of, in consequence of
    vivicca viviccati secluded from
    ṭhapetvā ṭhapeti except for, apart from (+ acc.)

    Cakkhuñca paṭicca rūpe ca uppajjati viññāṇaṁ, cakkhuviññāṇantveva saṅkhyaṁ gacchati. (MN 38 — M i 259)

    cakkhuṁthe eyen acc sgcaandpaṭiccadependent onpostpos + accrūpeformsn acc pluppajjatiarises3 sg presviññāṇaṁconsciousnessn nom sgcakkhuviññāṇaṁeye-consciousnessn nom sgtubutevajustsaṅkhyaṁreckoningf acc sggacchatigoes3 sg pres

    “Dependent on the eye and forms, consciousness arises; it is reckoned as just eye-consciousness.”

    Paṭicca is the most consequential word in this list. It is an absolutive used as a preposition, and it names the doctrine on which the whole of the canon’s causal analysis is built: paṭiccasamuppāda, dependent origination.

    Ṭhapetvā, literally ‘having set aside’, has become the ordinary word for except, and it is how the Vinaya’s definitions carve out what they do not cover:

    Ṭhapetvā gāmañca gāmūpacārañca avasesaṁ araññaṁ nāma. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)

    ṭhapetvāexcept forpostpos + accgāmaṁan inhabited aream acc sgcaandgāmūpacāraṁthe access to an inhabited aream acc sgcaandavasesaṁthe remaindern nom sgaraññaṁwildernessn nom sgnāmais called

    “Except for an inhabited area and its access, the remainder is called ‘wilderness’.”

    Nothing in that sentence is being set anywhere. The verb has given up its own sense and kept only the case it governs, which is the process the whole table records.

    A second group has become adverbs outright. Sakkaccaṁ ‘respectfully, carefully’ (112 root-text occurrences) is the absolutive of sakkaroti, and asañcicca ‘unintentionally’ (98) is the absolutive of a negated sañceteti. Neither is felt as a verb any longer.

    The second matters in the Vinaya, and a rule read together with its exemption shows why. The rule against killing runs Yo pana bhikkhu sañcicca pāṇaṁ jīvitā voropeyya, pācittiyaṁ (Bu Pc 61 — Vin iv 124), ‘whatever monk should intentionally deprive a living being of life: an offence entailing confession’. The list of exemptions that follows it opens Anāpatti — asañcicca (Bu Pc 61), ‘there is no offence: unintentionally’. One negated absolutive makes the difference between an offence and none, and it is an adverb in both places, agreeing with nothing and governing nothing. Oberlies notes that the process can go one step further and yield an ordinary noun: upanidhā ‘comparison’ is a feminine noun abstracted from the absolutive upanidhāya.11

    The infinitive

    The infinitive names the bare idea of a verb, with no indication of when it happens: English “to do.” Like the absolutive it is indeclinable, and the two share an origin: both are frozen case-forms of an old action noun in -tu-, the infinitive from its accusative or dative, the absolutive from its instrumental. The commonest suffix is -tuṁ:

    Form From Meaning
    kātuṁ karoti to do
    gantuṁ gacchati to go
    desetuṁ deseti to teach
    bhāvetuṁ bhāveti to develop
    sotuṁ suṇāti to hear

    One consequence of that shared origin shows on the page. When an infinitive is compounded with kāma- ‘wanting’, it drops its final -ṁ: daṭṭhuṁ ‘to see’ plus kāma gives daṭṭhukāmo, ‘wanting to see’. The pattern is very productive (the root texts carry 138 distinct words ending in -ukāmo), and recognising it on sight matters, because if you meet sotukāmo or gantukāmo and look for a stem sotuka- you will not find one.12

    Pāli reaches for the infinitive less often than English reaches for “to,” because the cases already cover much of the same ground. The dative of an action noun in -āya serves the same purpose, and so, less often, does the accusative; Ñāṇatusita lists the dative among the infinitive formations outright.13 Chapter 15 takes up the overlap.

    Infinitive syntax

    An infinitive ordinarily shares its agent with the main verb, and usually precedes it. It attaches most naturally to a predicate of ability, worthiness or ease, and those predicates are frequently impersonal, with no grammatical subject at all. Where that happens, the agent appears in the instrumental, even though the infinitive itself is read actively:

    Na dāni sukaraṁ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke pariccajituṁ. (MN 76 — M i 524)

    nanotdāninowsukaraṁeasyn nom sgamhehiby us1 pl inslābhasakkārasilokegain, honour and famem acc plpariccajituṁto give upinf

    “It is not easy for us now to give up gain, honour and fame.”

    There is no nominative anywhere in that sentence for amhehi to compete with, and that makes room for it: the instrumental marks the one by whom the giving-up would be done. It is the case, with the same function, that Chapter 9 gave for the agent of a passive, appearing here beside an infinitive that is not passive at all.

    The contrast between the two forms this chapter has treated is easiest to see where the canon sets them side by side. A monk considers giving up the training:

    Yannūnāhaṁ sikkhaṁ paccakkhāya hīnāyāvattitvā bhoge ca bhuñjeyyaṁ puññāni ca kareyyanti. (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)

    yaṁ nūnawhat ifahaṁInom sgsikkhaṁthe trainingf acc sgpaccakkhāyahaving renouncedabshīnāyato the lowern dat sgāvattitvāhaving returnedabsbhogepossessionsm acc plcaandbhuñjeyyaṁI might enjoy1 sg optpuññānimeritsn acc plkareyyaṁI might make1 sg optti(quotation)

    “What if I, having renounced the training and returned to the lower life, were to enjoy possessions and make merit?”

    The Buddha’s reply, a few lines earlier in the same discourse, uses the same two verbs and puts neither in the optative:

    Sakkā bhoge ca bhuñjituṁ puññāni ca kātuṁ. (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)

    sakkāit is possibleindeclbhogepossessionsm acc plcaandbhuñjituṁto enjoyinfpuññānimeritsn acc plkātuṁto makeinf

    “It is possible to enjoy possessions and to make merit.”

    Two absolutives and two optatives in the first; two infinitives and no finite verb at all in the second.14 The monk frames a course of action he might take and wants; the answer states, flatly, what can be done. Between them they use most of what this chapter contains.

Daṭṭhuṁ belongs to Oberlies’s third absolutive suffix, -(t)tu(ṁ), which also supplies the bare absolutive daṭṭhu ‘having seen’: one more sign of how close the two categories are.↩︎

Oberlies §57, p. 264, gives one example of each, glossing them ‘go out in order to see …’ (accusative) and na ca mayaṁ labhāma bhagavantaṁ dassanāya, “and we did not get an opportunity to see the Blessed One” (dative).↩︎

    14. Sakkā is an indeclinable meaning ‘it is possible’, historically an old optative, and it takes an infinitive to complete it. It carries no person or number of its own, so the sentence has no finite verb.↩︎
