    Chapter 7

    Verbs: The Present System

    A finite verb is one that inflects for person: it has separate forms for ‘I’, ‘you’, ‘he’. Everything else in the verbal system is infinite: the participles, which inflect for gender, case and number but not for person, and the absolutives and infinitives, which do not inflect at all. Those are Part IV’s. This part is about the forms that carry a person, and this chapter is about the three of them that are built on one stem.

    How much of the Old Indic verb Pāli has let go is the first thing to establish, because a reader arriving from Sanskrit will otherwise spend some time looking for things that are not there. The dual is gone completely. The subjunctive and the precative are gone from the moods.1 The middle voice survives only in scattered forms, and its old work has largely passed to the causative. The perfect has been reduced to two or three stray forms in the whole canon.2 What is left is a system a reader can hold in the head: three tenses (present, past and future) and four moods, of which the indicative, the imperative and the optative are formed on the present stem and belong here.

    The present stem

    Almost everything in this part follows from one fact, and it is a fact about Pāli, not about how Pāli is taught. In Old Indic the root was the centre of the verb and every form was derived from it. In Pāli the root has lost that place. Oberlies states it clearly: “The focus of the verbal conjugation is no longer the root but the present stem, i.e. the 3sg. of the indicative without the ending -ti. All regular verb forms are based on it; forms that are not so constructed are chiefly historical relics.”3

    That gives the reader a procedure, not a fact to memorise. A dictionary gives a verb in its third person singular, gacchati ‘goes’. Remove the -ti and what is left is the stem, gaccha-, and the stem is what every form in this chapter is built on:

    Dictionary form Stem Gloss
    gacchati gaccha- goes
    deseti dese- teaches
    karoti karo- does, makes
    jānāti jānā- knows
    suṇāti suṇā- hears

    Look down the stem column and one feature is immediately visible: the stems end either in a short -a or in a long vowel. A reader needs no other division. Oberlies distinguishes two conjugations on this basis: one, much the larger, with the stem in -a; the other with the stem in a long vowel, “most commonly -e, fairly often -ā, rarely -ī or -o”.4 The rare -ū type is a handful of forms of one verb, brūti ‘says’, which a reader meets often enough in verse to recognise and never needs to conjugate.

    Why the stems differ: vowel gradation

    The stems do not differ arbitrarily, and two mechanisms account for nearly all of it. The first is vowel gradation: the systematic lengthening of a root vowel that runs through the whole of Pāli word-formation, nominal as well as verbal.5 A root vowel appears in one of three grades:

    Zero grade Guṇa grade Vuddhi grade
    i, ī e, ay āy, e
    u, ū o, av āv, o
    a (or none) a ā

    The guṇa ‘quality’ grade adds an a to the vowel; the vuddhi ‘growth’ grade adds another. So the root bhū- ‘be’ in the guṇa grade is bhav-, giving bhavati ‘is’; jut- ‘shine’ gives jotati ‘shines’; nī- ‘lead’ gives nayati ‘leads’. Push the same roots to the vuddhi grade and they yield the causatives of Chapter 9: bhāveti ‘develops’, literally ‘causes to be’, and kāreti ‘makes another do’. The vowel a is already at the guṇa grade and so does not change there, and so the largest class of verbs shows no gradation at all.6

    Why the stems differ: the conjugation classes

    The second mechanism is that most verbs insert something between the root and the ending. The tradition calls these class affixes vikaraṇa, and sorts every root in the language by which one it takes. Suṇāti has its long -ā- because the root su- ‘hear’ takes the affix -ṇā-; jhāyati has its -ya-; muñcati has a nasal pushed inside the root itself.

    Class Name Affix Example
    1 bhūvādigaṇa -a- pacati ‘cooks’
    2 rudhādigaṇa nasal infix + -a- muñcati ‘releases’
    3 divādigaṇa -ya- jhāyati ‘meditates, burns’
    4 svādigaṇa -ṇu-, -ṇā-, -uṇā- suṇāti ‘hears’
    5 kiyādigaṇa -nā- jānāti ‘knows’
    6 gahādigaṇa -ppa-, -nhā- gaṇhāti ‘takes’
    7 tanādigaṇa -o-, -yirā- karoti ‘does’
    8 curādigaṇa -e-, -aya- deseti ‘teaches’

    The grammars do not agree on how many classes there are, and the disagreement instructs more than it troubles. Kaccāyana and the Saddanīti give the eight above, each with its own rule; the Padarūpasiddhi folds the sixth into the fifth and gets seven; Perniola gives ten, because he follows the Sanskrit division and says so (“the Pali verbal system is directly connected with the Sanskrit verbal system; hence we can follow the Sanskrit division of the verbs into classes”); Warder arrives at seven on a different cut again.7 They count the same forms and divide them at different joints, as happens whenever a classification is imposed on a continuum that has been eroding for centuries. This book does not depend on the number.

    What the classes are for, in a book about reading, is to stop a reader being surprised. Suṇāti, jānāti, karoti and deseti look nothing like each other and behave identically once the stem is isolated. The class explains the stem; the stem is what carries the endings; the endings are the same for all of them.

    Voice

    Before the paradigms, one distinction that Appendix A.3 presents in a second column and that the reader will otherwise take for a misprint.

    Pāli inherits two sets of personal endings. The tradition calls them parassapada, ‘the word for another’, and attanopada, ‘the word for oneself’: what Western grammars call the active and the middle.8 In principle the middle marks an action that returns to the agent, done for oneself instead of passing to something else. In practice it barely survives. Oberlies says the middle “survives only in some forms” and that its function has been partly taken over by the causative; Duroiselle is blunter, and describes what a reader will find:

    It must here be remarked that the Reflective Voice has lost very much of its importance, and that the distinction between Active and Reflective has been almost if not altogether effaced, and that the choice between the Active or Reflective is mostly determined now by metrical exigencies.

    So the middle is largely a verse form, chosen for its sound, not its sense, and the reader’s practical need is only to recognise it. A third singular in -te where -ti was expected, a first plural in -mhe or -māse, a third plural in -re: these are middle endings, not errors.

    Pare ca na vijānanti, mayamettha yamāmase. (MN 128 — M iii 154)

    pareothersm nom plcaandnanotvijānantiunderstandmayaṁweetthahereyamāmaselet us restrain ourselves1 pl imp middle9

    Others do not understand that here we must restrain ourselves.

    Whatever yamāmase means (and its grammar is settled where its sense is not), it is the middle voice, first person plural, and it is a verse line, which is where such forms live.10 The same verse is Dhammapada 6, and whoever has met it in either place has met the attanopada, named or not.

    There is a second distinction that is easy to run together with this one and must not be. Voice in the sense of active against passive is carried by the stem, not by the endings. A passive stem is made with an affix -ya- and then takes whatever personal endings it needs, active-looking ones included. That is Chapter 9‘s subject; what matters here is that vuccati ’is called’ has an ordinary-looking -ti and is passive, so the endings alone will never tell a reader whether a verb is active or passive.11

    The present indicative

    The endings, active and middle, are these:

    Person Active Middle
    1 sg. -mi -e
    2 sg. -si -se
    3 sg. -ti -te
    1 pl. -ma -mhe, -mase
    2 pl. -tha -vhe
    3 pl. -nti -nte, -re

    Perniola calls these the primary endings, and the term is useful across the whole of Part III: the primary endings serve the present indicative and the future, the secondary endings serve the aorist and the conditional, the optative takes both, and the imperative has its own.12 Hold that rule and the future in the next chapter is simply the present endings on a different stem, not a new paradigm to learn.

    Attached to a stem in -a, the active endings give:

    singular plural
    1st gacchāmi ‘I go’ gacchāma ‘we go’
    2nd gacchasi ‘you go’ gacchatha ‘you go’
    3rd gacchati ‘he, she, it goes’ gacchanti ‘they go’

    Two changes to the stem vowel are visible there, and neither is arbitrary. The stem vowel lengthens before -mi and -ma, so the two first-person forms have gacchā- where the rest have gaccha-.13 And in the third plural the stem vowel is absorbed into the ending, so that a stem in a long vowel appears with a short one: jānāti but jānanti, suṇāti but suṇanti. These alternations are a rule and not an accident of the verbs, and it applies everywhere in the chapter, the imperative and the optative included.

    The three other stem types take the same endings without further comment:

    dese- karo- jānā-
    1 sg. desemi karomi jānāmi
    2 sg. desesi karosi jānāsi
    3 sg. deseti karoti jānāti
    1 pl. desema karoma jānāma
    2 pl. desetha karotha jānātha
    3 pl. desenti karonti jānanti

    Because each person has its own ending, the personal pronoun is not needed and is usually absent. Dhammaṁ desenti is ‘they teach the Dhamma’ with no te in it; the -nti has already said ‘they’. When a pronoun does appear it adds something (contrast, or emphasis of the kind set out in Chapter 5), and it should be noticed, not translated away.

    What the present tense does

    The present indicative states a fact, and its plainest use is what its name suggests: something is the case now. English has to choose between ‘he teaches’ and ‘he is teaching’; Pāli does not, and either may be the right rendering.14

    Beyond that it does two things a reader must be ready for. The first is to state what is always the case. A general truth has no particular time, and Pāli puts it in the present:

    Jātassa maraṇaṁ hoti, jāto dukkhāni phussati; (SN 5.6 — S i 132)

    jātassafor one who is bornm gen sgmaraṇaṁdeathn nom sghotiisjātoone bornm nom sgdukkhānisufferingsn acc plphussatiencounters

    For one who is born there is death; one born encounters sufferings.

    No action in that line is happening at the moment of speaking. Hoti means ‘is the case’, not ‘is dying now’, and the sentence reaches into the future (for one who is born there will be death) without any future form in it.

    The second is the historic present, and it is very common. Pāli narrates past events in the present tense, where English can do so only in a consciously colloquial register.15 Every sutta opens this way:

    ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā kurūsu viharati kammāsadhammaṁ nāma kurūnaṁ nigamo. (MN 10 — M i 55)

    ekaṁ samayaṁat one timebhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgkurūsuamong the Kurusm loc plviharatidwellskammāsadhammaṁ nāmanamed Kammāsadhammakurūnaṁof the Kurusm gen plnigamoa townm nom sg

    At one time the Blessed One was dwelling among the Kurus, at a town of the Kurus named Kammāsadhamma.

    The verb is present and the event is centuries past. To render viharati as ‘dwells’ here is an error of English, not of grammar; but to conclude from the tense that the Buddha is described as presently resident is to misread the sentence.

    The imperative

    The imperative commands, and it also invites, blesses, permits and entreats: a range much wider than the English term suggests. Its endings go on the same present stem:

    Person Active Middle
    1 sg. -mi -e
    2 sg. -hi, or the bare stem -ssu
    3 sg. -tu -taṁ
    1 pl. -ma, -mu -āmase, -mhase
    2 pl. -tha -vho
    3 pl. -ntu -ntaṁ, -ruṁ

    Three of the active endings are identical to the present indicative’s (the first person singular and plural and the second plural), so gacchāma is ‘we go’ or ‘let us go’ and only the context decides.16 On the stem gaccha-:

    singular plural
    1st gacchāmi ‘let me go’ gacchāma ‘let us go’
    2nd gacchāhi, gaccha ‘go!’ gacchatha ‘go!’
    3rd gacchatu ‘let him go’ gacchantu ‘let them go’

    Look at the second person singular. It has two forms: the ending -hi with the stem vowel lengthened before it, and the bare stem with no ending at all. Both are ordinary; gaccha and gacchāhi are the same command.17 Only stems in -a take the bare form, because otherwise nothing would distinguish it from the stem itself.

    The commonest use is the plain one, and the Buddha’s most quoted instruction to enquirers is a string of them:

    Etha tumhe, kālāmā, mā anussavena, mā paramparāya … mā samaṇo no garūti. (AN 3.65 — A i 189)

    ethacome!2 pl imptumheyounom plkālāmāKālāmasvoc plmādo notanussavenaby oral transmissionm ins sgparamparāyaby lineagef ins sgsamaṇothe asceticm nom sgnoourgaruteachertiend-quote

    Come, Kālāmas: do not go by oral transmission, nor by lineage … nor by thinking ‘the ascetic is our teacher’.

    The third person is where the range widens, because a third-person imperative in English needs a paraphrase (‘let him’, ‘may he’), and the Pāli carries no such politeness marker of its own. It covers instruction, invitation, blessing and entreaty alike, and who is speaking to whom decides which is meant.18 The best-known instance in the canon is a benediction:

    Sukhino va khemino hontu, sabbasattā bhavantu sukhitattā. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 25)19

    sukhinohappym nom plvaindeedkheminosafem nom plhontulet them be3 pl impsabba·sattāall beingsm nom plbhavantulet them be3 pl impsukhit·attāhappy in themselvesm nom pl

    May they be happy and safe; may all beings be happy in themselves.

    Two imperatives in one line, hontu and bhavantu, from the two stems of the same verb ‘to be’. Neither is a command to anyone. The mood is the same one that orders a monk to go; the sense is entirely from the setting.

    The singular is at its most formal in the Vinaya, where every act of the Sangha opens with the same four words:

    Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196)

    suṇātulet it listen3 sg impmeto me1 sg datbhantevenerable sirsvocsaṅghothe Sangham nom sg

    “Let the Sangha listen to me, venerable sirs.”

    No command is given here either, and none could be: a monk has no standing to order the Sangha. The sentence is a request for the floor, and it opens every kammavācā, ‘act-statement’, in the Vinaya: the formula by which a community does anything formally at all.20 A nun’s version differs in one word, ayye for bhante. So in two examples the third person has moved from benediction to formal request with no change in the form, the point this section makes and the reason the ten senses are a list, not a paradigm.

    The optative

    The optative, also called the potential, covers what may, might, could, would or should happen. Its endings are added to the present stem, and unlike the imperative’s they are unmistakable, because every one of them carries -eyy- or its contraction:

    Person Active Middle
    1 sg. -eyyaṁ, -e -eyyaṁ
    2 sg. -eyyāsi, -e -etho
    3 sg. -eyya, -e -etha
    1 pl. -eyyāma -emase
    2 pl. -eyyātha -eyyavho
    3 pl. -eyyuṁ -eraṁ

    On the stem gaccha- these give gaccheyyaṁ ‘I might go’, gaccheyyāsi, gaccheyya, gaccheyyāma, gaccheyyātha, gaccheyyuṁ. The short forms in -e alongside the full ones in -eyya- are contractions of the same ending and mean the same thing; the tradition also records an optative in -yā, which survives in a few common verbs: vajjā ‘he would say’, dajjā ‘he would give’.21

    The optative’s commonest job in the canon is the conditional clause, where it is regularly preceded by sace, ce or yadi, all meaning ‘if’:

    Sace maṁ, bhante, evaṁ puccheyyuṁ … (DN 9 — D i 200)

    saceifmaṁmeacc sgbhantevenerable sirvocevaṁthuspuccheyyuṁthey should ask3 pl optative

    If they were to ask me thus, venerable sir …

    Its second use is the polite or provisional statement, and in the Buddha’s speech the mood is used most this way: a rule stated as what one should do, an enquiry framed as what one might find. The Kālāma Sutta puts an optative directly after the imperatives quoted above, and the shift is the point of the passage: having told them what not to rely on, the Buddha moves to what they may come to know for themselves:

    Yadā tumhe, kālāmā, attanāva jāneyyātha … (AN 3.65 — A i 189)

    yadāwhentumheyounom plkālāmāKālāmasvoc plattanāby oneselfm ins sgvajust, onlyjāneyyāthayou should know2 pl optative

    But when you come to know for yourselves …

    One caution, against seeing a distinction that is not there. The imperative and the optative overlap in use to the point that Oberlies calls them “syntactically often exchangeable”.22 A wish may appear in either; a mild instruction may appear in either. The forms are always distinguishable and the senses frequently are not, so having parsed gaccheyya correctly as an optative you still have to decide from the context whether it is ‘he might go’, ‘he should go’ or simply ‘let him go’.

    Atthi and hoti

    One verb is irregular enough in the present to be learnt whole, and it is among the most frequent words in the canon. Atthi ‘is, exists’ does not take a stem in the way the verbs above do; the endings attach to the root itself, with the results that Old Indic left behind:

    singular plural
    1st asmi, amhi ‘I am’ amha ‘we are’
    2nd asi ‘you are’ attha ‘you are’
    3rd atthi ‘he, she, it is; there is’ santi ‘they are’

    Its imperative is atthu, santu, and its optative is siyā or assa, plural siyuṁ or assu, the last of these among the most common verb forms in the whole canon, since a very large part of the Buddha’s conditional and hypothetical speech runs through it.23

    Two practical notes about this paradigm, because the table above is tidier than the page a reader will meet. The first-person forms are usually printed joined to the word before them. Standing alone, asmi occurs just once in the root texts; joined, it is everywhere: ahamasmi ‘I am’ forty-five times, and asmī’ti sixty-five.24 Search a text for asmi, find nothing, and you have met a fact about how the editions set sandhi, not about the language.

    And the doublets the handbooks print beside these are real, although they do not occur as words of their own. The first singular is often given as asmi or amhi, the first plural as amha or amhā. Amhi and amhā do not occur as words in the root texts at all, as one would expect of a pair of grammarians’ constructions. They are hidden by the sandhi just described, and hidden more completely, because a joined amhi gives up its own initial vowel and leaves only -mhi behind. Kho + amhi gives khomhi ‘I am indeed’, which stands in the root texts a hundred and thirty-four times; na + amhi gives nāmhi ‘I am not’, which is how the Buddha asks to be left undisturbed for a fortnight.25 So the first person singular has two living forms, not one. The first plural does not, and there the handbooks are nearly right: amhā has two occurrences in the whole of the root texts against amha’s eight, and is the rarer word by a long way.

    The other common verb to name here is hoti ‘is, becomes’, a contraction of bhavati, which supplies most of the ordinary copula and whose imperative hotu ‘so be it’ a reader meets constantly. Hoti and bhavati say that A is B; atthi says that A exists, or that there is an A. The distinction is not absolute, but it is the safe assumption.

    Beyond these, a minority of verbs are irregular in the present and a much larger number are irregular in the past. Since almost all of that irregularity falls in the aorist, the irregular verbs are gathered in the next chapter, where they can be met alongside the tense that makes them irregular.

The root √yam leans towards restraint: its Pāli derivatives are overwhelmingly the vocabulary of restraint (saṁyama, saññata, vāyamati ‘makes effort’), and no other word in the family means ‘die’. This chapter does not depend on the choice, since the form is a first person plural middle either way, and the disagreement is recorded here, not resolved.↩︎

It is a tidy account of a real oddity, and a reader needs only its consequence.↩︎
