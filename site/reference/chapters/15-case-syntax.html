    Chapter 15

    Case Syntax

    The declension chapters taught what the endings are. This one is about what they do, and an ending does less than a beginner hopes and more than a translator often notices. Wijesekera put the stakes clearly in his introduction: “A considerable number of wrong translations (sometimes doctrinally important) have been found to contain errors which could only be detected through a more intense study of the Pāli case syntax.”1 That is a strong claim from a careful man, and this chapter takes it as its warrant.

    One sentence, four accusatives

    Before any theory, a sentence every reader of the canon has met a hundred times.

    Atha kho bhagavā kismiñcideva pakaraṇe bhikkhusaṅghaṁ paṇāmetvā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya kapilavatthuṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi. (SN 22.80 — S iii 91)

    atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgkismiñcidevafor some or othern loc sgpakaraṇeaffair, occasionn loc sgbhikkhusaṅghaṁthe Saṅgha of monksm acc sgpaṇāmetvāhaving dismissedpubbaṇhasamayaṁin the morningm acc sg, adverbialnivāsetvāhaving dressedpattacīvaraṁbowl and roben acc sgādāyahaving takenkapilavatthuṁKapilavatthun acc sgpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sgpāvisihe entered

    Then the Blessed One, having dismissed the Saṅgha of monks over some matter or other, dressed in the morning, took his bowl and robe, and entered Kapilavatthu for alms.

    Four accusatives, with three different functions. Bhikkhusaṅghaṁ and pattacīvaraṁ are ordinary objects, the things dismissed and taken. Kapilavatthuṁ is not an object at all: nothing is done to Kapilavatthu, and the accusative marks it as the place the going is towards. Pubbaṇhasamayaṁ is not even a participant in the action: it is the time at which the action happened, and it functions as an adverb, as the Digital Pali Dictionary in fact files it.2 Beside them stands one dative, piṇḍāya, the purpose of entering, neither the thing entered nor the direction.

    So the -aṁ on a noun does not mean ‘object’. It means: this word is in the second case, and now look at the verb. That is the whole method of this chapter, and everything else in it is an elaboration.

    Eight endings, six roles

    Western grammars of Pāli count eight cases and name them after Latin. The Pāli tradition counts differently, and the difference is not a quibble.

    The tradition has eight vibhatti, ‘divisions’, the sets of endings themselves, numbered, not named: paṭhamā the first (nominative), dutiyā the second (accusative), and so on to sattamī the seventh (locative), with the vocative counted as a variety of the first. Against those eight endings it sets six kāraka, ‘factors of action’, the roles a participant can play in what the verb describes. Aggavaṁsa’s definition in the Saddanīti is as compressed as a definition gets: kiriyānimittaṁ kārakaṁ, “a factor of action is an element of the action”; and again, kiriyābhisambandhalakkhaṇaṁ kārakaṁ, “a factor of action is characterised by connection to the action.”3

    Kāraka What it is Usual case
    kattā the agent — who brings the action about nominative, or instrumental
    kamma the object — what the action reaches accusative
    karaṇa the means — what the action is done with instrumental
    sampadāna the recipient — who receives, or for whom dative
    apādāna the point of departure — what is moved away from ablative
    okāsa the place — what supports the action locative

    Two of the eight endings are left over, and the tradition says so explicitly: na chaṭṭhīvihitattho kārakaṁ, yathā mantanaṁ: “what is expressed by the sixth case is not a factor of action, nor is address.”4 The genitive and the vocative are outside the system, and for a stated reason. A genitive relates one noun to another and reaches the verb only at second hand, if at all. A vocative has no connection to the action at the moment it is uttered: it makes someone available to be addressed, and only then can they be told to go or to eat.

    One caution about the tradition’s evidence, since this chapter quotes it repeatedly. The sentences the grammarians use to illustrate their rules are constructed, not quoted. Wijesekera says so in his introduction (“in laying down rules they merely follow the precedence of Sanskrit writers, particularly of Pāṇinī”) and again at §30, that both Pāli grammarians “for the most part merely repeat the conventional illustrations of Pāṇinī (e.g., kaṭaṁ karoti).” That judgement can now be checked instead of taken on trust. The two tables below (the fourfold locative from the vutti on Kaccāyana, and the genitive’s relations from the Saddanīti) carry fourteen illustrations between them, and not one of the fourteen occurs in a root text; twelve are confined to the grammatical literature and the commentarial texts, and two occur nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all.5 They are quoted here because the analysis they illustrate is excellent, and they are labelled as the tradition’s throughout. Every canonical example in this chapter carries a citation; the grammarians’ do not, because there is no source to cite.

    It predicts the structure of the rest of the chapter. The six cases that are kārakas are read against the verb; the genitive is read against the noun beside it. So the genitive most often defeats a beginner, and the grammars’ lists of its uses are much longer and vaguer than their lists for the other cases. Wijesekera, who is not much given to complaint, says of the tradition’s own treatment of the genitive that it “is not so clear-cut and exhaustive as of the others.”6

    Read that way, the sentence the chapter opened with sorts itself out. Each word is set against the verb that governs it, not against the verb at the end of the sentence:

    Word Case, and what it does Read against
    bhagavā nominative: the agent, kattā all four verbs
    bhikkhusaṅghaṁ accusative: the object, kamma paṇāmetvā
    pubbaṇhasamayaṁ accusative: time when nivāsetvā
    pattacīvaraṁ accusative: the object, kamma ādāya
    kapilavatthuṁ accusative: the goal of motion pāvisi
    piṇḍāya dative: purpose pāvisi

    Only two of the rows name a kāraka, and that is the instructive thing about them. The object and the agent are the tradition’s kamma and kattā. The goal, the time and the purpose are uses of a case, which the sections below take one at a time, and what tells them apart is the word in the right-hand column, not the ending. Pattacīvaraṁ and kapilavatthuṁ end alike and do different work because ādāya takes a thing and pāvisi takes a place. Pubbaṇhasamayaṁ is set against nivāsetvā, the action it stands in front of, though the morning is the time of everything after it as well.

    Why the cases run together

    The other thing to know before reading any of the cases in detail is that several of them have partly merged, and the merger is in the endings, not in anybody’s analysis. This is the single most useful structural fact in the chapter and it is nowhere stated as a rule; it has to be assembled out of three of Wijesekera’s introductory sections.7

    Cases Where they are identical Where they stay apart
    Instrumental and ablative throughout the plural (-ehi, -āhi, -ūhi); and in the -ā singular of the a-stems the a-stem singular -ena against -asmā, -amhā
    Dative and genitive everywhere, in both numbers one form only: the a-stem dative singular -āya
    Feminine ā-stems -āya is instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive and locative in the singular nowhere, in the singular
    Locative — distinct in every declension

    Where the forms merged, the functions bled into each other, and Pāli now uses each of the pair in senses that properly belong to the other. Where the forms stayed apart, the functions stayed comparatively clean. So a beginner finds the accusative and the locative easiest, while the instrumental, ablative, dative and genitive seem to shade into one another: they do, and the reason is morphological. It also explains why the feminine ā-stems are the hardest nouns in the language to read, a point the declension chapter made about the forms and this chapter now uses.

    Perniola ends his own chapter on the cases with the same observation, arrived at from the other direction: “Paying attention to the uses of the cases, one may have noticed how the same idea can be expressed by means of different cases. This indistinctness of the cases started already in Sanskrit and became much more common in Pali.”8

    The nominative

    The nominative marks the grammatical subject: the word that changes number when the verb changes number. That much is mechanical, and the declension chapters have already used it.

    It does not mark the agent. The two coincide in an active sentence and come apart in a passive one, and Pāli’s fondness for the passive makes this a daily problem, not an occasional one. The tradition sees it clearly: Aggavaṁsa divides agents into the abhihitakattā, the agent “expressed” by the verb’s own ending, and the anabhihitakattā, the agent the verb ending does not express and which must therefore be named separately, in the instrumental.9

    The formula every Buddhist knows is a passive sentence and shows this:

    Svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko paccattaṁ veditabbo viññūhi. (AN 6.10 — A iii 285)

    svākkhātoawell proclaimedm nom sg, past participlebhagavatāby the Blessed Onem ins sgdhammoathe teachingm nom sgsandiṭṭhikoavisible here and nowakālikoatimelessehipassikoainviting inspectionopaneyyikoaleading onwardspaccattaṁindividuallyadverbial accveditabboato be knownm nom sg, gerundiveviññūhiby the wisem ins pl

    The teaching is well proclaimed by the Blessed One, visible here and now, timeless, inviting inspection, leading onwards, to be known individually by the wise.

    Dhammo is the nominative and bhagavatā is the agent, and they are different words. Equate nominative with agent and you will render this as though the Dhamma proclaimed something.

    Predicative nominative. A second nominative can stand beside the first as what the subject is said to be, whether or not a form of hoti or atthi is present:

    Sīle patiṭṭhāya naro sapañño, cittaṁ paññañca bhāvayaṁ. (SN 1.23 — S i 13)

    sīlein virtuen loc sgpatiṭṭhāyahaving established himselfnaroaa personm nom sgsapaññoawisem nom sgcittaṁmindn acc sgpaññañ< paññaṁ wisdom f acc sgcaandbhāvayaṁadevelopingm nom sg, present participle

    A wise person, established in virtue, developing mind and wisdom …

    Where no verb appears at all the sentence is called nominal, and a form of hoti is understood. English does this too, though rarely and mostly in proverbs.

    The accusative

    The accusative is the kamma, the object; Kaccāyana’s definition is yaṁ karoti taṁ kammaṁ, “what one does is the object” (Kacc 282). Wijesekera notes that this covers verbs of making rather well and other verbs less well, and that Moggallāna, who says instead that the object is “what is done by, or intimately connected with, the action of the agent”, comes nearer to the case’s function.10

    Five uses cover nearly everything a reader meets.

    The object proper, which needs no illustration beyond the sentence at the head of this chapter.

    The internal object: a noun from the same root as the verb, or of the same meaning, which gives the action something to be measured by more than it adds a participant. English has a little of this in ‘sing a song’, ‘dream a dream’; Pāli has a great deal:

    Atha kho bhagavā imaṁ udānaṁ udānesi. (SN 56.11 — S v 424)

    atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgimaṁthisn acc sgudānaṁinspired utterancen acc sgudānesiuttered

    Then the Blessed One uttered this inspired utterance.

    The construction is far commoner than its oddity in English suggests, and Perniola’s observation about it holds: the internal object is almost never bare, but is qualified by a pronoun or an adjective, which is what gives the phrase its content.11

    The goal of motion, as kapilavatthuṁ above. Pāli has three ways of saying where the going goes (accusative, dative and locative), and they are not interchangeable in emphasis, though they overlap. The accusative is the plain one.

    Extent in time and space, and time when. So cattāro māse parivasati, “he lives on probation for four months” (DN 16 — D ii 152); dvādasa yojanāni, ‘twelve leagues’; and ekaṁ samayaṁ, ‘on one occasion’, which opens most of the suttas in the canon.

    Two accusatives with one verb, where the second is what the object is called or made:

    Tamahaṁ brūmi brāhmaṇaṁ. (Dhp 385 — Dhp p. 55)

    taṁahimm acc sgahaṁInombrūmiI callbrāhmaṇaṁaa brahminm acc sg

    Him I call a brahmin.

    The verbs that behave this way are those meaning to call, to name, to appoint, to choose; and, separately, verbs of asking and telling, which take an accusative of the person as well as of the thing. When such a verb is turned passive the person becomes nominative and the thing stays accusative: Buddho tena pañhaṁ puṭṭho, “the Buddha was asked a question by him.”12

    The instrumental

    The karaṇa, “that by which an action is done” (Kacc 290). Brugmann’s name for it is better than the Latin one and Wijesekera adopts it: the with-case, since ‘with’ in English covers both the instrument and the company.13

    Means. Aggavaṁsa divides these into internal and external (taṁ duvidhaṁ ajjhattikabāhiravasena), and the division is doctrinal as much as grammatical, because the internal means are the sense faculties. Cakkhunā rūpaṁ passati ‘he sees a form with the eye’, manasā dhammaṁ vijānāti ‘he cognises a mental object with the mind’; against hatthena kammaṁ karoti ‘he does work with the hand’, pharasunā rukkhaṁ chindati ‘he cuts a tree with an axe’.14 The internal set is the more important for a reader of the suttas, because the whole analysis of experience is phrased in it.

    Agent of a passive or causative verb, as bhagavatā above.

    Accompaniment, usually reinforced by saddhiṁ or saha, though the bare instrumental will do it. The reinforcement is so nearly universal in Pāli that the bare sociative reads as archaic.

    Cause and manner. Kenaci karaṇīyena ‘on account of some business’; tuṇhībhāvena ‘in silence’; sirasā vandati ‘pays homage with the head’.

    Route and time. The instrumental of the way through which motion passes (yena dvārena nikkhami ‘the gate by which he went out’) and of the time within which something happens.

    Direction, in the yena … tena construction. This is one of the most frequent sentence patterns in the canon and belongs here, not under the accusative:

    Atha kho aññatarā devatā abhikkantāya rattiyā abhikkantavaṇṇā kevalakappaṁ jetavanaṁ obhāsetvā yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami. (SN 1.1 — S i 1)

    atha khothenaññatarā devatāa certain deityf nom sgabhikkantāya rattiyāwhen the night was far gonef loc sg absoluteabhikkantavaṇṇāof surpassing beautykevalakappaṁthe whole ofm acc sgjetavanaṁJeta’s Groven acc sgobhāsetvāhaving illuminatedyenaby which, wherem ins sgbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgtenaby that, therem ins sgupasaṅkami< upa + saṁ + √kam + i — he approached aor

    Then, late in the night, a certain deity of surpassing beauty, lighting up the whole of Jeta’s Grove, went up to the Blessed One.

    Literally it is “by which the Blessed One, by that he approached”: a relative and correlative pair in the instrumental, with the person in the nominative, not in any case governed by the verb. English has nothing corresponding and the idiom simply has to be learnt.

    Comparison, and the words that take it. The second term of a comparison may go into the instrumental (na tena seyyo ‘none better than he’), and three common words govern it outright: alaṁ ‘enough of’, kiṁ ‘what is the use of’, and attho ‘there is need of’: puññena attho ‘there is need of merit’.

    With sakkā and its relatives. The impersonal sakkā ‘it is possible’, labbhā ‘it is obtainable’, dujjānaṁ ‘it is hard to know’, sukaraṁ ‘it is easy to do’ take an infinitive and put the person in the instrumental. Poṭṭhapāda’s question and the Buddha’s reply are the construction twice over, once in each person:15

    Sakkā panetaṁ, bhante, mayā ñātuṁ … Dujjānaṁ kho etaṁ, poṭṭhapāda, tayā … (DN 9 — D i 187, 24.3)

    sakkāit is possibleindpanabutetaṁthisn acc sgbhantesirvocmayāby me, for me1 ins sgñātuṁto knowinfdujjānaṁhard to known nom sgkhoindeedpoṭṭhapādaPoṭṭhapādavoctayāby you, for you2 ins sg

    “But is it possible for me to know this, sir?” … “That is hard for you to know, Poṭṭhapāda …”

    The dative

    The sampadāna, whose name means giving-over and whose commonest sense is therefore the recipient. In Pāli the case is nearly extinct in form. Only the a-stem singular in -āya is a true dative; everywhere else the genitive endings serve for both, and Perniola accordingly declines to discuss any dative but the -āya one, classing the rest under the genitive.16

    Purpose is what the -āya dative overwhelmingly expresses, and the formula that says what the Dhamma is for is seven of them in a row:

    Etaṁ nibbidāya virāgāya nirodhāya upasamāya abhiññāya sambodhāya nibbānāya saṁvattati. (DN 9 — D i 189)

    etaṁthisn nom sgnibbidāyato disenchantmentf dat sgvirāgāyato dispassionm dat sgnirodhāyato cessationupasamāyato peaceabhiññāyato direct knowledgesambodhāyato awakeningnibbānāyato extinguishmentsaṁvattatileads

    This leads to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to peace, to direct knowledge, to awakening, to extinguishment.

    Recipient, with verbs of giving; and, hardly distinguishable from it, the person for whose advantage or disadvantage something happens. Wijesekera keeps a separate heading for the sympathetic dative, the dative of the person whose interest is engaged, not whose benefit is at stake, and traces the whole dative-genitive collapse back to it: the sympathetic use began with the enclitics me and te, spread to proper names, and finally to nouns generally.17 The chapter on pronouns has already met the result of that history in evaṁ me sutaṁ, where the tradition itself declines to say whether me is instrumental or genitive.

    The recipient is at its plainest in the formal acts of the Sangha, because a legal text has to say who receives. The procedure for a robe that has been forfeited ends with the Sangha handing it back:

    Yadi saṅghassa pattakallaṁ, saṅgho imaṁ cīvaraṁ itthannāmassa bhikkhuno dadeyya. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196)

    yadiifsaṅghassafor the Sangham dat sgpattakallaṁthe right time has comen nom sgsaṅghothe Sangham nom sgimaṁathisn acc sgcīvaraṁaroben acc sgitthannāmassabof such-and-such a namem dat sgbhikkhunobto the monkm dat sgdadeyyashould give3 sg opt

    “If the Sangha is ready, let the Sangha give this robe to the monk so-and-so.”

    Agent, object, recipient and a verb of giving, in that order, and every one of the datives ends in the genitive’s -assa or -uno. The same rule’s opening sentence coordinates three more of them: nissajjitabbaṁ saṅghassa vā gaṇassa vā puggalassa vā, “it is to be relinquished to the Sangha, or to a group, or to an individual” (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196). Not one true dative ending appears in either sentence. Take every -assa for a possessive and you turn the recipients into owners.

    Purpose expressed as a verbal noun, where the dative of an action noun stands in for an infinitive: kassapaṁ bhagavantaṁ … dassanāya upasaṅkamissāma, “let us approach the Blessed One Kassapa in order to see him” (MN 81 — M ii 46). Chapter 11 met the same interchange from the infinitive’s side.

    The dative that carries a doctrine. The last clause of the arahant formula is a dative, and getting it wrong changes what the sentence claims:

    ‘Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṁ brahmacariyaṁ, kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ, nāparaṁ itthattāyā’ti pajānāti. (DN 2 — D i 84)

    khīṇāexhaustedf nom sgjātibirthf nom sgvusitaṁlived outbrahmacariyaṁthe spiritual lifekataṁdonekaraṇīyaṁwhat was to be donenanotaparaṁfurtheritthattāyafor this state of beingn dat sgtiend of quotationpajānātihe understands

    He understands: ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived out, what was to be done has been done, there is no further coming to this state of being.’

    Itthatta is an abstract noun, ittha ‘thus, here’ plus -tta, ‘the state of being thus’; itthattāya is its dative singular. The proof lies in a passage where the same dative governs a verb of coming-into-being (api nu kho nāmarūpaṁ itthattāya abhinibbattissathā ti, “would name-and-form be produced into this state of being?”, DN 15 — D ii 63), not in the formula, where the word stands alone, and also in the commentary, which glosses the formula’s itthattāya with a string of further datives, itthabhāvāya … kilesakkhayāya.18 Rhys Davids read the phrase cosmologically and rendered it “after this present world there is no beyond”. It is about coming back, not about a world.

    The ablative

    The apādāna, literally ‘a taking away from’: Kaccāyana defines it as that “from which there is a moving away, or fear, or a taking” (Kacc 273). Speyer’s remark, which Wijesekera endorses, is that the ablative is the exact opposite of the dative: the one is from, the other is for.19

    Starting point, whether of place, person or thing, and by easy extension origin and descent.

    Separation, release, abstention: the sense in which the precepts are phrased:

    Musāvādā veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ samādiyāmi. (Kp 2)

    musāvādāfrom false speechm abl sgveramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ< veramaṇī abstaining + sikkhāpadaṁ training rule — the training rule of abstaining n acc sgsamādiyāmiI undertake

    I undertake the training rule of abstaining from false speech.

    The ablative here is governed by the veramaṇī buried inside the compound, not by samādiyāmi, and so the phrase survives being compounded at all. Thānuttamo lists ‘abstention from’ as one of the ablative’s senses and names ārati ‘abstinence’ as another noun that takes it. Wijesekera would want it added that adnominal ablatives in the Nikāyas are few and mostly presuppose a verb that has dropped out.20

    Cause and reason, which is the ablative that carries dependent origination:

    Jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. (SN 12.1 — S ii 1)

    jātipaccayāwith birth as conditionm abl sgjarāmaraṇaṁageing and deathn nom sgsokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsāsorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness and distressm nom plsambhavanticome to be

    With birth as condition, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness and distress come to be.

    Comparison, the second term standing in the ablative: suttā jāgaritaṁ seyyo, “waking is better than sleep” (Iti 47 — It p. 41).

    Point of view, and this one has a signature. When the ablative means ‘as, in terms of, regarded from the side of’, it is very often formed with the suffix -to instead of an ordinary ending, and the chapter on nouns has already introduced that suffix. The Mahāpadāna Sutta’s list of what the Buddha recollects is a string of them: jātitopi anussarati, nāmatopi anussarati, gottatopi anussarati, “he recollects them by birth, by name, by clan” (DN 14).21

    The locative

    The okāsa in Kaccāyana’s terminology and the adhikaraṇa in Pāṇini’s, but the tradition’s preferred word is ādhāra, ‘support’: the place holds the action up as well as being where it happens.22 The metaphor deserves to be taken seriously, because it unifies uses that otherwise look unrelated.

    The tradition divides the locative fourfold, and the division is a good one:

    Type What it means The tradition’s example
    byāpika pervading — the support is permeated by what it supports tilesu telaṁ ‘oil in sesame seeds’
    opasilesika in contact — two separate things touching kaṭe nisīdati ‘he sits on the mat’
    sāmīpika neighbouring — in the vicinity of sāvatthiyaṁ viharati ‘he stays at Sāvatthī’
    vesayika domain — the sphere a kind of thing occupies jalesu macchā ‘fish in the waters’

    Every one of those examples is a grammarians’ sentence, not a canonical one. The vutti offers eight illustrations across the four types; six of them (tilesu telaṁ, kaṭe nisīdati, jalesu macchā, dadhimhi sappi, ākāse sakuṇā, gaṅgāyaṁ vajo) occur outside the root texts only, five of those six in the grammatical literature alone; and the remaining two, ucchusu raso and bhūmisu manussā, occur nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana at all.23 That is normal for a rule illustrated by a rule-book and it does not weaken the analysis, but a reader should know that the Buddha did not say these familiar sentences. The canon supplies its own instances readily enough: ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā sāvatthiyaṁ viharati is the sāmīpika type, and it opens hundreds of suttas.

    Beyond place, three uses account for most locatives a reader meets.

    Time when.

    Ajjeva rattiyā pacchime yāme tathāgatassa parinibbānaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 139)

    ajjatodayevajustrattiyāof the nightf gen sgpacchimelastm loc sgyāmein the watchm loc sgtathāgatassaof the Tathāgatam gen sgparinibbānaṁfinal extinguishmentn nom sgbhavissatiwill be

    This very day, in the last watch of the night, the Tathāgata’s final extinguishment will take place.

    The genitive rattiyā inside the locative phrase has the ordinary partitive sense of a genitive: the watch is a part of the night.

    Reference: ‘with regard to’, the nimitta-sattamī of the grammarians. The analytical suttas use this locative most:

    Evaṁ passaṁ, bhikkhave, sutavā ariyasāvako rūpasmimpi nibbindati, vedanāyapi nibbindati, saññāyapi nibbindati, saṅkhāresupi nibbindati, viññāṇasmimpi nibbindati. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)

    evaṁthuspassaṁseeingm nom sg, present participlebhikkhavemonksvoc plsutavālearnedm nom sgariyasāvakonoble disciplem nom sgrūpasmiṁregarding formn loc sgpialsonibbindatibecomes disenchantedvedanāyaregarding feelingf loc sgsaññāyaregarding perceptionf loc sgsaṅkhāresuregarding volitional formationsm loc plviññāṇasmiṁregarding consciousnessn loc sg

    Seeing thus, monks, a learned noble disciple becomes disenchanted with form, disenchanted with feeling, disenchanted with perception, disenchanted with volitional formations, disenchanted with consciousness.

    That sentence is the whole chapter in one line. Three of the five words are unmistakably locative: rūpasmiṁ, saṅkhāresu, viññāṇasmiṁ carry endings that can only be locative. Two of them, vedanāya and saññāya, are feminine ā-stems whose -āya could be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locative, and the words themselves do not say which. They are locative because the other three are, and because nibbindati takes a locative. The construction decides, and the ending only narrows.

    Contact, with verbs of seizing. Where English uses ‘by’, Pāli puts the part taken hold of in the locative:

    Seyyathāpi, bho gotama, puriso purisaṁ narakapapātaṁ patantaṁ kesesu gahetvā uddharitvā thale patiṭṭhapeyya. (DN 12 — D i 234)

    seyyathāpijust asbho gotamagood Gotamavocpurisoa manm nom sgpurisaṁa manm acc sgnarakapapātaṁinto a pitm acc sgpatantaṁfallingm acc sg, present participlekesesuby the hairm loc plgahetvāhaving seizeduddharitvāhaving pulled outthaleon firm groundn loc sgpatiṭṭhapeyyawould set down

    Just as, good Gotama, a man might seize by the hair another man falling into a pit, pull him out, and set him on firm ground.

    Both locatives in that sentence are the opasilesika type of the table above, contact between two separately established things: the hand on the hair and the man on the ground.

    The genitive, which is not a role

    Everything so far has been read against the verb. The genitive is read against the noun beside it, and so the tradition leaves it out of the kārakas.

    Aggavaṁsa’s own list of what the genitive can express is the most useful thing any source says about it, because it is short, and because it makes plain that ‘possession’ is only the commonest member of a set:24

    Relation Sense The tradition’s example
    sāmi ownership (named, not illustrated)
    bhacca maintained by rañño puriso ‘the king’s man’
    santaka belonging to rañño raṭṭhaṁ ‘the king’s realm’
    samīpa in the vicinity of ambavanassa avidūre ‘not far from the mango grove’
    samūha accumulation of dhaññānaṁ rāsi ‘a heap of grain’
    avayava part of rukkhassa sākhā ‘a branch of a tree’
    vikāra alteration of suvaṇṇassa vikati ‘a thing made of gold’

    Sāmi, ownership, is the only relation Aggavaṁsa names without illustrating: it is the sense he takes the genitive to be known by, “by synecdoche”, and the other six are what he wants added to it. His reason for putting the whole set outside the kārakas is also the clearest short statement of what a genitive is. When a man is called rañño puriso, it is because the king provides and the man receives that the phrase means anything — and neither the providing nor the receiving is in the sentence.

    To that, Western grammar adds two divisions the tradition does not make, and they matter. A subjective genitive names the doer of the action the head noun describes; an objective genitive names what it is done to. The phrase tathāgatassa dassanaṁ could be either (the Tathāgata’s seeing of something, or somebody’s seeing of the Tathāgata), and in the only place it occurs in the canon, a second genitive settles it:

    Idaṁ pacchimakaṁ, ānanda, tathāgatassa vesāliyā dassanaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 122)

    idaṁthisn nom sgpacchimakaṁlastn nom sgānandaĀnandavoctathāgatassaof the Tathāgatam gen sg, subjectivevesāliyāof Vesālīf gen sg, objectivedassanaṁseeingn nom sgbhavissatiwill be

    This, Ānanda, will be the Tathāgata’s last sight of Vesālī.

    Two genitives on one noun, one on each side of it, with opposite functions. The endings do not show which is which; the world does, because a city cannot look at a person.

    Genitives also nest, and then the reader has to decide which noun each one hangs on. The Vinaya defines the precincts of a village by measuring them, and the measurement is a chain:

    Parikkhittassa gāmassa indakhīle ṭhitassa majjhimassa purisassa leḍḍupāto. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46)

    parikkhittassaaenclosedm gen sggāmassaaof an inhabited aream gen sgindakhīleat the gate-postm loc sgṭhitassabstandingm gen sg ppmajjhimassabof middling staturem gen sgpurisassabof a manm gen sgleḍḍupātoa stone’s throwm nom sg

    “A stone’s throw of a man of middling stature standing at the gate-post of an enclosed inhabited area.”

    Five of the seven words are genitives, and they do not all attach to the same thing. Majjhimassa purisassa belongs to leḍḍupāto: it is his throw. Parikkhittassa gāmassa belongs to indakhīle: it is the village’s gate-post, and indakhīle is a locative inside the phrase that ṭhitassa heads. One genitive phrase therefore sits inside the other, at a depth no ending records. What can sensibly own what settles it. A village has a gate-post. It does not have a stone’s throw.

    Ṭhitassa deserves a second look, because it looks like the construction Chapter 16 calls the genitive absolute, a past participle in the genitive with a genitive noun beside it. It is not one. An absolute detaches its subject from the main clause, and this man is not detached from anything: he is the man whose throw is being measured, and the participle agrees with him as an adjective would.

    The partitive genitive names the whole out of which a part is taken, and turns up regularly with superlatives and with words meaning ‘one of’. The sentence that records the Buddha’s last disciple is one:

    Aññataro kho panāyasmā subhaddo arahataṁ ahosi. (DN 16 — D ii 153)

    aññataroa certain onem nom sgkho panamoreoverāyasmāthe venerablem nom sgsubhaddoSubhaddam nom sgarahataṁof the arahantsm gen plahosibecame

    And the venerable Subhadda became one of the arahants.

    Pāli has no verb ‘to have’, and this is the gap the genitive fills. What English says with ‘have’, Pāli says with ‘of me there is’:

    Puttā matthi dhanaṁ matthi. (Dhp 62 — Dhp p. 9)

    puttāsonsm nom plmatthi< me of me 1 gen./dat. sg + atthi there isdhanaṁwealthn nom sg

    ‘I have sons, I have wealth’ —

    Literally, “of me there are sons, of me there is wealth.” Note that me here could be read as either genitive or dative and the sentence does not settle it; this is the merger described at the head of the chapter, met in a real sentence.25

    The genitive with verbs. A list of verbs governs the genitive where English would use an object or a preposition, and it has to be learnt as a list. The commonest are those of fearing, trembling, remembering, envying, believing, filling and thinking lightly of:26

    Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno. (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19)

    sabbeallm nom pltasantitrembledaṇḍassaat the rodm gen sgbhāyantifearmaccunodeathm gen sg

    All tremble at the rod, all fear death.

    The genitive of the agent with a past participle, where the instrumental would be equally correct and is commoner: ñāto yasassī titthakaro sādhusammato bahujanassa, “well known, renowned, a founder of a sect, held in high regard by many people” (DN 2 — D i 47).

    The vocative

    The vocative addresses, and no more. The tradition excludes it from the kārakas because at the moment of address there is no action yet to be connected to: the calling makes someone available to be told to go or to eat.

    Two facts about its behaviour need stating, because both are close enough to exceptionless to be useful. A vocative is never the first word of a sentence in the canonical language, and usually stands second or third; and a vocative may be dropped without breaking the grammar, which is the practical mark of its standing outside the system.

    Kathañca, mahārāja, bhikkhu sīlasampanno hoti? (DN 2 — D i 63)

    kathaṁhowcaandmahārājagreat kingm voc sgbhikkhua monkm nom sgsīlasampannoaccomplished in virtuem nom sghotiis

    And how, great king, is a monk accomplished in virtue?

    Perniola records the exceptions to the first rule (bhikkhavo, bhadante, āvuso, ambho, used to catch the attention of the person addressed and therefore free to lead) and gives the social range of the forms of address, which is a small ethnography of the canon: monks are addressed as bhikkhu or by name, lay disciples address monks as bhante, gods address them as mārisa, kings and queens as mahārāja and devī.27

    Where case syntax is treated elsewhere

    The endings themselves are in the chapters on nouns and on gender and adjectives, along with the ablative in -to and the collapse of the feminine -āya that this chapter has leaned on so heavily. Which case an indeclinable governs (saddhiṁ and saha the instrumental, aññatra and vinā the instrumental, yāva and antarā the accusative, upari and heṭṭhā the genitive) is in the chapter on indeclinables, together with the absolutives that have hardened into postpositions and take the accusative. Case forms frozen into adverbs are there too. The absolute constructions (locative, genitive, and the rarer accusative and nominative) are in the chapter on clause syntax, because they are dependent clauses; here belongs only the observation that they are named after their case because the case marks them off, and that the locative is by a wide margin the commonest of the four. And the compound is a case relation with the ending taken off: a tappurisa resolves into two words in two cases, so the chapter on compounds spends its longest section on identifying which case the first member would have worn.

    When the ending will not decide

    Everything this chapter has set out is a set of tendencies, and it is important to say so before a reader treats it as a set of rules. Wijesekera, having spent three hundred pages classifying, ends his account of the genitive by warning against his own categories: divisions such as the genitive of possession, of material, of origin, subjective and objective “are made merely for the sake of convenience; they do not imply that absolute categories are possible.”28 The classifications arrange what has been observed. The language does not follow them.

    So the practical question is what to do with an ending that could be several cases. Two steps, in this order.

    Look at the verb first. Most of the time the verb settles it, because most verbs take one case and not another. Nibbindati takes a locative; tasati takes a genitive; saṁvattati takes a dative; viramati takes an ablative. When a form could be any of five cases, the verb it hangs on is generally the only witness that will testify.

    Then look for the parallel. Pāli is a language of lists, and a form whose case is unclear is very often standing in a series with forms whose case is not. That is how vedanāya was settled two sections ago: it stood between rūpasmiṁ and saṅkhāresu.

    Where neither helps, say that the sentence is open. The tradition does so when it has to. Its treatment of evaṁ me sutaṁ, the commonest sentence in the canon, is to list the three senses in which me is found and then decline to choose between two of them for this passage.29 To demand that Pāli be more determinate than its own commentators found it is to assert things the language does not say: the failure Wijesekera’s introduction warned about.

    One last example, and it is the reason the feminine ā-stems were called the hardest nouns in the language:

    Taṇhāya jāyatī soko, taṇhāya jāyatī bhayaṁ. (Dhp 216 — Dhp p. 32)

    taṇhāyafrom cravingf, an ending that may be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locativejāyatīis bornsokosorrowm nom sgbhayaṁfearn nom sg

    From craving is sorrow born, from craving is fear born.

    Taṇhāya could be five cases and is one. It is ablative, of cause, and we know it from jāyati, which takes an ablative of the source, not from the word, and from the four verses standing immediately before it in the same chapter of the Dhammapada, which build the identical sentence five times over:

    Dhp The word The ending
    212 piyato -to, ablative and nothing else
    213 pemato -to, ablative and nothing else
    214 ratiyā -āya/-iyā, any of five cases
    215 kāmato -to, ablative and nothing else
    216 taṇhāya -āya, any of five cases

    Three of the five carry an ending that can only be ablative, and two carry an ending that could be almost anything. The two ambiguous ones sit inside a sequence built by the three unambiguous ones, and so are read as those are. That is how a case is decided in practice: a word placed against everything around it, not a rule applied to a word.
