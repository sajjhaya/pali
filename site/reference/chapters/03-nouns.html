    Chapter 3

    Nouns: Stems and Declension

    A Pāli noun is a stem plus an ending. The stem carries the meaning and does not change; the ending carries the grammar and changes constantly. Buddha is a stem, and it is not a word: what the texts contain is buddho, buddhaṁ, buddhena, buddhassa, buddhe, buddhānaṁ. The consequence is one a reader meets on the first page of any sutta. No Pāli word ends in a bare consonant, and no Pāli word is a bare stem; every word in the canon ends in a vowel or in the niggahīta ṁ. When a grammar prints rājan or bhagavant or satthar, it is printing something the texts never say.

    The set of endings a stem takes is its declension (vibhatti, ‘the division’), and stems that take the same endings form a declension group. There are around twenty of these, which sounds worse than it is: three of them account for most of the canon, and the rest are variations legible from those three.

    Cases, numbers and genders

    Every inflected noun in the canon simultaneously answers three questions: what job is it doing in its sentence, how many of it are there, and what gender is it.

    Seven cases. Nominative, vocative, accusative, instrumental, ablative, genitive/dative, locative. This book’s tables print seven rows and not eight, because the genitive and the dative have fallen together: the same ending serves both almost everywhere.1 What each case does is the business of the chapter on case syntax; here it is only their forms.

    Two numbers, singular and plural. Old Indic had a third, the dual, and Pāli has lost it entirely. Only a single word survives, ubho ‘both’, which still inflects as one: the same fact met from the other side in the chapter on numerals, where it explains why ‘two’ has no paradigm of its own.2

    Three genders, masculine, neuter and feminine, abbreviated m., n. and f. throughout. Gender here is a grammatical category and not a statement about the world: the chapter on gender and adjectives takes that up, and shows three words for ‘road’ in three different genders.

    The one fact that organises the rest

    Before any paradigm, the fact that makes them learnable instead of memorisable. Perniola states it in a sentence and then moves on, and it deserves more weight than he gives it:

    the masculine and the neuter nouns are declined in the same way with the exception of the nominative, vocative and accusative singular and plural. The feminine nouns have different case endings.3

    So the masculine and neuter are one declension with a small exception, not two declensions. Everywhere except the three cases Perniola names, buddhena and cittena, buddhassa and cittassa, buddhesu and cittesu (a masculine and a neuter) are simply the same. The gender difference lives in the nominative, vocative and accusative, and nowhere else. That is a great deal less to learn than a table implies, and it holds across every stem class in the language, not merely the a-stems where it is easiest to see.

    The a-stems

    By far the largest class, and the one every other stem is read against. The masculine goes like purisa ‘man’, the neuter like rūpa ‘form’.

    Case Masc. Neut.
    Nom. sg. puriso rūpaṁ
    Voc. sg. purisa rūpaṁ
    Acc. sg. purisaṁ rūpaṁ
    Instr. sg. purisena rūpena
    Abl. sg. purisā, -asmā, -amhā, -ato rūpā, -asmā, -amhā, -ato
    Gen./Dat. sg. purisassa, -āya (dat.) rūpassa, -āya (dat.)
    Loc. sg. purise, -asmiṁ, -amhi rūpe, -asmiṁ, -amhi
    Nom./Voc. pl. purisā rūpāni, rūpā
    Acc. pl. purise rūpāni, rūpe
    Instr./Abl. pl. purisehi rūpehi
    Gen./Dat. pl. purisānaṁ rūpānaṁ
    Loc. pl. purisesu rūpesu

    Read the two columns against each other and Perniola’s rule is visible: they differ in four rows and are identical in the other seven.

    A caution about how to read that table, and about every paradigm table in this book and in every other grammar. A paradigm teaches endings, and its forms are only specimens of them. Purisasmā and purisamhi occur nowhere in the canon or its commentaries; they are found only in the grammarians’ own texts, where somebody has done what the table above does and completed the paradigm by rule. The endings are real and common (kāyasmā, kāyamhi and lokasmiṁ are ordinary canonical Pāli, and lokasmiṁ alone occurs over four hundred times in the root texts), but nobody in the canon happens to have said purisasmā.4

    This holds for every table below, and it is not a defect in them. A paradigm has to be complete to be a paradigm. It only misleads if it is read as a list of words the texts contain, and the rule for this book is accordingly narrow: where a specific form is asserted in the prose and not merely displayed in a table, it has been checked against the texts first. Appendix A.1 sidesteps the question altogether by printing bare endings and never a whole word.

    • Nāhaṁ, bhikkhave, aññaṁ ekarūpampi samanupassāmi yaṁ evaṁ purisassa cittaṁ pariyādāya tiṭṭhati … (AN 1.1 — A i 1)

    • Mendicants, I do not see a single sight that so occupies a man’s mind …

    • Idh’evam eso lokasmiṁ, mūlaṁ khaṇati attano. (Dhp 247 — Dhp p. 36)

    • Right here in this world this man digs up his own root.5

    • Nibbindaṁ virajjati; virāgā vimuccati. Vimuttasmiṁ vimuttamiti ñāṇaṁ hoti. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)

    • Being disillusioned, desire fades; through the fading of desire he is freed. When he is freed, the knowledge comes that he is freed.

    The last of those has two a-stem obliques with different functions in consecutive clauses: virāgā an ablative of cause, vimuttasmiṁ a locative, and both of them ordinary canonical forms, not paradigm fillers.

    Why the endings look as they do

    Four rules account for most of the pattern of that table, and Perniola sets them out where a reader can use them.6 The stem is purisa- throughout, and:

    • Before the nominative singular marker -s, the stem vowel and the marker together give -o: purisa-s > puriso.
    • Before the nominative plural -as, they give -ā: purisa-as > purisā.
    • The stem vowel lengthens before an ending beginning with n-: purisa-naṁ > purisānaṁ, rūpa-ni > rūpāni.
    • The stem vowel drops before an ending beginning with e-: purisa-ena > purisena, purisa-ehi > purisehi, purisa-esu > purisesu.

    None of this has to be applied consciously while reading. It is here because, once you have seen it, you stop experiencing purisānaṁ and purisesu as two unrelated forms and start hearing one stem under both, and that is the whole difficulty of the declension in miniature.

    Where the a-stems vary

    Three variants a reader will meet and should not be thrown by.

    A nominative singular in -e instead of -o turns up occasionally in both masculine and neuter: purise, manusse, jīvite, sukhe, dukkhe.7 A nominative plural in -āse appears in verse — paṇḍitāse, gatāse. And an ablative singular in -to is available to every stem in the language, not only this one; it is common enough to meet on its own, below.

    The ā-stems

    The feminine counterpart, and the third of the three paradigms that carry the canon. It goes like kaññā ‘girl’.

    Case Fem.
    Nom. sg. kaññā
    Voc. sg. kaññe
    Acc. sg. kaññaṁ
    Instr. sg. kaññāya
    Abl. sg. kaññāya, -āto
    Gen./Dat. sg. kaññāya
    Loc. sg. kaññāya, -āyaṁ
    Nom./Voc./Acc. pl. kaññā, kaññāyo
    Instr./Abl. pl. kaññāhi
    Gen./Dat. pl. kaññānaṁ
    Loc. pl. kaññāsu

    What is striking here is a collapse, not a form: in the singular, -āya serves four cases (instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative), and -āya or -āyaṁ the locative besides. Five of the seven cases are one ending. A feminine -ā noun in an oblique case therefore shows almost nothing about which case it is in; only the sentence around it does. This is the sharpest instance in the nominal system of a general truth about reading Pāli, that an ending narrows the possibilities and the context decides among them.

    • … pubbe ananussutesu dhammesu cakkhuṁ udapādi, ñāṇaṁ udapādi, paññā udapādi, vijjā udapādi, āloko udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)
    • … regarding teachings not heard before, vision arose, knowledge arose, wisdom arose, realisation arose, light arose.

    Two feminine ā-stems there, paññā and vijjā, both nominative singular and both recognisable as feminine by their ending alone, and nowhere else in this paradigm is the ending decisive.

    The i- and u-stems

    These two behave so alike that they are best met together: whatever the i-stem does with i, the u-stem does with u. Masculine aggi ‘fire’ and bhikkhu ‘monk’; neuter akkhi ‘eye’ and cakkhu ‘eye’.

    Case i- m. i- n. u- m. u- n.
    Nom./Voc. sg. aggi akkhi, akkhiṁ bhikkhu cakkhu, cakkhuṁ
    Acc. sg. aggiṁ akkhi, akkhiṁ bhikkhuṁ cakkhu, cakkhuṁ
    Instr. sg. agginā akkhinā bhikkhunā cakkhunā
    Abl. sg. agginā, -ismā, -imhā akkhinā, -ismā bhikkhunā, -usmā, -umhā cakkhunā, -usmā
    Gen./Dat. sg. aggino, aggissa akkhino, akkhissa bhikkhuno, bhikkhussa cakkhuno, cakkhussa
    Loc. sg. aggismiṁ, -imhi, aggini akkhismiṁ, akkhini bhikkhusmiṁ, -umhi cakkhusmiṁ, cakkhumhi
    Nom./Voc. pl. aggī, aggayo akkhīni, akkhī bhikkhū, bhikkhavo cakkhūni, cakkhū
    Acc. pl. aggī, aggayo akkhīni, akkhī bhikkhū, bhikkhavo cakkhūni, cakkhū
    Instr./Abl. pl. aggīhi akkhīhi bhikkhūhi cakkhūhi
    Gen./Dat. pl. aggīnaṁ akkhīnaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ cakkhūnaṁ
    Loc. pl. aggīsu akkhīsu bhikkhūsu cakkhūsu

    Two observations justify the table. The plural lengthens the stem vowel: aggi-hi > aggīhi, bhikkhu-su > bhikkhūsu. The specimen is aggi because it declines cleanly, not because the canon has occasion to speak of fires in the instrumental plural; the same ending on commoner stems gives isīhi ‘by the seers’ and gahapatīhi ‘by the householders’. And in the nominative and accusative plural the vowel is strengthened, not lengthened: aggi-o becomes aggayo by way of agge-o, and bhikkhu-o becomes bhikkhavo by way of bhikkho-o.8 That explains bhikkhavo, and the same operation produces the vocative bhikkhave, which a reader meets in the first line of most suttas in the canon.

    • Te taṁ rattiṁ tikkhattuṁ paccuṭṭhāya pañjalikā aggiṁ namassanti. (AN 10.119 — A v 234)

    • Getting up three times in the night, with palms joined, they worship the fire.

    • Idha pana, upavāṇa, bhikkhu cakkhunā rūpaṁ disvā rūpappaṭisaṁvedī ca hoti. (SN 35.70 — S iv 41)

    • Here, Upavāṇa, a mendicant, having seen a form with the eye, experiences the form.

    The second of those shows the two stems side by side doing different work in one clause: bhikkhu is a masculine u-stem in the nominative, subject of the sentence, and cakkhunā a neuter u-stem in the instrumental, the means by which the seeing is done.

    Feminine ī- and ū-stems

    The feminines in -ī and -ū (nadī ‘river’, vadhū ‘daughter-in-law’) differ from the feminines in -i and -u in the nominative singular and almost nowhere else.9 The oblique singular is nadiyā, vadhuyā, and the plural nadiyo, vadhuyo, nadīhi, vadhūhi, nadīnaṁ, nadīsu.

    Because the difference is so slight, a good many words are found in both shapes: hiri beside hirī ‘moral shame’, itthi beside itthī ‘woman’. Nothing hangs on which a manuscript prints.

    The really rare stems here are the two with a u: the feminine in -ū and its short-vowel partner in -u. Perniola’s paradigm supplies a specimen of each (vadhū ‘daughter-in-law’, dhenu ‘cow’), and the root texts barely use either.10 Vadhū reaches them only inside the compound kulavadhū; dhenu reaches them as dhenuṁ and dhenuyo and scarcely another case. The clearest instance of a -ū stem in the early texts is the river-name Sarabhū, not a common noun at all. Both paradigms are more of a curiosity than a tool.

    The odd one: go

    One stem in the language ends in -o: go ‘cow, ox’. In the singular it declines much like purisa apart from the nominative, and in the plural the sequence -ava- has contracted, giving gāvo, gohi, gunnaṁ, gosu.

    It gets a paragraph because Oberlies explains where its stranger forms came from, and the explanation demonstrates a process at work throughout the nominal system. A new stem gava- was built out of the instrumental gavā, which had come to be understood as an ablative; and another stem, goṇa-, was extracted from the newly-created feminine goṇī-.11 Forms are not only inherited. They are also back-formed, reverse-engineered by speakers from a form they had in front of them, and a paradigm that looks chaotic is often several such rebuildings laid on top of one another.

    Consonant stems

    Old Indic had a large and productive consonant declension. Pāli has the wreckage of it, and of the sources consulted here only Oberlies says so outright:

    Only traces of (a) the root nouns and (b) the consonant stems without vowel alternation — mainly neuter nouns in -as, -is and -us, and very few masc./fem. as-nouns — have survived.12

    Traces, and the direction of travel is towards the a-stems. Several consonant stems have a set of forms built the old way and a second set built as though the word were an ordinary a-stem: beside the accusative singular rājānaṁ stands rājaṁ, and beside attānaṁ stands attaṁ.13 Expect the consonant stems to be tidy and you will be puzzled; expect them to be halfway through a migration and you will not.

    How far the migration has gone can be measured instead of assumed: less far than the grammars imply. Perniola gives six transferred forms for these two words, and only the two accusatives above are found in the canon at all; the genitives rājassa and attassa and the plurals rājehi and attehi are constructions.14 The transfer is real, and in the canon it is still mostly confined to the accusative.

    Stems in -(n)t

    Words in -(n)t are adjectives or present participles by origin, though several of the commonest are used as nouns. This class has two stems, a strong one in -ant and a weak one in -at, and it uses both: bhagavant- gives bhagavanto and bhagavatā, sīlavant- gives sīlavanto and sīlavato. The nominative singular is shorter than either, ending in a bare -ā.

    Case Sg. Pl.
    Nom. bhagavā bhagavanto
    Voc. bhagavā, bhagava bhagavanto
    Acc. bhagavantaṁ bhagavante
    Instr./Abl. bhagavatā, bhagavantena bhagavantehi
    Gen./Dat. bhagavato bhagavataṁ, bhagavantānaṁ
    Loc. bhagavati bhagavantesu

    • … bhagavati pasādo. (SN 55.30 — S v 390)

    • … confidence in the Blessed One.

    • Mahantasmiṁ kho pana lokasannivāse santi samaṇabrāhmaṇā iddhimanto dibbacakkhukā paracittaviduno. (AN 3.40 — A i 148)

    • In this vast human society there are ascetics and brahmins with psychic power, with clairvoyance, who know the minds of others.

    The lower right of that table is thinner than it looks, and no case in the chapter more clearly shows a paradigm completed by rule. The -(n)t class largely has no oblique plural at all. Perniola says so directly: there are no forms for the instrumental, ablative or locative plural except sabbhi, the instrumental and ablative plural of sant ‘good, true’. The texts agree with him (bhagavantehi and bhagavantesu occur only in the grammarians’ texts, and bhagavantena nowhere at all), while sabbhi is thoroughly canonical, with 36 occurrences in the root texts.15 A reader will meet sabbhi in verse long before meeting the paradigm it belongs to.

    Stems in -in

    All words in -in are adjectives by origin (ātāpin ‘ardent’, sukhin ‘happy’, saññin ‘perceiving’), though some, like gihin ‘householder’, have settled into nouns. The stem is a possessive suffix: -in added to a noun makes an adjective meaning ‘having that thing’, so ātāpin is ‘having ardour’ and gihin ‘having a house’. Two related suffixes, -min and -vin, serve the same purpose: sassatasamin, medhāvin ‘having wisdom, wise’.16

    Case Sg. Pl.
    Nom. hatthī hatthino, hatthī
    Voc. hatthī hatthino
    Acc. hatthinaṁ hatthino, hatthī
    Instr./Abl. hatthinā hatthīhi
    Gen./Dat. hatthino hatthīnaṁ
    Loc. hatthini hatthīsu

    The nominative singular -ī is the point of difficulty, because it looks like a feminine ī-stem and is not one. Hatthī ‘elephant’ is masculine; so is bhikkhu’s near-neighbour dhammacārī ‘one who lives by the Dhamma’.

    • Ātāpino saṁvegino bhavātha. (Dhp 144 — Dhp p. 21)
    • Be ardent and full of urgency.

    Stems in -an

    A small class, and two of its members are among the most frequent words in the canon: rājan ‘king’ and attan ‘self’.

    Case rājan sg. rājan pl. attan sg.
    Nom. rājā rājāno attā
    Voc. rāja rājāno atta
    Acc. rājānaṁ rājāno attānaṁ
    Instr./Abl. raññā, rājinā rājūhi attanā
    Gen./Dat. rañño, rājino raññaṁ, rājūnaṁ attano
    Loc. raññe17 rājūsu attani

    The raññā / rañño forms are the ones to fix in memory, because they do not look as though they belong to rājā at all and they are everywhere.

    • Attā hi attano nātho. (Dhp 160 — Dhp p. 24)
    • Oneself is indeed one’s own protector.

    Others of this class are brahman ‘Brahmā’, yuvan ‘youth’, addhan ‘road’, puman ‘man’, and the neuters kamman ‘action’ and muddhan ‘head’.18 Most of these have gone over to the a-declension almost completely (kamma, kammaṁ, kammena), and their -an forms survive chiefly in verse and in compounds.

    Stems in -ar

    The -ar stems are two groups wearing one paradigm, and Perniola makes a distinction here that no other source consulted makes.

    Agent nouns (satthar ‘teacher’, dātar ‘giver’, netar ‘leader’, sotar ‘hearer’) are formed from verbs and mean ‘the one who does this’. Relationship nouns (pitar ‘father’, mātar ‘mother’, bhātar ‘brother’, dhītar ‘daughter’) are inherited kinship terms. They decline alike except in the accusative singular and the nominative, vocative and accusative plural, where the agent nouns have a long stem vowel and the relationship nouns a short one: satthāraṁ, satthāro against pitaraṁ, pitaro, mātaraṁ, mātaro.19

    Case satthar pitar mātar
    Nom./Voc. sg. satthā pitā mātā
    Acc. sg. satthāraṁ pitaraṁ mātaraṁ
    Instr./Abl. sg. satthārā pitarā mātarā
    Gen./Dat. sg. satthu pitu mātu
    Loc. sg. satthari pitari mātari
    Nom./Voc./Acc. pl. satthāro pitaro mātaro
    Instr./Abl. pl. satthūhi pitūhi mātūhi
    Gen./Dat. pl. satthūnaṁ pitūnaṁ mātūnaṁ
    Loc. pl. satthūsu pitūsu mātūsu

    • Punappunaṁ vaccho upeti mātaraṁ. (SN 7.12 — S i 174)

    • Again and again the calf goes to its mother.

    • … anuttaro purisadammasārathi satthā devamanussānaṁ. (DN 2 — D i 49)

    • … the unsurpassed trainer of those who can be trained, teacher of gods and humans.

    Stems in -as, -is and -us

    A dozen or so nouns end in -as: manas ‘mind’, cetas ‘mind, heart’, tapas ‘ardour’, tejas ‘fire, majesty’, yasas ‘fame’, vacas ‘speech’, siras ‘head’, ayas ‘iron’, vayas ‘age’, uras ‘breast’, jaras ‘old age’. Beside the -as stems stand a very few in -is and -us.

    Their distinguishing forms are a nominative and accusative in -o, and an oblique singular in -asā, -aso, -asi:

    Case Sg.
    Nom./Acc. mano
    Instr./Abl. manasā
    Gen./Dat. manaso
    Loc. manasi

    Every one of those is common. What is not stable is anything else about the class.

    These nouns are in the middle of changing declension, and their gender is disputed because of it. Alongside the forms above, the same words are declined throughout as ordinary neuter a-stems: manaṁ, manena, manasmā, manassa, manasmiṁ, manāni, manehi, manānaṁ, manesu. Oberlies gives the phenomenon its own section, transfer of °as- into a-stems, and it underlies the sources’ disagreement about gender. Perniola and Ñāṇatusita call the class neuter, which is what it was; the Digital Pāli Dictionary calls manas, cetas and tapas masculine, as a word whose nominative is mano looks once it has arrived in the a-declension. Oberlies’s formula (“mainly neuter nouns in -as, -is and -us, and very few masc./fem. as-nouns”) is the accurate one, and it is accurate partly because it declines to be tidy.20

    For a reader, the practical consequence is small: treat these words as neuter, expect masculine agreement occasionally, and do not be surprised by either.

    • Manasā ce paduṭṭhena, bhāsati vā karoti vā. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    • If with a corrupted mind one speaks or acts.

    • Mātā yathā niyaṁ puttam āyusā ekaputtamanurakkhe. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

    • As a mother would guard her own son, her only son, with her life.

    The second example above is a small lesson in reading a digital edition. Searched for as a word, āyusā finds nothing in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, which would ordinarily suggest a form built by grammarians, not used by anyone. It is nothing of the kind: the edition prints the line with the words run together as puttamāyusā, so a search for the word cannot see it, and the form sits in one of the best-known passages in the canon.21

    The word āyu ‘life, lifespan’ is itself the clearest case of the drift this section describes. It is historically a -us stem, and Oberlies flags its gender as an open question in the scholarship; in the canon it behaves as a neuter u-stem (āyunā, āyuno), with the older āyusā surviving in verse.22

    Root stems

    Finally, and mostly for recognition, not use: a handful of words in the canon are root nouns, in which the case ending was added directly to a verbal root with no stem-forming vowel between. Pāli has fragments of these and no complete paradigm.

    What survives is a scattering of individual forms, chiefly in verse: instrumental vācā ‘by speech’ and padā ‘with the foot’, locative parisati ‘in the assembly’, nominative plural āpo ‘waters’, genitive plural dvipadaṁ … catuppadaṁ ‘of two-footed and four-footed beings’.23 Met in isolation these look like errors, or like forms of some other word. They are neither, and knowing the class exists is enough.

    The ablative in -to

    One ending cuts across every class in the chapter, and it is easier to meet once than five times. Any noun can form an ablative singular in -to, whatever its stem: dūrato ‘from afar’, gharato ‘from the house’, gāmato ‘from the village’, dukkhato ‘from suffering’, rūpato ‘from form’, jātito ‘by birth’, kammato ‘by action’, aggito ‘from fire’, cakkhuto ‘from the eye’. The last two are the point of the suffix: an i-stem and a u-stem forming an ablative with the same ending an a-stem uses, so it is met here as one fact, not as one fact per declension.24

    There is a companion to it in -so, no longer really a case ending at all but a maker of adverbs: yoniso ‘thoroughly, from the root’, dhātuso ‘according to nature’, hetuso ‘by way of cause’, mattaso ‘in moderation’. Anyone who has met yoniso manasikāra ‘proper attention’ before meeting any grammar has already met this suffix.

    What belongs elsewhere

    Three subjects belonging to the noun are treated elsewhere on purpose. What the cases mean (why an instrumental and not a locative, what a dative of purpose expresses) is the chapter on case syntax. How gender is assigned, and how a feminine stem is formed from a masculine one, is the next chapter, along with the adjectives, which share every ending in this chapter and add agreement. And the complete tables, with every rare and poetic variant that has been left out here, are in Appendix A.1.
