    Chapter 18

    Metre

    What a verse is made of

    The canon contains one short discussion of what a verse is, and this chapter has to start there, because it names the two things the chapter is about. A deity comes to the Buddha at night and asks four questions about verses; the Buddha answers all four in a single verse.

    Kiṁsu nidānaṁ gāthānaṁ, kiṁsu tāsaṁ viyañjanaṁ; Kiṁsu sannissitā gāthā, kiṁsu gāthānam-āsayo ti. (SN 1.60 — S i 38)

    kiṁsuwhatnidānaṁsource, originn nom sggāthānaṁof versesf gen pltāsaṁof themf gen plviyañjanaṁdistinguishing markn nom sgsannissitāresting on, dependentf nom plāsayodwelling-place, homem nom sg

    What is the source of verses? What is their distinguishing mark? What do verses rest on? What is the home of verses?

    Chando nidānaṁ gāthānaṁ, akkharā tāsaṁ viyañjanaṁ; Nāma-sannissitā gāthā, kavi gāthānam-āsayo ti. (SN 1.60 — S i 38)

    chandometren nom sgakkharāsyllablesn nom plnāmaname, nounnkavithe poetm nom sg

    Metre is the source of verses, syllables their distinguishing mark; verses rest on names, and the poet is the home of verses.

    Two of those four answers are the subject of this chapter. Chandas, “metre”, is what a verse comes out of; akkharā, “syllables”, are what marks it off from prose. Buddhaghosa, glossing the first of them, does not stop at the abstraction: chando nidānan-ti gāyattiādiko chando gāthānaṁ nidānaṁ, “‘metre is the source’ means: metre (the Gāyattī and the rest) is the source of verses”.1 He answers a question about metre in general by naming a particular metre, and the one he names is Vedic. The tradition understood well that Pāli verse sits inside a much older Indian practice of counting syllables.

    The canon does not name its own metres. The Vuttodaya says so itself, as a point of information, not a complaint: having set out how the technical names work, Saṅgharakkhita adds that ‘gāthā’ icceva niddiṭṭhaṁ, munindavacane pana, “in the word of the Lord of Sages, however, it is pointed out simply as gāthā”.2 A verse in the canon is a gāthā, with no more particular name.

    There is one exception. At the opening of the Mahāsamaya Sutta the Buddha announces what he is about to do:

    Bhagavā etad-avoca: “Silokam-anukassāmi, yattha bhummā tad-assitā.” (DN 20 — D ii 255)

    bhagavāthe Blessed Oneetad-avocasaid thissilokaṁa siloka, a versem acc sganukassāmiI shall recite1 sg futyatthawherebhummāthe earth-dwelling (gods)m nom pltad-assitādependent on thatm nom pl

    The Blessed One said this: “I shall recite a siloka, of where the earth-dwelling gods have gathered.”

    Siloka is the name of a metre (the commonest metre in the canon, and the one described below), and nowhere else in the four nikāyas is a metre named at all. The gloss is not obvious, because siloka has a second and much commoner sense, “fame, praise”, which is what it carries in the stock phrase lābhasakkārasiloka, “gain, honour and praise”. The commentary settles which sense is meant here, in the most useful possible way, by defining the word: silokam-anukassāmī-ti akkharapadaniyamitaṁ vacanasaṅghātaṁ pavattayissāmi, “I shall recite a siloka means: I shall set going an assemblage of words regulated by syllable and by line”.3

    That is a definition of metrical composition in three words, and it is a good one. Prose is words in an order; verse is words in an order that a count constrains. Everything else in this chapter is an account of which counts, and of what happens to words when a count is imposed on them.

    Why a grammar has a chapter on this

    The obvious reason is quantity. Verse is a large part of the canon’s bulk, not an ornamental corner of it, and some of the most-read texts in it are verse throughout. Ānandajoti’s estimate, from the PTS editions, puts the Sutta Piṭaka alone well past twenty thousand verses:

    Collection Verses Collection Verses
    Dīghanikāya 280+ Suttanipāta 1,149
    Majjhimanikāya 230+ Vimānavatthu 1,291
    Saṁyuttanikāya 1,000+ Petavatthu 823
    Aṅguttaranikāya 570+ Theragāthā 1,279
    Khuddakapāṭha 72 Therīgāthā 522
    Dhammapada 423 Jātaka 6,905
    Udāna 77 Apadāna 5,228
    Itivuttaka 263 Buddhavaṁsa 960+

    The less obvious reason matters more for this book. Metre causes some of the forms this grammar has spent seventeen chapters describing. A poet who needs a light syllable where the language offers a heavy one will lengthen a vowel, shorten a vowel, double a consonant, simplify a conjunct, or drop a niggahīta, and the result is a form that looks like an error to a reader who has learnt the paradigms and does not know why it is there. Geiger files his whole treatment of metre under phonology for this reason: for a grammarian, the interesting thing about metre is what it does to words.4

    So this chapter has two audiences in one reader. It is an introduction to Pāli prosody, and it is the place where a number of loose ends from earlier chapters are finally tied off.

    Light and heavy, short and long

    Everything begins with a distinction that is easy to state and easy to blur, and blurring it makes the rest of the subject incoherent. Vowels are short or long. Syllables are light or heavy. These are different properties, and a syllable with a short vowel is very often heavy.

    The tradition keeps four separate words for the four terms, and they should be learnt as two pairs:

    Of a vowel Of a syllable
    rassa short — a i u lahu light
    dīgha long — ā ī ū garu heavy

    The Vuttodaya states the whole rule for syllabic weight in half a verse, and states it as a list of three conditions:

    Saṁyogādi ca, dīgho ca, niggahītaparo ca, yo; garu.

    That which stands at the head of a conjunct, or is long, or has the niggahīta after it: heavy. (Vuttodaya 7)

    Unpacked, and put the way a reader will use it:

    1. A syllable whose vowel is long — ā, ī, ū — is heavy.
    2. A syllable whose vowel is followed by a conjunct, two or more consonants, is heavy. This is called making position, and the conjunct need not be in the same word: a word ending in a short vowel before a word beginning tv- or br- has a heavy final syllable.
    3. A syllable whose vowel is followed by the niggahīta -ṁ is heavy.
    4. Anything else — a short vowel a, i, u with at most one consonant after it — is light.

    E and o are the awkward pair, and they are awkward because they are long by origin. They count as heavy, and they count as heavy whether the syllable is open or closed. Where a conjunct follows they are pronounced short (upekkhā has a shorter e than upekhā does), but the syllable is heavy either way, once for the vowel and once for the conjunct. There is no position in which e or o is light by rule.5

    The whole rule fits in a small grid. Read it as: what is the vowel, and what follows it?

    Vowel Open syllable — nothing, or one consonant, after it Closed syllable — a conjunct or -ṁ after it
    short a i u light ⏑ heavy —
    variable e o heavy — heavy —
    long ā ī ū heavy — heavy —

    Only the top-left cell is light. That is the whole of the rule: everything after this is either an exception to it or a pattern built out of it.

    The two marks, and a warning about them

    A light syllable is written ⏑ and a heavy one —. This is the European convention and the one used throughout this book.

    The Indian tradition uses the opposite convention, and the reversal is total: a straight stroke marks the light syllable and a bent one the heavy. The Vuttodaya says so in the same verse that defines the terms, in two words: the heavy syllable is vaṅka, “bent”, and the light one uju, “straight”.6 So a Devanāgarī or Sinhala treatment of a metre may print what looks to a European eye like the inverse of the pattern it means. The signs are not international, and there is no way to tell from the marks alone which system is in use; the only defence is to check a syllable one already knows.

    Scanning a verse

    The method is mechanical and should be done mechanically at first. Divide the line into syllables (each syllable is one vowel, with the consonants that follow it up to the next vowel), then apply the four rules above. The first verse of the Maṅgalasutta is a good place to start, because it is short and familiar, and contains almost every case:

    ⏑ — — — ¦ ⏑ — — ×
    a Ba hū de vā ma nus sā ca
     
    — ⏑ — ⏑ ¦ ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    b maṅ ga lā ni a cin ta yuṁ
     
    — — ⏑ — ¦ — — — ×
    c Ā kaṅ kha mā nā sot thā naṁ
     
    — ⏑ — ⏑ ¦ ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    d brū hi maṅ ga la mut ta maṁ

    Bahū devā manussā ca, maṅgalāni acintayuṁ; Ākaṅkhamānā sotthānaṁ, brūhi maṅgalam-uttamaṁ. (Kp 5)

    bahūmanym nom pldevāgodsmanussāhumansmaṅgalāniblessingsn acc placintayuṁhave thought about3 pl aorākaṅkhamānādesiringpres part, m nom plsotthānaṁwell-beingn gen plbrūhideclare!2 sg impmaṅgalam-uttamaṁthe highest blessingn acc sg

    Many gods and humans have thought about blessings, desiring well-being: declare the highest blessing.

    Every mark in that grid comes from one of the four rules, and each of them can be named. Ba- is light: short a, one consonant after it. -hū is heavy for its long vowel; so are de-, -vā, -sā, -lā, -mā, -nā, brū-, -thā. Nus- is heavy because -ss- is a conjunct, and so are maṅ- (-ṅg-), cin- (-nt-), kaṅ-, sot- (-tth-), mut-. Yuṁ, naṁ and maṁ are heavy for the niggahīta. A- at the start of acintayuṁ is light although a conjunct follows two letters later, because what immediately follows it is the single c. And ca at the end of line a is naturally light and is marked heavy anyway, for a reason given in a moment.

    Two features of that verse are habits, not rules, and both come back later. Brū- begins with a conjunct, and a conjunct at the start of a line has nothing before it to make heavy; the weight of br- only ever matters to the syllable in front of it. And the caesura marked ¦ after the fourth syllable of each line is a word break, not a pause, and the tradition’s name for it says so: padaccheda, “the cutting of words”, is the Vuttodaya’s gloss on yati.7 Reading it as a pause produces a sing-song recitation that no reciting tradition uses.

    The last syllable of a line

    The final syllable of a pāda is marked heavy whatever its nature. This is a convention, not a claim about the sound, and it reflects something real: a line-end is a place where a reciter can dwell, so a light syllable there is drawn out and the difference stops mattering. Ānandajoti marks that syllable × instead of —, to record that the weight is imposed, not found, and this book follows him.8 The Vuttodaya states the same rule in the same verse as the others: heavy, “or [being] at the end of a line”.

    So of the eight syllables in line a above, seven are weighed and one is declared, and so ca is marked ×.

    What disturbs the count

    Three features regularly make a line refuse to scan when the four rules are applied to it as written. In all three the spelling and the sound have come apart, and knowing them saves a great deal of time.

    Aspirates are single consonants. In Roman script kh, gh, ch, jh, ṭh, ḍh, th, dh, ph, bh are written with two letters and are one sound each; ḷh is the same, and it looks least like a single sound. None of them makes position. So sukhaṁ is ⏑— and not ——: the -u- is followed by a single consonant, and the syllable is open. Compare sukkaṁ, ——, where -kk- is a true conjunct. This is purely an artefact of the alphabet the book is printed in: the Sinhala, Burmese and Thai scripts write each aspirate with one character and the question does not arise.

    Some conjuncts fail to make position. The regular offender is br-, which reliably leaves the preceding syllable light in brahma-, brāhmaṇa-, brūti and anubrūhaye, although br does make position elsewhere in the middle of a word. Tvaṁ, dvāra and nhātaka sometimes fail in the same way, and by- (also written vy-) fails quite often. Beyond that list the cases are doubtful and should be treated as doubtful.9

    Sarabhatti vowels are written and not counted. Some words carry a sarabhatti (Sanskrit svarabhakti), a “broken” or partial vowel inserted between two consonants that would otherwise form a conjunct, most often separating two semi-vowels, or a semi-vowel from an aspirate, nasal or sibilant. When such a word is scanned, the inserted vowel is ignored, and ignoring it restores the conjunct, which then makes position. So the effect is to make the word shorter by one syllable and heavier at the join, not merely shorter by one light syllable. The common ones are ariya (scanned ar-ya, —⏑), iriyati (—⏑⏑), cariya (—⏑), viriya (—⏑), araha (—⏑) and kayira (—⏑).

    The tenth verse of the same Maṅgalasutta needs two of these three at once, which makes it the best single illustration in the chapter. Applied literally, the four rules give nine syllables in the wrong shape:

    ⏑ — — — ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ — ×
    Ta po cab rah ma ca ri yañ ca

    That is one too many for a Siloka line, and the pattern belongs to no metre. Notice also where the third syllable has landed: with br- making position, the b is dragged back to close ca, and the division falls inside the word. Now release br-, so that ca stays light and the cluster goes wholly to the next syllable, and drop the sarabhatti vowel of cariya, so that it is car-ya in two:

    ⏑ — ⏑ — ¦ ⏑ — — ×
    Ta po ca brah ma car yañ ca

    Tapo ca brahmacariyañ-ca, ariyasaccāna’ dassanaṁ; Nibbānasacchikiriyā ca, etaṁ maṅgalam-uttamaṁ. (Kp 5)

    tapoausterityn nom sgbrahmacariyaṁthe spiritual lifen nom sgariyasaccānaṁof the noble truthsn gen pldassanaṁseeingn nom sgnibbānasacchikiriyāthe realisation of nibbānaf nom sgetaṁthisn nom sgmaṅgalam-uttamaṁthe highest blessing

    Austerity and the spiritual life, seeing the noble truths, and the realisation of nibbāna: this is the highest blessing.

    Eight syllables, and the pattern a Siloka odd line should have.

    The second line needs two accommodations of its own, and each does separate work. Ariyasaccāna’ has a sarabhatti vowel like cariya, and it has also lost the niggahīta of ariyasaccānaṁ:

    — ⏑ — — ¦ ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    ar ya sac cā na das sa naṁ

    Keep the sarabhatti vowel and the line has nine syllables. Keep the niggahīta and it has eight, but closes ——⏑× where the Siloka’s even line requires ⏑—⏑×. Neither accommodation can replace the other. That makes four separate departures from the written text in two lines, in an ordinary verse, not a difficult one.

    Metre as the cause

    Pāli verse was composed in a language that offered alternatives, and the composer took whichever alternative fitted. Some of this is ordinary choice among forms the language already had, not licence at all: the genitive plural of an -i stem can be -inaṁ or -īnaṁ, and a poet needing a heavy syllable simply uses the second. Ānandajoti calls this fluidity, and it should be kept apart from what follows, because no form is being bent.10

    Beyond that, forms are altered. The changes available are few, and they are applied at predictable places: end syllables most often, medial syllables only at a junction between the parts of a compound or between stem and ending, initial syllables rarely.

    Vowels are lengthened or shortened. This is the commonest change of all, and the lengthening of a final -i is commoner than everything else together.

    Consonants are doubled or simplified. A single consonant doubled closes the preceding syllable and makes it heavy; a conjunct simplified opens it and makes it light. The result is the same as changing the vowel, and the two devices are interchangeable.

    The niggahīta is dropped. This is the most visible of the three, because the reader can see the apostrophe where the editor has marked it. The final line of Dhp 183 is the standard example, and the dropped nasal can be shown to matter:

    — — — — ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    e taṁ bud dhā na sā sa naṁ

    Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna’ sāsanaṁ. (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

    sabbapāpassaof all eviln gen sgakaraṇaṁnot-doingn nom sgkusalassaof the wholesomen gen sgupasampadāundertakingf nom sgsacittapariyodapanaṁthe cleansing of one’s own mindn nom sgbuddhānaṁof the Buddhasm gen plsāsanaṁthe teachingn nom sg

    Not doing any evil, undertaking the wholesome, cleansing one’s own mind: this is the teaching of the Buddhas.

    The word is buddhānaṁ, genitive plural. Written in full, the line scans ——————⏑×, and its last four syllables are ——⏑×. The Siloka’s even line requires the cadence ⏑—⏑× and no other; ——⏑× is not a possible ending. Drop the niggahīta, and -na is light, and the cadence is ⏑—⏑×. One nasal decides whether the most quoted verse in the Dhammapada is in metre or out of it.

    Where the licence is the editor’s

    One case ought to be raised here, not left for a reader to find, because it changes what a metrical licence is evidence of.

    Ānandajoti’s one example of a variable vowel scanned light in an open syllable comes from the Ratanasutta, and turns on the word abhabbo, “unable”. He prints abhabbŏ with a breve, and explains that its final -o must be read light against its nature. The licence is necessary: scan his text as written, with -o heavy, and the line closes ———×, neither a Tuṭṭhubha cadence nor any other. Read the -o light and the cadence is —⏑—×, as it should be. On his text the licence is what makes the line scan at all.

    But the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana does not print abhabbo. It prints abhabba, with a short -a that is light without any licence at all, and it records abhabbo in its own apparatus against the siglum sī., the Sinhalese edition. The Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print abhabbo, so the licence is a fact about the edition in front of the scanner:

    Catūh’-apāyehi ca vippamutto, Chaccābhiṭhānāni abhabba kātuṁ; Idam-pi saṅghe ratanaṁ paṇītaṁ, Etena saccena suvatthi hotu. (Kp 6)

    catūhi apāyehifrom the four states of lossm abl plvippamuttofreedpp, m nom sgchasixabhiṭhānāniheinous deedsn acc plabhabbaunablem nom sgkātuṁto doinfsaṅghein the Saṅgham loc sgratanaṁjeweln nom sgpaṇītaṁsublimeetena saccenaby this truthn ins sgsuvatthi hotumay there be well-being

    Freed from the four states of loss, unable to do the six heinous deeds: this too is the sublime jewel in the Saṅgha; by this truth, may there be well-being.

    Both readings scan, and both give a correct Tuṭṭhubha, because the first syllable of the line is anceps and takes either weight:

    Reading Scansion Licence needed
    Cha chābhiṭhānāni abhabbŏ kātuṁ (Ce, Ee) ⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—× the -o scanned light
    Chaccābhiṭhānāni abhabba kātuṁ (Be) ——⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—× none

    So the licence is a consequence of an editorial choice, not a fact about the verse, and the other choice removes it. The caution generalises: a claim that a poet shortened a vowel is always also a claim about which edition one is reading, and the apparatus should be consulted before the licence is asserted. The same holds in reverse: an edition that prints a metrically comfortable form may have smoothed a difficulty that the manuscripts preserve.

    Where the sources need care. Ānandajoti also says of this line that “the double consonant in abhiṭṭhānāni has been simplified to meet the requirements of the metre”. Abhiṭṭhānāni appears in none of the three romanised editions, and DPD lexicalises the word as abhiṭhāna, from abhi + √ṭhā + -ana, with a single ṭh. The Sanskrit abhiṣṭhāna would regularly give Pāli abhiṭṭhāna, so the simplification is real in the word’s history; what is not shown is that it happened in this verse. It seems safer to treat abhiṭhāna as simply the Pāli form of the word.

    Two syllables for one

    Behind everything in this chapter lies an equivalence that Pāli verse takes for granted: two light syllables are worth one heavy, ⏑⏑ = —. It follows that a metre can be satisfied in two ways at any given position, and both directions are used.

    Resolution replaces one heavy syllable with two light ones. It is common, particularly at the start of a line, and it explains why a line that should have eight syllables sometimes has nine without ceasing to be in metre. The constraint is that resolution is only allowed across the first two syllables of a word (including a word standing as the second member of a compound, or following a prefix), so a run of two light syllables in the middle of a word cannot be a resolution. The negative particle na is the only regular exception, presumably because of how tightly it binds to what it modifies.

    Replacement is the converse: two light syllables where the metre expects them are replaced by one heavy syllable, giving a line one syllable short. It is much rarer. And it obeys the mirror image of the same constraint: it happens after a word break, which shows that the two light syllables being replaced were themselves the first two of a word.

    The rule of resolution is more useful than it looks, because it is a diagnostic. When a line has an extra syllable and will not fit any pattern, finding where the resolution is often identifies the metre; and in the Siloka it can decide which variation one is looking at. The author of the Buddhavaṁsa was a considerable master of the device, and that text is where to go to see it worked hard.

    The four kinds of metre

    Pāli metres are built on two different principles, and each of the two divides again, giving four families. This classification is Ānandajoti’s, and it is the most useful one available because it is organised by how a line is constructed, not by how many syllables it happens to have.11

    Family Pāli The line is fixed by Examples
    Flexible syllabic vaṇṇacchandas (1) the number of syllables, with the cadence fixed and much of the rest free Siloka, Tuṭṭhubha, Jagatī
    Measure mattāchandas the number of measures, the syllable count varying Vetālīya, Opacchandasaka, Āpātalikā
    Bar gaṇacchandas measures grouped into bars of four Ariyā, Gīti, Gubbinī
    Fixed syllabic vaṇṇacchandas (2) the weight of nearly every syllable Upajāti, Vaṁsaṭṭhā, Rathoddhatā

    In the syllabic metres one counts syllables: the Siloka has eight to the line, the Tuṭṭhubha eleven, the Jagatī twelve. In the measure metres one counts mattā, measures, a light syllable counting one and a heavy two; the totals are fixed and the syllable count is not. The bar metres add a second layer of organisation on top of the measure count, gathering the measures into gaṇas of four (which may be ——, ⏑—⏑, ⏑⏑—, —⏑⏑ or ⏑⏑⏑⏑), and the effect is much more like a bar of music than a foot of verse. The fixed metres are the late arrivals, and are the flexible ones with the freedom taken out.

    Almost all of the canon a reader will meet is in the first family, and much the largest part of that is in one metre.

    A word about feet

    Older grammars describe Pāli metres with the eight trisyllabic gaṇas of classical Indian theory, each named by a letter: ma ———, na ⏑⏑⏑, bha —⏑⏑, ya ⏑——, ja ⏑—⏑, ra —⏑—, ta ——⏑, sa ⏑⏑—. The Vuttodaya opens with them and Duroiselle’s chapter is built on them.12 They are a compact shorthand and there is nothing wrong with them as such.

    They are avoided in this chapter, for a reason Ānandajoti gives and demonstrates: they cut the line in the wrong places, and so invent a rhythm the metre does not have. Described in gaṇas, the Indavajirā is ta ta ja ga ga, which written out is ——⏑¦——⏑¦⏑—⏑¦——. That grouping suggests a repeating three-beat figure. The metre’s actual pattern is ——⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑——, an opening, a break and a cadence, and it sounds nothing like the first. Where the gaṇas truly reflect the structure, in the bar metres, where they are bars, they are used below.

    The Siloka

    The Siloka is the most prevalent metre in the canon: the metre of the Dhammapada, of the narrative verse of the Suttanipāta, and of the great bulk of verse embedded in prose suttas. It is flexible enough to carry aphorism, dialogue, narrative and epic, and once you can hear it you have most of the canon’s verse in hand.

    A Siloka verse is four lines of eight syllables, arranged in dissimilar pairs: an odd or prior line and an even or posterior line, the pair repeated. The normal form of each, the pathyā, is:

    Pattern
    Odd (prior) line [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓ ⏓ ⏓ ¦ ⏑ — — ×
    Even (posterior) line [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓ ⏓ ⏓ ¦ ⏑ — ⏑ ×

    The first four syllables are essentially free (the first may even be resolved into two light ones, as the bracket records), and the last four are the metre. Everything that identifies a Siloka line lies in its second half. The odd line ends ⏑——, the even line ends ⏑—⏑, and the difference between them is one syllable in seventh position. That opposition makes the verse feel like a couplet, not a list.

    One further constraint on the opening explains a good many forms: two successive light syllables in second and third position are normally avoided, and one occasionally catches a poet changing a word’s weight to avoid them. The Dhammapada is not consistent about this.

    The Maṅgalasutta verse scanned above is a Siloka, and it deserves a second look now that the pattern is known:

    Line Scansion
    a ⏑———¦⏑——× pathyā — the cadence ⏑—— is the odd line’s
    b —⏑—⏑¦⏑—⏑× the even line’s cadence ⏑—⏑
    c ——⏑—¦———× not pathyā — see below
    d —⏑—⏑¦⏑—⏑× the even line again

    The seven variations

    An odd line may depart from the pathyā in seven recognised ways, and each has a name taken from the gaṇa that characterises it. They are given here as a table, to consult when a line will not fit, not to memorise:

    Name Pattern Also called
    pathyā (the normal form) [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓⏓⏓ ¦ ⏑——×
    javipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓⏓⏓ ¦ ⏑—⏑× Anuṭṭhubha
    navipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏓— ¦ ⏑⏑⏑× 1st vipulā
    bhavipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏓— ¦ —⏑⏑× 2nd vipulā
    mavipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏓— ¦ ———× 3rd vipulā
    ravipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓⏓⏓ ¦ —⏑—× 4th vipulā
    savipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓⏓⏓ ¦ ⏑⏑—× 5th vipulā
    tavipulā [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏑— ¦ ——⏑× 6th vipulā, very sporadic

    Line c of the Maṅgalasutta verse is a mavipulā: syllables two to four are —⏑—, and the cadence is ———×. It even has the word break the mavipulā normally takes after the fifth syllable, at Ākaṅkhamānā | sotthānaṁ. So the opening verse of one of the best-known suttas in the canon contains a named variation, and someone who had learnt only the pathyā would have concluded that the line was corrupt.

    The ravipulā takes its break after the fourth syllable instead, and that difference matters. A line running ⏓—⏑—⏑⏑⏑—× is ambiguous on the marks alone; the caesura decides it. Break after the fourth and it is ravipulā; break after the fifth and it is a pathyā whose sixth syllable has been resolved.

    The proportions changed over time, which makes the Siloka a rough dating instrument. In the earlier texts the pathyā accounts for about sixty per cent of odd lines and the javipulā (the variation that makes the odd line look like an even one) for about fifteen. By the end of the canonical period the javipulā has all but disappeared and the pathyā can account for eighty-five per cent. The questions and answers of the Pārāyanavagga and the introductory verses prefixed to them sit on opposite sides of that shift and can be compared directly.

    What the metre is called

    This book calls it the Siloka, because that is the name in general use and the name the reader will meet everywhere else. It should be said that the tradition calls it something else. The Vuttodaya’s section is headed Vattappabhedo, “the varieties of Vatta”, and treats pathyāvatta and capalāvatta as its kinds; vatta stands six times in the treatise and siloka not once. Duroiselle, following the Vuttodaya, also calls it Vatta. Ānandajoti used Vatta in his first edition and changed to Siloka in his second, on the ground that siloka is the word the Buddha uses at DN 20, as the opening of this chapter showed it is. Both names are defensible; they answer different questions, one about the canon and one about the commentarial tradition that analysed it.

    Tuṭṭhubha and Jagatī

    The second family of flexible syllabic metres is a pair, and they are best learnt together because the second is the first with one syllable added.

    Pattern Syllables
    Tuṭṭhubha [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏑— ¦ ⏓⏑⏓ ¦ —⏑—× 11
    Jagatī [⏑/—/⏑⏑] —⏑— ¦ ⏓⏑⏓ ¦ —⏑—⏑× 12

    The line has three parts, and the names for them are useful: an opening of four syllables, a break of three, and a cadence of four (five in the Jagatī). The opening is normally ⏓—⏑—; the break is normally —⏑⏑, which accounts for about eighty-five per cent of cases; the cadence is the fixed part. The Jagatī adds its extra light syllable in the penultimate position, so the two metres differ only in the pattern of the ending.

    The opening of the Aṭṭhakavagga’s Guhaṭṭhakasutta is early Tuṭṭhubha, and shows both the normal line and one of the common departures:

    — — ⏑ — ¦ — ⏑ ⏑ ¦ — ⏑ — ×
    a Sat to gu hā yaṁ ba hu nā bhi chan no
     
    — — ⏑ — ¦ — ⏑ — ¦ — ⏑ — ×
    b Tiṭ ṭhaṁ na ro mo ha nas miṁ pa gā ḷho

    Satto guhāyaṁ bahunābhichanno, Tiṭṭhaṁ naro mohanasmiṁ pagāḷho; Dūre vivekā hi tathāvidho so, Kāmā hi loke na hi suppahāyā. (Snp 4.2 — Sn p. 151)

    sattoattached, stuckpp, m nom sgguhāyaṁin a cavef loc sgbahunāby muchn ins sgabhichannocovered overpp, m nom sgtiṭṭhaṁstandingpres part, m nom sgnaroa personm nom sgmohanasmiṁin delusionn loc sgpagāḷhosunkpp, m nom sgdūrefaradvvivekāfrom seclusionm abl sgtathāvidhoof such a kindkāmāsensual pleasuresm nom plsuppahāyāeasy to give upm nom pl

    A person stuck in a cave, thickly covered over, sunk in delusion, stands far from seclusion — for sensual pleasures in the world are not easy to give up.

    Line a is textbook: opening ——⏑—, break —⏑⏑, cadence —⏑—×. Line b has the break —⏑— instead, one of the documented alternatives, and is otherwise identical. Hold the three-part pattern in mind and the substitution is heard as a variation, not a fault.

    The two metres are mixed freely. A Jagatī line inside an otherwise Tuṭṭhubha verse was always acceptable, and in the middle period the mixture is so ordinary that it is better to speak of a single Tuṭṭhubha-Jagatī metre. The Ratanasutta does it in consecutive lines:

    ⏑ — ⏑ — ¦ — ⏑ ⏑ ¦ — ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    Ya thin da khī lo pa tha vis si to si yā Jagatī

    Yath’-indakhīlo pathavis-sito siyā, Catubbhi vātehi asampakampiyo; Tath’-ūpamaṁ sappurisaṁ vadāmi, Yo ariyasaccāni avecca passati. (Kp 6)

    yathāasindakhīloIndra’s pillar, the boundary postm nom sgpathavissitoset in the earthm nom sgsiyāwould be3 sg optcatubbhi vātehiby the four windsm ins plasampakampiyonot to be shakenf.p.p., m nom sgtathūpamaṁcomparable to thatm acc sgsappurisaṁthe true personm acc sgvadāmiI call1 sg presaveccahaving penetratedabspassatisees

    As Indra’s pillar, set in the earth, would be unshakeable by the four winds, so, I say, is the true person who sees the noble truths with penetration.

    The first, second and fourth lines are Jagatī, twelve syllables; the third is Tuṭṭhubha, eleven. Only the count marks the switch, and no more is needed.

    In the later canon both metres harden into fixed forms with the flexibility removed: the Tuṭṭhubha becomes Upajāti, ⏓—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×, and the Jagatī becomes Vaṁsaṭṭhā, ⏓—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—⏑×. These are simply the normal forms of their parents with the alternatives disallowed. Tālaputta’s verses at Th 1091–1145 use both, separately and mixed, and are the place to hear the difference.

    The measure metres

    The second principle counts mattā, not syllables. A light syllable is one measure and a heavy one two, so a fixed total can be reached by many different arrangements, and lines occupying the same position in the verse vary in syllable count while coming out to the same length. The total and the pattern of the cadence distinguish one measure metre from another; the openings are variable but come in groups of two measures.

    The Vetālīya has fourteen measures in the odd lines and sixteen in the even, with the cadence ¦—⏑—⏑× in both. The Opacchandasaka has sixteen and eighteen, with one extra heavy syllable in the cadence, ¦—⏑—⏑—×. The Āpātalikā has the same totals as the Vetālīya and a different cadence, —⏑⏑—×.

    The Jarāsutta opens in Vetālīya, and counting the measures out shows the principle at work:

    — — ⏑ ⏑ ¦ — ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    Ap paṁ va ta jī vi taṁ i daṁ
     
    — — — ⏑ ⏑ ¦ — ⏑ — ⏑ ×
    O raṁ vas sa sa tā pi miy ya ti

    Counted in measures, the first line is 2+2+1+1 + 2+1+2+1+2 = 14 and the second is 2+2+2+1+1 + 2+1+2+1+2 = 16.

    Appaṁ vata jīvitaṁ idaṁ, Oraṁ vassasatā-pi miyyati; Yo ce-pi aticca jīvati, Atha kho so jarasā-pi miyyati. (Snp 4.6 — Sn p. 158)

    appaṁlittlen nom sgvataindeedjīvitaṁlifen nom sgidaṁthisoraṁshort of, withinvassasatāthan a hundred yearsn abl sgmiyyatione diesyo ce pieven if oneaticcahaving gone beyondabsjīvatilivesatha khothen indeedjarasāby old agef ins sg

    Short indeed is this life; one dies short of a hundred years. And even one who lives beyond it dies all the same, of old age.

    The odd line has nine syllables and the even has ten, and each comes out to the total its own position requires: fourteen measures and sixteen. The two lines are not the same length as each other, and the Vetālīya does not ask them to be. What the measure count fixes is that every odd line in the metre will be fourteen and every even line sixteen, however many syllables the poet spends getting there. Note that the last syllable of the even line is light and counts two anyway: the pādantagaru rule described earlier, without which every even line in every measure metre comes out one short.

    The bar metres

    The bar metres take the measure count and add a second organisation on top of it, grouping the measures into gaṇas of four. A four-measure bar can be filled in five ways (——, ⏑—⏑, ⏑⏑—, —⏑⏑, ⏑⏑⏑⏑), and the rhythm of these metres comes from the alternation of two of those patterns. The effect is musical, and the traditional grouping into bars describes how the lines move instead of imposing an analysis on them.

    The Ariyā is the common one, with eight bars to each half-verse and totals of thirty measures in the first and twenty-seven in the second; the Gīti repeats the first half-verse instead, giving thirty and thirty; the Uggīti and Upagīti are the other two permutations. An older and looser form, Old Gīti, is probably transitional between the measure metres and these, and it is the metre of the Mettāsutta and of the Tuvaṭakasutta.

    These metres are considerably harder to analyse than anything above, for two reasons that compound each other: resolution can turn any bar into ⏑⏑⏑⏑, and the line divisions printed in the editions do not always fall where the pādayugas do. There are only about four hundred and fifty verses in the whole canon in bar metres. With the Siloka and the Tuṭṭhubha secure you have the great majority of the verse, and can reasonably leave these to a second pass with Ānandajoti’s descriptions to hand.

    When a line will not scan

    Three answers, in the order to try them.

    The text may need one of the accommodations already described: a sarabhatti vowel to ignore, a conjunct that does not make position, a dropped niggahīta, a resolution. Most difficulties are here.

    The metre may have changed mid-verse. Mixing is one of the most characteristic features of canonical Pāli verse, and it happens within the verse and not only between verses. Metres built on the same principle mix most freely: Tuṭṭhubha with Jagatī, as shown above; Vetālīya with Opacchandasaka, where the rule seems to be that the Vetālīya takes the odd lines and the Opacchandasaka the even. Even the Siloka, which is structurally unlike all of them, turns up alongside other metres constantly, usually with its odd and even lines in their proper positions, which suggests the mixing was done with the Siloka’s own shape in mind. A verse with one Tuṭṭhubha line and three Siloka lines is not a corruption.

    The line may simply be wrong. Ānandajoti’s §1.12 is a single sentence, and it may be the most useful sentence in his book:

    It may come as a surprise that when so many changes are considered to be permissible, quite often the expected change does not in fact take place, even in cases where it appears to be easy to do so, and the metre is simply left “wrong” according to the norms that otherwise prevail.

    The permissible alterations are available, not obligatory. A composer could have shortened a vowel and did not; a reciter transmitted what he received. Geiger’s warning belongs here (the orthography should not be altered for the sake of the metre), and it is the right instinct. Where an edition prints a line that does not scan, the useful response is to note it, consult the apparatus, and leave it alone.

    Two smaller matters belong under the same heading, because both look like metrical faults and neither is. The quotation marker ti at the end of a verse normally stands outside the metre and is not counted; it is a mark of reported speech, not part of the line. And the so-called reciter’s remarks (interjections like iti Bhagavā, “so said the Blessed One”, or iti Dhaniyo gopo, “so said Dhaniya the herdsman”) are also outside the metre, and are presumed to have been added by reciters to keep track of who is speaking. A line with one of these embedded in it will be hypermetrical by exactly the length of the remark. Remove it and the line scans.

    Where to go next

    Scan first the verse a reader already knows by heart, because the ear catches what the eye misses. The Maṅgalasutta, the Ratanasutta and the Karaṇīyamettasutta are short, memorised by most people who recite in Pāli at all, and between them cover the Siloka, the Tuṭṭhubha-Jagatī and the Old Gīti. After those, the Dhammapada is largely Siloka with two short runs of Vetālīya (verses 15–18 and 235–238), so it is the best place to become fluent in one metre and then meet a second by contrast.

    The Lakkhaṇasutta, DN 30, is a curiosity that is also useful. It is a late text and a display piece: twenty sets of verses in seven different metres, several of which occur nowhere else in the canon, and Ānandajoti tabulates every one of them by paragraph.13 If a metre is rare, that is generally where it is.

    For anything beyond this chapter, go to Ānandajoti’s Outline, which is short. Warder’s Pāli Metre (1967) remains the fullest study of the subject, and the historical argument is made there. Norman’s translations of the Theragāthā, Therīgāthā, Suttanipāta and Dhammapada carry running metrical commentary on individual verses, which is the only way to see the analysis applied at scale.

    A last word on what the analysis is for. It is easy to treat scansion as a technical exercise standing at some distance from what the verses are saying, and for the first week or two it is one. But a metre is a pattern a reciter’s memory was built around, and the verses in this canon were carried for centuries by people who knew them as sound before they were ever written down. Learning to hear where a line ends, where it turns, and which syllable was lengthened to make it turn there belongs to reading the texts, as a way of reading them as they were made to be heard.
