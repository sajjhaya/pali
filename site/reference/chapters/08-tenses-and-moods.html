    Chapter 8

    Verbs: The Past and the Future

    The three forms built on the present stem (the indicative, the imperative and the optative) are Chapter 7’s. This chapter takes what is built from the root instead: the past, the future, and the conditional, together with the negation and prohibition that go with them.

    One line of the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta has all three tenses in a row, and it is a good place to begin, because the form of each is visible against the other two:

    na cāhu na ca bhavissati na cetarahi vijjati añño samaṇo vā brāhmaṇo vā bhagavatā bhiyyobhiññataro yadidaṁ sambodhiyaṁ. (DN 16 — D ii 82)

    nanotcaandāhuthere has been3 sg aornanotcaandbhavissatithere will be3 sg futnanotcaandetarahinowvijjatithere is found3 sg presaññoanotherm nom sgsamaṇoasceticm nom sgvāorbrāhmaṇobrahminm nom sgbhagavatāthan the Blessed Onem ins sgbhiyyobhiññataroof greater direct knowledgem nom sgyadidaṁthat issambodhiyaṁas to awakeningf loc sg

    “There has not been, there will not be, and there is not now, another ascetic or brahmin whose direct knowledge is greater than the Blessed One’s when it comes to awakening.”

    Āhu, bhavissati, vijjati: past, future, present, and each carries its tense in a different place. The present is the bare stem plus an ending, as Chapter 7 had it. The future inserts -ssa- between the stem and the same ending. The past does neither: āhu is a form of the root with nothing recognisable added at all.1 That asymmetry is the subject of this chapter.

    The augment

    Both of the past formations in this chapter and the conditional share one mark: a prefixed a-, called the augment (vuddhi in the Sanskrit tradition’s sense of increase, though the Pāli grammarians simply call it a-kāra-āgama, ‘the coming of an a’). It is inherited from Old Indic, where it was obligatory, and in Pāli it is not.

    Two facts about it matter more than its history. It is not a negation. Agamā is ‘he went’, not ‘he did not go’; the negative is the separate word na, treated at the end of this chapter. Having learnt the prefix a- as a negative on nouns and adjectives (anicca ‘impermanent’, asukha ‘unpleasant’), you will misread a great many past-tense verbs unless the two are firmly separated.

    And when a prefix is present, the augment goes between the prefix and the root, not in front of the whole word: ud + a + pādi gives udapādi ‘it arose’; saṁ + a + bhavi gives samabhavi ‘it came to be’. Here a reader is most likely to lose it, because the augment is then buried in the middle of a long word and looks like part of the stem.2

    Whether it appears at all is not a free choice, and two sources state the pattern as a rule instead of a list of examples, arriving at it from opposite ends.3 Perniola grades it by the type of aorist. The root aorist always takes the augment; the a-aorist and the s-aorist generally do; and it is often dropped when the verb carries a prefix ending in -a, so pahāsi and upapajjatha stand beside augmented forms of the same verbs. Geiger grades it by the length of the word instead: short forms keep the augment, long ones shed it, and the shedding spreads as the language gets later. The two meet where it matters most, since the root aorist’s forms are the monosyllables: adā, agā, ahu. In verse, the metre decides.

    The aorist

    Pāli had inherited three separate past tenses from Old Indic: the imperfect, the perfect and the aorist. It kept one. The aorist absorbed the work of all three, and it is the ordinary past tense of the canon; a page of narrative prose is made of it. Oberlies accordingly heads his §48 “the preterite”, not “the aorist”, because in Pāli the two words have come to mean the same thing.

    The complication is that the aorist is four formations, not one. Old Indic formed its aorist in several ways, and Pāli inherited the several ways without inheriting the reasons for choosing between them, so which type a verb takes is a fact about that verb and has to be learnt with it. The four are conventionally numbered, and Appendix A.3 sets out all four in full. The account below gives what a reader needs in order to recognise them. To learn the commonest forms before the system, begin at the end of the chapter instead, under “Irregular finite verbs”, where five verbs are given whose aorists are best learnt whole.

    Two features run through all four. Every type uses the secondary endings: the shorter set, contrasted in Chapter 7 with the primary endings that the present and the future take. And in every type the third person singular is the form a reader meets most, because so much of the canon is narrative about what somebody did.

    Type 1: the root aorist

    The endings go straight onto the root, with the augment, and no other element comes between.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -ṁ
    2 sg. -o, -ā
    3 sg. -ā
    1 pl. -mhā, -mha
    2 pl. -ttha
    3 pl. -ū, -u, -uṁ

    This is the oldest type and the smallest: it survives on a handful of roots and no more. Perniola names them (dā- ‘give’, gā- ‘go’, ṭhā- ‘stand’, bhū- ‘be’, kar- ‘do’), giving adā, agā, aṭṭhā, ahu, akā.4 Those five are among the commonest verbs in the language, so a type with almost no members is nevertheless met constantly. In the first and second person plural it has no forms at all; the gap is filled from the other types.

    Type 2: the a-aorist

    A vowel -a- is inserted between root and ending. This is the type that produces the most irregular-looking forms, because the root itself is often reshaped in the process.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -aṁ, -a, -ā
    2 sg. -o, -a, -ā, -asi
    3 sg. -ā, -a
    1 pl. -āma, -amhā
    2 pl. -attha
    3 pl. -uṁ

    The single most useful form in the whole chapter belongs here. Vac- ‘speak’ has the a-aorist avoca ‘he said’, and it is everywhere in the canon, because it is what stands at the end of the formula introducing direct speech:

    Atha kho te bhikkhū sāvatthiyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkamiṁsu; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavantaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdiṁsu. Ekamantaṁ nisinnā kho te bhikkhū bhagavantaṁ etadavocuṁ. (AN 10.27 — A v 49)

    atha khothentethosem nom plbhikkhūmonksm nom plsāvatthiyaṁin Sāvatthīf loc sgpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sgcaritvāhaving wanderedpacchābhattaṁafter the mealpiṇḍapātapaṭikkantāreturned from the almsroundm nom plyena … tenato wherebhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgupasaṅkamiṁsuthey approached3 pl aorupasaṅkamitvāhaving approachedbhagavantaṁthe Blessed Onem acc sgabhivādetvāhaving paid homageekamantaṁto one sidenisīdiṁsuthey sat down3 pl aornisinnāseatedm nom pletadavocuṁ< etaṁ this n acc sg + avocuṁ they said 3 pl aor

    “Then those monks, having wandered for alms in Sāvatthī and returned from the almsround after the meal, approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to him, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those monks said this to the Blessed One.”

    Three aorists in one sentence, two of them type 4 and the third avocuṁ. Note that etadavocuṁ is one printed word and two: the -d- is the old stem-final of etad surfacing before a vowel, as Chapter 2 sets out. Unless you resolve that junction you will look for a verb davocuṁ and not find it.

    Avoca needs a note of its own, because its shape is not obvious. Perniola derives it as a reduplicated and weakened aorist: a-va-vac-a-t > a-va-uc-a-t > avoca, the first syllable of the root doubled, then weakened to -uc-, then contracted.5 Nothing of vac- is left on the surface, and the verb has no present in vac- either: its present is vadati and its passive vuccati. The three have to be learnt as a set.

    The verb ‘see’ also belongs here, and its aorist is the likeliest to stop a reader. Dassati ‘sees’ has the a-aorist addasā ‘he saw’ (290 occurrences in the root texts), first person addasaṁ, from a-dras-s-at, with everything assimilated.

    Type 3: the s-aorist

    An -s- is inserted between the root or stem and the ending. This type produces the -si forms, and it needs watching, because the third person singular -si is identical to the second person singular of the present indicative.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -siṁ
    2 sg. -si
    3 sg. -si
    1 pl. -simha, -mha
    2 pl. -sittha, -ttha
    3 pl. -suṁ, -ṁsu

    So desesi is either ‘he taught’ or ‘you teach’, and only the sentence decides. In practice the aorist reading is far commoner, because deseti is a verb of narrative; but the ambiguity is real and it recurs in every verb of the -e- class.

    The verbs that take this type include several of the highest frequency: karoti ‘does’ has akāsi, suṇāti ‘hears’ has assosi, tiṭṭhati ‘stands’ has aṭṭhāsi, bhāsati ‘speaks’ has abhāsi, gacchati ‘goes’ has agamāsi beside its root aorist agā.

    Atha kho rājā māgadho ajātasattu vedehiputto yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā ekamantaṁ aṭṭhāsi. (DN 2 — D i 50)

    atha khothenrājāthe kingm nom sgmāgadhoof Magadham nom sgajātasattuAjātasattum nom sgvedehiputtoson of Vedehīm nom sgyena … tenato wherebhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgupasaṅkamihe approached3 sg aorupasaṅkamitvāhaving approachedekamantaṁto one sideaṭṭhāsihe stood3 sg aor

    “Then Ajātasattu, king of Magadha, son of Vedehī, approached the Blessed One; having approached, he stood to one side.”

    Two aorists of two different types in one sentence (upasaṅkami of type 4, aṭṭhāsi of type 3), and neither is marked as anything but past.

    Type 4: the is-aorist

    An -i- or -is- is inserted between the stem and the ending. Appendix A.3, following Ñāṇatusita, calls this the i-aorist; Perniola and Oberlies call it the is-aorist, after the Sanskrit suffix it descends from. They are the same thing.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -iṁ, -isaṁ
    2 sg. -i
    3 sg. -i
    1 pl. -imha, -imhā
    2 pl. -ittha
    3 pl. -iṁsu, -isuṁ

    Learn this type first. Perniola states outright what the other sources leave a reader to infer: formed from the present stem, the is-aorist “has become the most common aorist in Pāli and can be formed from any present stem ending in -a”.6 It alone is still alive: a verb that needs an aorist and has no inherited one takes this. So if you know a verb’s present stem you already know its most likely aorist, and the other three types are then a list of exceptions, not a system to be memorised in parallel.

    The paradigm on upasaṅkamati ‘approaches’, whose stem is upasaṅkama-:

    singular plural
    1st upasaṅkamiṁ ‘I approached’ upasaṅkamimha ‘we approached’
    2nd upasaṅkami ‘you approached’ upasaṅkamittha ‘you approached’
    3rd upasaṅkami ‘he, she, it approached’ upasaṅkamiṁsu ‘they approached’

    idha mayaṁ, bhante, yena assajipunabbasukā bhikkhū tenupasaṅkamimha; upasaṅkamitvā assajipunabbasuke bhikkhū etadavocumha. (MN 70 — M i 474)

    idhaheremayaṁwenom plbhantevenerable sirvocyena … tenato whereassajipunabbasukāthe monks of Assaji and Punabbasum nom plbhikkhūmonksm nom plupasaṅkamimhawe approached1 pl aorupasaṅkamitvāhaving approachedassajipunabbasuke bhikkhūthose monksm acc pletadavocumha< etaṁ this n acc sg + avocumha we said 1 pl aor

    “Here, venerable sir, we approached the monks of Assaji and Punabbasu; having approached, we said this to them.”

    That line holds something else, unrelated to the aorist. As a bare word, upasaṅkamimha does not occur in the canon at all, because the editions never print it bare. Every one of its eleven root-text occurrences is joined to the tena before it, as tenupasaṅkamimha.7 A form can be perfectly ordinary Pāli and still be invisible to a word search, and the reason is always the same: sandhi.

    What the aorist does

    Chiefly, it reports the past: the simple past of a narrative, nine-tenths of its use. That use is easiest to watch in the Vinaya’s case law, because it repeats a frame and changes one word at a time. Four consecutive cases under the rule against stealing open with the same eleven words:

    Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu rajakattharaṇaṁ gantvā mahagghaṁ dussaṁ passitvā theyyacittaṁ uppādesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56)

    tena samayenaat that timekho pananowaññataroa certainm nom sgbhikkhumonkm nom sgrajakattharaṇaṁto the dyers’ drying-groundn acc sggantvāhaving gonemahagghaṁcostlyn acc sgdussaṁclothn acc sgpassitvāhaving seentheyyacittaṁa thought of theftn acc sguppādesiproduced3 sg aor

    “Now at that time a certain monk went to the dyers’ drying-ground, saw a costly cloth, and produced a thought of theft.”

    • … theyyacitto āmasi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — “… and, with theft in mind, touched it.”
    • … theyyacitto phandāpesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — “… and, with theft in mind, made it stir.”
    • … theyyacitto ṭhānā cāvesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 56) — “… and, with theft in mind, shifted it from its place.”

    Āmasi is a type 4 aorist on the -a- stem of āmasati. The other three are type 3, on the -e- stems of three causatives: uppādeti, phandāpeti and cāveti. So the two commonest types stand in the same slot of the same sentence, four times over, while the rest of the page stays still.

    The frame also settles the ambiguity the type 3 table warned about. Uppādesi, phandāpesi and cāvesi all have the form of a second person singular present. Not one of them can be one here: the subject is aññataro bhikkhu, nominative and third person, standing in the same place in all four sentences. The sentence decides.8

    Two further uses of the aorist need naming because they will otherwise read as errors.

    It also has the sense of a present perfect: dhammaṁ desesiṁ is at least as well rendered ‘I have taught the Dhamma’ as ‘I taught it’. Pāli has no separate perfect in living use, so the aorist covers that ground too.

    And after mā it loses its past sense entirely and forms a prohibition, which is treated below. This is a survival of the Old Indic injunctive, a form that was never past to begin with and merely looks like the aorist now, not a stretched use of the past.

    The middle voice of the aorist is nearly extinct. Perniola finds only stray third-person singulars (alattha ‘he obtained’, from labh-) and, in the is-aorist, a scatter of second and third singulars: maññittho, pucchittha.9 A reader will meet these and should recognise them; nobody needs to produce them.

    The tenses that vanished

    Old Indic had an imperfect and a perfect as well, and Pāli has the residue of both. Neither is productive, and a reader needs them only in order to parse what the texts happen to print.

    Nothing useful can be said of the imperfect, and the frankest sources say so. Duroiselle’s own account, which Thānuttamo quotes, is that the imperfect is supposed to be formed from the present stem and the aorist from the root, “however, this is not an absolute measure and, in the end, these two can hardly be told apart”.10 Where a grammar labels a form an imperfect and another labels the same form an aorist, nothing hangs on which is right.

    The perfect is different, because although it has all but disappeared, the forms that survive are extremely common. Perniola’s judgement is that in the canonical books the perfect is represented by āha in the second and third person singular, and by āhu, āhaṁsu for the third plural.11 Āha ‘he said’, ‘he has said’ is one of the most frequent verb forms in the whole literature, and without knowing it as a perfect you will look in vain for a present āhati. Beside it stands vidū ‘they knew’, from vid-.

    The future

    English has no true future tense: it builds one with a helping verb, ‘he will go’. Pāli has a real one, formed by inserting -(i)ssa- between the stem and the personal ending.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -(i)ssāmi
    2 sg. -(i)ssasi
    3 sg. -(i)ssati
    1 pl. -(i)ssāma
    2 pl. -(i)ssatha
    3 pl. -(i)ssanti

    These are the present indicative’s own endings, unchanged, on a future stem instead of the present stem: the primary endings of Chapter 7 with -(i)ssa- interposed. Perniola puts the consequence usefully: the future “is inflected like the present tense”, and lengthens its vowel before the first-person endings for the same reason the present does.12 Only the infix has to be learnt. Appendix A.3 completes the paradigm with a middle-voice future, built the same way on the middle’s own present endings; by the future’s time attanopada had all but stopped being used at all, and this book asks a reader to recognise the tense only in the active, as Chapter 7 already warned.

    On gacchati ‘goes’, whose stem is gaccha-:

    singular plural
    1st gacchissāmi ‘I will go’ gacchissāma ‘we will go’
    2nd gacchissasi ‘you will go’ gacchissatha ‘you will go’
    3rd gacchissati ‘he, she, it will go’ gacchissanti ‘they will go’

    … yaṁ kammaṁ karissāmi—kalyāṇaṁ vā pāpakaṁ vā—tassa dāyādo bhavissāmi. (AN 5.57 — A iii 74)

    yaṁwhichn acc sgkammaṁdeedn acc sgkarissāmiI will do1 sg futkalyāṇaṁgoodn acc sgvāorpāpakaṁbadn acc sgvāortassaof thatn gen sgdāyādoheirm nom sgbhavissāmiI will be1 sg fut

    “Whatever deed I do, good or bad, of that I shall be the heir.”

    When the -ss- disappears

    A future built directly on a root ending in a consonant assimilates: the s meets the final consonant of the root and the two collapse into a double consonant that is no longer -ss-. The result is a set of futures that do not look like futures at all, and several are common enough to learn as vocabulary.

    Future Gloss From
    dakkhati will see dassati (dṛś-)
    lacchati will get labhati
    vakkhati will say vadati (vac-)
    bhecchati will split bhindati
    sakkhissati will be able sakkoti

    Atthi, kassapa, maggo atthi paṭipadā, yathāpaṭipanno sāmaṁyeva ñassati sāmaṁ dakkhati. (DN 8 — D i 165)

    atthithere is3 sg preskassapaKassapam voc sgmaggoa pathm nom sgpaṭipadāa practicef nom sgyathāpaṭipannoone practising in accordance with whichm nom sgsāmaṁyeva< sāmaṁ by oneself + eva indeed, joined by the hiatus glide yñassatiwill know3 sg futsāmaṁby oneselfdakkhatiwill see3 sg fut

    “There is a path, Kassapa, there is a practice, practising in accordance with which one will know for oneself, will see for oneself.”

    Two irregular futures side by side. Ñassati ‘will know’, from jānāti, is the other kind of irregularity: a future built straight on the root ñā-, not on the present stem, and not an assimilation, so that jānissati and ñassati both exist and mean the same thing.

    The editions differ here. At this line the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints dakkhati and records in its own apparatus that the Sinhalese edition reads dakkhiti; the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print dakkhiti, which is what that note predicts. The variation is in the ending, not the stem (a competing -iti future beside -ati), and the same alternation gives hessati beside hehiti. No doctrine depends on it, but anyone comparing two printed editions will meet it.

    In verse a further set of contracted futures appears, in which the -ss- has weakened to -h-. The two commonest verbs in the language both have them: bhavati gives hessati, hehiti, and karoti gives kāhati, kāhāmi, kāhasi.13 They are metrically convenient, a syllable shorter, and that largely explains why they exist.

    What the future does

    Future time, chiefly. But it is also the ordinary way of stating a general law: what always happens, put more emphatically than the present would put it: manussā marissanti, ‘human beings will die’. And it draws an inference: na vatimāni manussabhūtassa padāni bhavissanti (AN 4.36 — A ii 37), ‘these cannot be the footprints of a human being’, where nothing about the future is meant at all and the sense is ‘it must be that’.14 That idiom is frozen in bhavissati ‘it must be’ and, negated, na bhavissati ‘it cannot be’.

    The conditional

    The conditional is the rarest finite form in the language and the easiest to build, because it is the future with a past ending on it: take the future stem, prefix the augment, and use the secondary endings instead of the primary ones.

    Person Active
    1 sg. -(i)ssaṁ
    2 sg. -(i)ssa, -(i)sse
    3 sg. -(i)ssā, -(i)ssa
    1 pl. -(i)ssāma, -(i)ssamhā
    2 pl. -(i)ssatha
    3 pl. -(i)ssaṁsu

    Its sense follows from its form. A future seen from a point in the past is something that was going to happen and did not, so the conditional is the counterfactual: ‘if he had …, then he would have …’. Oberlies notes that the optative usually stands in for it, and so the conditional is scarce.15

    No cedaṁ, bhikkhave, paṇḍito sucintitacintī ca abhavissa subhāsitabhāsī ca sukatakammakārī ca, kena naṁ paṇḍitā jāneyyuṁ. (AN 3.3 — A i 102)

    nonotceifidaṁthisn nom sgbhikkhavemonksm voc plpaṇḍitothe astute personm nom sgsucintitacintīone who thinks well-thought thoughtsm nom sgcaandabhavissawere to be, had been3 sg condsubhāsitabhāsīone who speaks well-spoken speechm nom sgcaandsukatakammakārīone who does well-done deedsm nom sgcaandkenaby whatn ins sgnaṁhimm acc sgpaṇḍitāthe astutem nom pljāneyyuṁwould know3 pl opt

    “If, monks, an astute person were not one who thinks well, speaks well and acts well, by what would the astute know him?”

    The construction is fixed: the conditional stands in the protasis, the clause carrying the condition, and the optative in the apodosis, the clause carrying the consequence. That division of labour is the normal one, though either mood can appear on either side.

    Negation and prohibition

    Three words do this work, and they are not interchangeable.

    Na negates a finite verb, and it is the ordinary negative.

    … yāvassa bhikkhū āpattiṁ na passanti. (AN 8.10 — A iv 169)

    yāvaas long asassafor himm gen sgbhikkhūthe monksm nom plāpattiṁthe offencef acc sgnanotpassantithey see3 pl pres

    “… as long as the monks do not see an offence of his.”

    No has the same function with more weight behind it, and negates a whole statement instead of a verb: no hetaṁ, bhante, ‘certainly not, venerable sir’.

    Mā forms a prohibition: a distinct word with its own syntax, not a negative at all. It takes the aorist, and the aorist then loses its past sense entirely:

    Alaṁ, puṇṇa, tiṭṭhatetaṁ; mā maṁ etaṁ pucchī’ti. (MN 57 — M i 387)

    alaṁenoughpuṇṇaPuṇṇam voc sgtiṭṭhatetaṁ< tiṭṭhatu let it stand 3 sg imp + etaṁ this n nom sgmādo notmaṁmeacc sgetaṁthisn acc sgpucchiask2 sg aor

    “Enough, Puṇṇa, let it be. Do not ask me that.”

    Mā bhāyi, vakkali. (SN 22.87 — S iii 122)

    mādo notbhāyifear2 sg aorvakkaliVakkalim voc sg

    “Do not fear, Vakkali.”

    This is the standing construction, and so an aorist in the canon is not automatically a past tense. Mā also occurs with the optative (mā, bhikkhave, pāpake akusale vitakke vitakkeyyātha, ‘monks, do not think bad, unskilful thoughts’, SN 56.7 — S v 417), where the prohibition is softened towards advice. And it shades from prohibition into wish: mā bhāyi is as much ‘may you not be afraid’ as ‘don’t be afraid’, and the setting decides which.

    Beside mā, the negated optative of Chapter 7 states a rule about what one ought not to do: na seveyya ‘one should not resort to’. The two come out as ‘don’t’ in English and are different constructions: mā forbids a particular act, na + optative lays down a precept. All three of these words are indeclinables, and Chapter 12 takes them again as a word class, with alaṁ ‘enough’ beside them and the negative prefix a-, which negates with no particle at all.

    Irregular finite verbs

    A minority of verbs are irregular in the present; a much larger number are irregular in the aorist, and the four types above are the reason. Five of the commonest verbs in the canon have aorists that cannot be worked out from the present, and these are best learnt whole.

    Verb 1 sg. 2 sg. 3 sg. 1 pl. 2 pl. 3 pl.
    gacchati ‘goes’ agamāsiṁ, agaṁ agamā agamā, agā — agamittha agamaṁsu
    dassati ‘sees’16 addasaṁ addasā addasā, addasa addasāma — addasaṁsu
    vadati ‘says’ avocaṁ avoca avoca avocumha, avocumhā avacuttha avocuṁ
    hoti ‘is, becomes’ ahosiṁ, ahuṁ ahosi, ahū ahosi, ahu ahesumha ahuvattha ahesuṁ, ahuṁ
    karoti ‘does’ akāsiṁ akāsi akāsi, akā akaramhā akattha akaṁsu

    The dashes are not omissions. Four forms that the grammars print for these verbs are not in the canon, and none of them is given here: agamhā and addasatha do not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, and aguttha and agū only in the grammarians’ own works.17 Two of those four cells had a canonical form to put in their place, agamittha and agamaṁsu above. Two did not, and stand empty. A paradigm with holes in it is a truer picture of the language than one completed by rule, when the paradigm is of a single irregular word rather than a display of endings.

    How the page prints them

    A table gives each aorist as a free word, and that is not how a reader meets the commonest of them. Counted as the words stand in the root texts:18

    As a free word Inside a junction
    avoca 245 etadavoca 3,091 · idamavoca 302
    ahosi 1,559 etadahosi 1,000
    upasaṅkami 29 tenupasaṅkami 1,671

    Avoca is the starkest case. For every time it stands alone in the canon, etadavoca ‘said this’ stands more than twelve times, and having learnt the form from the table above you will not see it on the page until the junction of Chapter 2 is undone. Upasaṅkami ‘approached’ is not irregular at all; it is here because it hides in the same way, joined to the tena of the stock approach, yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami.

    Nor are the five irregulars the five commonest aorists. Nisīdi ‘sat down’ stands 1,078 times as a free word and udapādi ‘arose’ 840, both regular, and each outnumbers addasā (290), akāsi (143) and agamāsi (139). These five are to be learnt whole because they cannot be worked out, not because nothing is commoner.

    One habit at the page

    Everything in this chapter comes down to one habit at the page. Faced with a verb you do not know, look for the augment, remembering that it stands after a prefix and not in front of it; then ask what is left, and which of the four types that leaves. Where the form ends in -si, do not choose between ‘he taught’ and ‘you teach’ from the word itself. Read the subject. And where a common verb will not parse at all, undo the junction before reaching for the dictionary.
