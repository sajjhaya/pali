    Chapter 17

    Relative Clauses

    Two clauses, and one term between them

    A relative clause is a subordinate clause that can only be read against the main clause, because a word in it points there. In Pāli that word almost always begins with y-, and the word it points to almost always begins with t-. Warder calls the resulting articulation “an outstanding characteristic of Pāli”; he is being restrained. In Lesson 27 he calls the relative clause “the most important and most frequent of all the elements in Pāli period building”, and the reason is arithmetical as much as stylistic: Pāli has few conjunctions and no relative pronoun that can be dropped, so a long sentence has to be built out of pairs.

    The tradition’s names for the two halves describe the construction better than the English ones do. A relative pronoun is aniyamattha, “of undetermined meaning”: it posits a term without saying which one.1 The answering word in the main clause determines it. So the sentence advances by holding something open and then closing it, and only at the closing can it be understood at all.

    That is a different habit from English, and the difference should be stated flatly at the outset, because it governs how a Pāli sentence is read. English subordinates by embedding; Pāli subordinates by pairing. English builds the mendicant who saw this went away by dropping a clause inside the main one and leaving the noun where it stood. Pāli says, in effect, which mendicant saw this, he went away: two clauses side by side, the noun standing in the first, and a pronoun holding its place in the second. The relative clause regularly comes first. So a reader meets the subordinate clause before there is anything to subordinate it to, and cannot begin to construe until the answering word arrives.

    That is the practical difficulty of the whole chapter, and it does not go away with familiarity. It is only managed, by learning what to expect.

    The rule that decides every relative pronoun

    Three sources state it in nearly the same words, and Collins calls it “the vital principle to remember”:2

    A relative pronoun takes its number and gender from the antecedent, and its case from its own clause.

    The rule has two parts, and they should be separated. Gender and number are agreement: they identify what the relative is pointing at, and they are the reader’s only means of doing so when the antecedent is several words away. Case is function: it says what the relative does inside the clause it opens. The two halves answer different questions, and reading a relative pronoun means asking both.

    Here is the rule with nothing complicating it. The Buddha has just left Pāṭaligāma by a particular gate:

    Atha kho bhagavā yena dvārena nikkhami, taṁ gotamadvāraṁ nāma ahosi. (DN 16 — D ii 89)

    atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Oneyena dvārenaaby which gaten ins sgnikkhamiwent outaortaṁathatn nom sggotamadvāraṁathe Gotama Gaten nom sgnāmaby nameahosibecameaor

    The gate by which the Blessed One went out came to be called the Gotama Gate.

    Yena and taṁ are the same word twice, neuter and singular both times because dvāra is neuter and singular. Their cases differ because their functions differ: yena is instrumental because you leave by a gate, taṁ is nominative because it is the subject of ahosi. Neither case shows anything about the other. Expect the pair to match in case and you will look for a second neuter instrumental and not find one.

    Warder gives this sentence as his sole illustration of the rule, and it is well chosen: it is the smallest possible sentence in which the two halves of the rule visibly pull in different directions.

    Four places the rule holds and still surprises

    Of the sources in the collection, only Bodhi treats agreement as a difficulty and not merely a fact, and in all four cases he assembles the rule is obeyed exactly and the result is not what a reader expects.3 They belong together.

    The case changes across the pair. This is the ordinary situation and it has just been shown; it is listed first because an unwarned reader takes a case mismatch for a mistake.

    The gender changes across the pair, because each word agrees with its own noun. The relative takes the gender of the noun in the relative clause; the correlative takes the gender of the noun in the main clause; if those two nouns differ in gender, so do the pronouns.

    Yo, bhikkhave, rāgakkhayo dosakkhayo mohakkhayo — idaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, asaṅkhataṁ. (SN 43.12 — S iv 362)

    yoawhichm nom sgbhikkhavemonksrāgakkhayoathe ending of greedm nom sgdosakkhayothe ending of hatemohakkhayothe ending of delusionidaṁbthisn nom sgvuccatiis calledasaṅkhataṁbthe unconditionedn nom sg

    The ending of greed, the ending of hate, the ending of delusion: this, monks, is called the unconditioned.

    Yo is masculine because khaya is masculine. Idaṁ is neuter because asaṅkhata is neuter. They refer to the same thing and agree with different words, which is the rule working correctly and looking as though it has failed. Notice also that the correlative here is not from the ta- stem at all.

    A relative standing before a run of nouns of different genders agrees with the first of them.

    Yā kho, bhikkhave, imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ saṅgati sannipāto samavāyo, ayaṁ vuccati cakkhusamphasso. (SN 35.93 — S iv 68, and again at each of the other sense bases)

    yāawhichf nom sgimesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁof these three thingsm gen plsaṅgatiathe meetingf nom sgsannipātothe coming togetherm nom sgsamavāyothe concurrencem nom sgayaṁbthism nom sgcakkhusamphassobeye-contactm nom sg

    The meeting, the coming together, the concurrence of these three things is called eye-contact.

    Saṅgati is feminine and sannipāta and samavāya are masculine; yā is feminine because the first of the three is. The main clause’s ayaṁ is masculine because it agrees with cakkhusamphasso. One noun phrase, two genders, and each pronoun taking the gender of the word standing next to it.

    The relative clause is completed by sabba, not by a demonstrative. When a relative clause is generalised, the main clause is very often closed by sabba “all”, not by a bare ta- form, with the demonstrative attached to it, or by sabba alone.

    Yāni kānici, bhikkhave, bhayāni uppajjanti sabbāni tāni bālato uppajjanti, no paṇḍitato. (AN 3.1 — A i 101)

    yāni kāniciawhatevern nom plbhayāniadangersn nom pluppajjantiarisesabbāni tāniaall thosen nom plbālatofrom the foolm abl sgno paṇḍitatonot from the astute one

    Whatever dangers arise, monks, all of them arise from the fool, not from the astute one.

    The one place the rule is broken

    Oberlies gives a sentence in which the relative does not agree with its noun at all, and, being Oberlies, he gives the reason as well.4 Māgaṇḍiya is speaking:

    Vinicchayā yāni pakappitāni, (iti māgaṇḍiyo) Te ve munī brūsi anuggahāya. (Snp 4.9 — Sn p. 164)

    vinicchayājudgementsm nom plyāniwhichn nom plpakappitānihave been formedn nom pl, pp of pakappetiiti māgaṇḍiyosaid Māgaṇḍiyatethosem acc plveindeedmunīsagem voc sgbrūsiyou speak ofanuggahāyawithout adopting (them)

    “The judgements that have been formed — those, sage, you speak of without adopting any of them,” said Māgaṇḍiya.

    One noun, and two pronouns disagreeing about its gender in consecutive lines: yāni neuter, te masculine. Oberlies’s account is that in the plural the masculine and neuter nominative and accusative endings had already fallen together under dialect mixture, so the distinction the rule depends on was not being maintained. The lexicon shows the same thing from the other side: DPD carries vinicchaya as masculine at this verse and as neuter three verses later in the same vagga, printing the neuter plural vinicchayāni.5 The word is of both genders, and its relative pronoun is free to choose.

    Two conclusions follow for a reader, and only the second is comfortable. A relative that does not agree is not necessarily a corruption. And the rule remains the right one to reason with, because the exception is confined to a place where the language itself had stopped distinguishing.

    The forms

    The relative pronoun is the stem ya(d)-, and it inflects as the demonstrative eta(d)- does; Oberlies establishes the identity, and that allows a table to be given at all.6

    sg. m. sg. n. sg. f. pl. m. pl. n. pl. f.
    nom. yo yaṁ (yad-) yā ye yāni yā
    acc. yaṁ (yad-) yaṁ (yad-) yaṁ ye yāni yā
    instr. yena yena yāya yehi yehi yāhi
    abl. yasmā, yamhā, yato yasmā, yamhā, yato yāya yehi yehi yāhi
    gen./dat. yassa yassa yassā yesaṁ yesaṁ yāsaṁ
    loc. yasmiṁ, yamhi yasmiṁ, yamhi yāyaṁ, yassaṁ yesu yesu yāsu

    Three notes, each of which a reader will need before they need the rest of the table.

    Yad- is not a word. It is what yaṁ becomes before a vowel, and the texts never print it standing alone, not once, in the root texts, the commentaries, the sub-commentaries or the later works. They print the result: yadidaṁ 953 times in the root texts, yadā 603, yadeva 120, yadaniccaṁ 66. The junction consonant is the old d of the stem, surfacing when a vowel gives it somewhere to go.7

    The genitive plural has a longer form that this book does not teach. Grammars print yesānaṁ beside yesaṁ, and the ending itself is real: the root texts carry katamesānaṁ twelve times, and sabbesānaṁ and etesānaṁ once each. But yesānaṁ occurs nowhere in the root texts, the commentaries or the sub-commentaries. Its seven occurrences in the whole Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana are six declension runs in Kaccāyana, Padarūpasiddhi and the Saddanīti, and one line of a mediaeval chronicle. It is a cell the grammarians completed by rule and nobody used.8

    The feminine plural forms are thin, and that is a fact about sentences, not forms. Yāhi stands seven times in the root texts and yāsaṁ five. A Pāli sentence rarely needs a feminine instrumental plural relative pronoun; when it needs one, this is the word.

    The word that answers

    The default answering word is the corresponding form of ta(d)-, and the pairs are so regular that a reader learns to hear the second as soon as the first arrives. But four things happen often enough that expecting only ta- will mislead.

    The answer may be a different pronoun. Idaṁ, ayaṁ and eta- all serve, as in the two sentences above; so do the personal pronouns, and so does sabba.

    The answer may be a noun. Where the main clause names the thing, it does not also need a pronoun for it. The relative clause then reads as an ordinary adjectival modifier.

    The answer may be missing altogether. Warder notes that this happens especially where the main clause carries a proper name with a title, and that it is otherwise the exception, not the rule.9 The sharpest case is one Wijesekera isolates, where the missing word is the relative, not the correlative: the same nominative serves as subject of the main sentence and of a subordinate clause with nothing to introduce it:

    Ayaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, bhikkhu phegguṁ aggahesi brahmacariyassa. (MN 29 — M i 196)

    ayaṁthis onem nom sgvuccatiis calledbhikkhua monkm nom sgphegguṁthe softwoodm acc sgaggahesitookaor of gaṇhati, a + gahe + si, √gahbrahmacariyassaof the spiritual lifen gen sg

    This one, monks, is called a monk who has taken the softwood of the spiritual life.

    There is no yo in the sentence, and English cannot manage without one. Bhikkhu is the complement of vuccati and at the same time the subject of aggahesi, and the two clauses are simply set down side by side.10

    The Vinaya sharpens Warder’s “exception rather than the rule”, because the Pātimokkha is built on the omission. Most of its rules open with a relative, and the shorter classes close without answering it:

    • Yā pana bhikkhunī lasuṇaṁ khādeyya, pācittiyaṁ. (Bi Pc 1 — Vin iv 259)

    Nothing answers yā. The main clause is the single word pācittiyaṁ, and it names an offence, not a nun, so there is nowhere for a correlative to stand even if one were wanted. Chapter 16 takes the same sentence for what it does to the conditional.

    Where a correlative does turn up in a rule, it adds something besides closing the clause. The second pārājika ends ayampi pārājiko hoti asaṁvāso (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46), “this one too is expelled and not in communion”, and the pi joined to the demonstrative is the point of it. The first pārājika has no correlative at all; the second, third and fourth all carry ayampi, six times between them in the root texts, once in each rule’s original formulation and once in its amendment.11 Each is being added to a list already running. The demonstrative appears where ‘also’ is wanted, and it closes the relative clause as well.

    The relative clause may follow the main clause. The order is reversible, and the reversal is not free: it emphasises the main clause, and Warder describes the effect as elevated speech.12 Thānuttamo gives a clean instance where the demonstrative and its clause come first and the relative answers:13 amataṁ tesaṁ, bhikkhave, sacchikataṁ yesaṁ kāyagatāsati sacchikatā — “the deathless has been realised by those, monks, by whom mindfulness of the body has been realised.”

    So expect a closure of some kind, not necessarily a ta- form: a demonstrative, a noun, a sabba, or nothing at all. The reader’s business is to keep the relative clause open until one of them arrives.

    The relatives, from emptiest to fullest

    Warder’s arrangement is the useful one, and no other source has anything like it. He ranks the relative words by how much each of them means: yaṁ is “the most general or ‘empty’ relative”; yathā is “the next most general or empty relative after yaṁ”; and then “the remaining relatives are more specialized in meaning.”

    The ranking is a difficulty ranking as much as a semantic one. A relative that means “where” shows what kind of clause is coming. A relative that means nothing shows only that a clause is coming, and leaves the relation to be worked out from the sentence. So the emptiest word is the hardest, and it is much the commonest.

    Yaṁ, and what the tradition does with it

    At its emptiest, yaṁ is a marker: it says that what follows is subordinate, much as ti says that what precedes is quoted, and it carries no case relation a reader can use directly. Warder lists the senses it covers (indirect speech, a supposition, a concession, a cause, or “merely a qualification”) and adds that the optative appears in it as usual for hypothetical cases.

    It is sometimes said that it is not always clear whether such relatives are pronouns or adverbs, and that for translation this does not usually pose a problem. The tradition disagrees with both halves of that, and the disagreement is the most valuable thing in this chapter.

    Commenting on the opening of the Mahāpadāna Sutta, Buddhaghosa meets a yaṁ with no visible work to do:

    Ito so, bhikkhave, ekanavutikappe yaṁ vipassī bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi. (DN 14 — D ii 2)

    itofrom nowsothatm nom sgekanavutikappeninety-one eonsm loc sgyaṁwhichn acc sgvipassī bhagavāthe Blessed One Vipassīarahaṁperfectedsammāsambuddhofully awakenedlokein the worldm loc sgudapādiaroseaor, ud + a + √pad + i

    Ninety-one eons ago, monks, the Blessed One Vipassī, perfected and fully awakened, arose in the world.

    His first move is to resolve the word instead of leaving it: Tattha yaṁ vipassīti yasmiṁ kappe vipassī, “yaṁ vipassī means in which eon Vipassī”.14 The empty relative has a case after all, and the case is locative; the sentence means “ninety-one eons ago is the eon in which Vipassī arose”, not “ninety-one eons ago and, separately, Vipassī arose”.

    His second move is to generalise, and it is a four-way classification of the word, each use named by its case in the tradition’s vocabulary and each supported by a citation:

    His term The case His illustration
    paccattavacane nominative yaṁ me, bhante, devānaṁ tāvatiṁsānaṁ sammukhā sutaṁ … ārocemi taṁ, bhagavato
    upayogavacane accusative Yaṁ taṁ apucchimha akittayī no, aññaṁ taṁ pucchāma tadiṅgha brūhi
    karaṇavacane instrumental Aṭṭhānametaṁ, bhikkhave, anavakāso, yaṁ ekissā lokadhātuyā
    bhummatthe locative the sentence above

    So the tradition’s position is that the empty relative is never really empty: it always has a case, the case is recoverable from the sentence, and recovering it is what reading the sentence consists of. That is a considerably more demanding claim than “this does not pose a problem”, and it is also a better description of what a translator actually does.

    One of the four is disputed, and both sides have something to be said for them.

    Grammars differ on the instrumental yaṁ. Buddhaghosa reads Aṭṭhānametaṁ … yaṁ ekissā lokadhātuyā dve arahanto … uppajjeyyuṁ as a nominative used in the sense of an instrumental (kāraṇatthe paccattaṁ, “the nominative in the sense of cause”) and glosses it yena kāraṇena, “by which reason”. Wijesekera holds that the form is rather an adverbial accusative on the Sanskrit model, and supports the reading with sentences in which the yaṁ clause is clearly the content of a demonstrative accusative: Tasmātihānanda, tuyhevetaṁ dukkaṭaṁ … yaṁ tvaṁ … na tathāgataṁ yāci (DN 16), “therefore, Ānanda, this is your own misdeed … that you did not entreat the Realised One”. The two analyses agree on the translation and disagree on the grammar behind it. Wijesekera’s has the merit of accounting for all four of Buddhaghosa’s uses by one mechanism.15

    And the temporal yaṁ answers a question this book has walked past for sixteen chapters. Wijesekera assembles the sentences in which yaṁ simply means “when” — So kho, ānanda, samayo yaṁ mahāvātā vāyanti, “that is the time when great winds blow” — and observes that they explain the accusative in the phrase every reader of this book has met a thousand times.16 Ekaṁ samayaṁ, the accusative that opens nearly every sutta in the canon, is an adverbial accusative of exactly this kind. It is not “one occasion” as an object of anything. It is “on one occasion”, the same construction as yaṁ for “when”, frozen into a formula.

    Yaṁ also fuses freely with what follows it, and two of the results are common enough to be treated as words. Yadidaṁ, “which is this”, corresponds to English “that is to say” or “namely”, and is often best left almost untranslated. Yadagge or yadaggena, “from which point”, means “since” of a stretch of time reckoned from a starting point.17

    Yathā, and the relatives that mean something

    Yathā is the next word up the scale. Its core sense is manner (“as”, “in the way that”), but Warder records it as also carrying consecutive, comparative, causal and final senses, a range wide enough that the answering word is usually needed to fix it. The commonest answer is tathā, and evaṁ serves as well.

    Doubled, yathā yathā … tathā tathā generalises the manner over every instance of it:

    Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 57)

    yathā yathāin whatever wayvā panaor moreoverassahism gen sgkāyobodym nom sgpaṇihitois disposedpphotiistathā tathāin just that waynaṁitm acc sgpajānātihe understands

    Or however his body is disposed, he understands it accordingly.

    That sentence is from the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta’s section on postures, and the doubling matters in it: the instruction is that the understanding tracks the posture whatever the posture happens to be.

    The remaining relatives are specialised, and a reader can take their senses from the pairs directly. What follows treats the three kinds of clause they build.

    Time

    Perniola distinguishes three ways of introducing a temporal clause, and the distinction is useful because the three behave differently.18 Yadā answers to tadā or atha and marks a point. Yāva answers to tāva and marks an extent. And yasmiṁ samaye answers to tasmiṁ samaye, a locative noun phrase with a relative adjective in front of it, not a relative adverb at all: the construction Buddhaghosa reached for when he resolved the empty yaṁ.

    Warder adds two observations, both of which save a reader trouble. The correlative tadā is optional and is often absent. And the same yadā construction can carry a condition instead of a time, in which case the tenses behave as they do in a conditional sentence.

    The extent construction carries one of the best-known passages in the canon. The word is not yāvakīvaṁ, which is how grammars cite it and which occurs nowhere; the canon has yāvakīvañca, with ca joined on, 95 times in the root texts.

    Yāvakīvañca, ānanda, vajjī ye te vajjīnaṁ vajjimahallakā, te sakkarissanti garuṁ karissanti mānessanti pūjessanti tesañca sotabbaṁ maññissanti, vuddhiyeva, ānanda, vajjīnaṁ pāṭikaṅkhā, no parihāni. (DN 16 — D ii 74)

    yāvakīvañcaand so long asvajjīthe Vajjiansm nom plye tethose whom nom plvajjīnaṁof the Vajjiansm gen plvajjimahallakāthe Vajjian eldersm nom pltethemm acc plsakkarissantiwill honourfutgaruṁ karissantiwill respectmānessantiwill esteempūjessantiwill veneratetesañcaand of themm gen pl + casotabbaṁwhat is to be listened ton nom sg, fppmaññissantiwill thinkfutvuddhiyevagrowth alonef nom sg + evapāṭikaṅkhāis to be expectedf nom sg, fppno parihāninot decline

    And so long as the Vajjians honour, respect, esteem and venerate the Vajjian elders among them, and think their word worth listening to, only growth is to be expected for the Vajjians, not decline.

    There are two relative clauses here, one inside the other, and their answering words are of different kinds. The outer yāvakīvañca is answered by nothing at all: the main clause simply states what is to be expected. The inner ye te is answered by te, and stands in the middle of the yāva clause as its object. Look only for yāva … tāva and you will find nothing to pair and conclude the sentence is broken.

    Cause

    Two words do this work, yato and yasmā, and every grammar in the collection treats them as interchangeable synonyms answering to tasmā or tato. Warder alone says otherwise: “yasmā, ‘because’, ‘since’, is a rarely used synonym of yato.”

    He is right, and he is right in a more interesting way than he says. In the root texts yato stands 1,370 times against yasmā’s 374, which is the ratio he describes. In the commentaries it reverses, and more sharply (yasmā 3,317 against yato’s 590), and in the sub-commentaries 1,605 against 327. Yasmā … tasmā is the exegetes’ connective. A commentator writes it when setting out a reason, and the canon, which argues less and narrates more, reaches for yato.

    That matters for two quite different reasons. It shows a reader what to expect where, and it explains why the grammars disagree: a grammar written mainly from commentarial Pāli will teach yasmā as the ordinary word for “because”, and will be describing the language it read.

    Neither is rare in absolute terms, and the canonical instances of yasmā include one of the most-quoted sentences in the Saṁyutta:

    Kittāvatā nu kho, bhante, suñño lokoti vuccatī ti? — Yasmā ca kho, ānanda, suññaṁ attena vā attaniyena vā tasmā suñño lokoti vuccati. (SN 35.85 — S iv 54)

    kittāvatāin what respectabl, adverbialsuññoemptym nom sglokothe worldm nom sgvuccatiis calledyasmābecausesuññaṁemptyn nom sgattenaof a selfm ins sgattaniyenaof what belongs to a selfn ins sgtasmātherefore

    “Sir, in what respect is it said that the world is empty?” — “Because it is empty of a self or of what belongs to a self, Ānanda, it is said that the world is empty.”

    The question and the answer are built from the same stem in three shapes: kittāvatā interrogative, yasmā relative, tasmā demonstrative. Bodhi points out that this triad runs right through the pronominal adverbs (kattha, yattha, tattha; kadā, yadā, tadā; kuto, yato, tato; kathaṁ, yathā, tathā), and once you see the pattern you have learnt three sets of words for the price of one.19

    Yato also keeps its older sense of “whence”, the place a thing comes from, and Warder gives sentences in which the two senses are hard to separate, as one would expect of an ablative that has become a conjunction.

    Place, and the connective yena

    Yattha answers to tattha and is the ordinary relative of place, in both the literal and the extended sense: yattha also covers “in which case”, “in which matter”. It is the commonest of the specialised relatives — 3,936 occurrences in the root texts, more than yadā and yāva together.

    Yena has two distinct jobs and they should not be confused. With a verb of motion it means “to where”, and the thing arrived at stands in the nominative: yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami, “he went up to where the Blessed One was”. That idiom belongs to the chapter on case syntax, which treats it as a use of the instrumental.

    The other use belongs here. Wijesekera shows that yena is also a connective, parallel to the empty yaṁ, in the sense “whereby, in that, such that”.20 It ordinarily follows a question about cause:

    Ko nu kho, bhante, hetu ko paccayo, yena mātugāmo neva sabhāyaṁ nisīdati, na kammantaṁ payojeti, na kambojaṁ gacchatīti? (AN 4.80 — A ii 82)

    kowhatm nom sghetucausem nom sgpaccayoconditionm nom sgyenawherebym ins sgmātugāmoa womanm nom sgna evanot at allsabhāyaṁin the assemblyf loc sgnisīdatisitskammantaṁworkm acc sgpayojetiundertakeskambojaṁto Kambojam acc sggacchatigoes

    “What is the cause, sir, what is the condition, whereby a woman does not sit in the assembly, does not undertake work, does not go to Kamboja?”

    The Buddha’s reply closes the sentence with the same word, not with a demonstrative: ayaṁ kho, ānanda, hetu ayaṁ paccayo, yena mātugāmo … (AN 4.80 — A ii 83). So yena here does not answer an interrogative at all; it attaches a clause to a noun, and that makes it a connective, not a case form in its case function.21

    Tense across the pair

    Warder devotes a paragraph to the tenses of correlative sentences that no other source in the collection attempts, and although he writes it about conditionals it holds for the construction generally.22 There are four rules, and the same tense is normally used in both clauses:

    • If the relation is purely hypothetical, both verbs are optative.
    • If the result is certain and always true, both are present.
    • If the result is certain in a particular case, both are future.
    • A past participle may stand for the subordinate verb.

    Two of those four need a further sentence. The present is the tense of what Warder calls an eternal truth, a result that holds whenever the condition does, and so much doctrinal statement stands in it. The past participle does what the tenses cannot: it marks the subordinate action as complete before the main one begins, a relation of order, not of time.

    The third rule accounts for a feature of the canon that puzzles readers: whole passages in the future tense that are clearly not about the future. The Vajjian sentence above is one: sakkarissanti, karissanti, mānessanti, pūjessanti, four futures in a row, closed by pāṭikaṅkhā, a future passive participle, “to be expected”, not a future tense, with the same effect by other means. The sentence states a particular case, certain if the condition holds, and does not predict.

    Making it general

    Three devices generalise a relative clause, and Pāli uses them constantly.

    Doubling the relative. Yo yo is “whoever”, yaṁ yaṁ “whatever”, yattha yattha “wherever”. The correlative is doubled to match: tassa tassa, taṁ taṁ.23

    Adding an indefinite. Koci, kāci, kiñci, keci attach to the relative and generalise it: yaṁ kiñci “whatever”, ye keci “whoever”, yāni kānici “whatever” (neuter plural). The pair is regularly closed by sabba, as in the AN 3.1 sentence above.

    The sentence of this book a reader is most likely to have met already is built this way, and it deserves a pause. When the Dhamma-eye arises in Koṇḍañña at the end of the first discourse, what it sees is stated as a correlative sentence and nothing more:

    Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

    yaṁ kiñciawhatevern nom sgsamudayadhammaṁais of the nature to arisen nom sg, adj compound samudaya + dhammasabbaṁ taṁaall thatn nom sgnirodhadhammaṁais of the nature to ceasen nom sg, nirodha + dhamma

    Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease.

    Every word in it is either a pronoun or a compound of dhamma, there is no finite verb, and the whole content lies in the relation between the two halves. The relative clause opens a class with no members named; the main clause closes it and says what holds of all of them. That is what a correlative sentence is for, and it is striking that the canon’s shortest statement of insight should be a pure specimen of the construction.

    Repeating relative and antecedent together. Collins records a third device, repeating both the relative and the noun instead of doubling the pronoun alone, and Warder gives the form with yadi: yaṁ yad eva parisaṁ upasaṅkamati, yadi khattiyaparisaṁ, yadi brāhmaṇaparisaṁ …, “whatever assembly he approaches, whether of nobles, whether of brahmins …”. The yadi there corresponds to English “whether”, its ordinary use in a disjunction.

    In all three the relative names no particular thing. What differs is only how the openness is marked.

    The relative adjectives

    Pāli has, as English does not, adjectives that behave like relatives. They agree with a noun and they open a clause, and each has a demonstrative partner.

    Relative Sense Demonstrative
    yāvataka as much as, however much tāvataka
    yattaka as much as tattaka
    yādisa, yādisaka of which kind tādisa, tādisaka
    yathārūpa of which kind tathārūpa

    Every source that lists these lists the first two pairs as equals, and they are unequal. In the root texts yāvatak- stands 117 times and tāvatak- 78; yattak- stands 12 times, and tattak- 15, of which four turn out on inspection to be tatta- “heated” inside a compound, not the pronoun at all, leaving eleven.24 That is a ratio of roughly ten to one, and it should decide where a reader spends their attention. Yādisa and yathārūpa, with their partners, are common; learn both by sight.

    Yāvatakaṁ, bhante, sekhena ñāṇena sekhāya vijjāya pattabbaṁ, anuppattaṁ taṁ mayā. (MN 73 — M i 494)

    yāvatakaṁhowever muchn nom sgsekhena ñāṇenaby a trainee’s knowledgen ins sgsekhāya vijjāyaby a trainee’s realisationf ins sgpattabbaṁis to be attainedn nom sg, fppanuppattaṁhas been attainedn nom sg, pptaṁthatn nom sgmayāby meins

    Whatever, sir, is to be attained by a trainee’s knowledge, by a trainee’s realisation — that has been attained by me.

    The clause you do not see

    The last thing to say about relative clauses is that Pāli builds fewer of them than English does, and that the yo … so construction is only one of five ways the language attributes something to a noun.

    Perniola is the only source in the collection to set this out as a taxonomy, and it reorganises the subject.25 A relative clause or phrase, he says, can be expressed:

    1. By a participle agreeing with the noun. Addasā bhagavantaṁ dūrato va āgacchantaṁ, “he saw the Blessed One coming from afar”, where English needs “who was coming”.
    2. By a tappurisa compound. Sīlasampanno bhikkhu, “a monk endowed with virtue”, for “a monk who is endowed with virtue”.
    3. By a kammadhāraya compound. Jātapuriso kālaṁ karissati, “the man who is born will die”.26
    4. By a bahubbīhi compound. Indriyesu guttadvāro bhikkhu, “a monk who guards the doors of his senses”: the compound is itself an adjective meaning “having guarded doors”.
    5. By two correlative clauses, which is everything this chapter has been about.

    Collins states the same fact without the taxonomy, and states the reason: relative clauses “occur less in Pali than in English because Pali, like Sanskrit … has the ability to make up compounds ad hoc which replace relative clauses.”

    The practical consequence is a reading habit, not a rule. When a Pāli sentence seems to lack a clause that the English rendering clearly has, the clause has usually been compounded or hung on a participle, not omitted, and the reader’s job is to find which. The chapters on compounds and on participles teach the forms; this is the sentence-level reason for caring about it.

    One sentence, read whole

    Every chapter of Parts IV to VI has read its examples for its own feature. The syntax ends here by reading one sentence for all of them at once, and the sentence chosen is one a reader will meet over and over. Chapter 8 used it for its aorists:

    Atha kho te bhikkhū sāvatthiyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkamiṁsu; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavantaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdiṁsu. Ekamantaṁ nisinnā kho te bhikkhū bhagavantaṁ etadavocuṁ. (AN 10.27 — A v 49)

    atha khothenteathosem nom plbhikkhūamonksm nom plsāvatthiyaṁin Sāvatthīf loc sgpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sgcaritvāhaving wanderedpacchābhattaṁafter the mealpiṇḍapātapaṭikkantāareturned from the almsroundm nom plyena … tenato wherebhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgupasaṅkamiṁsuthey approached3 pl aorupasaṅkamitvāhaving approachedbhagavantaṁthe Blessed Onem acc sgabhivādetvāhaving paid homageekamantaṁto one sidenisīdiṁsuthey sat down3 pl aornisinnāaseatedm nom pletadavocuṁ< etaṁ this n acc sg + avocuṁ they said 3 pl aor

    “Then those monks, having wandered for alms in Sāvatthī and returned from the almsround after the meal, approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to him, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those monks said this to the Blessed One.”

    Laid out word by word, with each word set against the one it answers to:

    Word What it is Read against
    atha kho particles, ‘then’ the whole sentence
    te bhikkhū m. nom. pl., the subject every verb that follows
    sāvatthiyaṁ f. loc. sg., the place caritvā
    piṇḍāya m. dat. sg., purpose caritvā
    caritvā absolutive upasaṅkamiṁsu
    pacchābhattaṁ frozen accusative, ‘after the meal’ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā
    piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā compound on a past participle, m. nom. pl. bhikkhū, with which it agrees
    yena … tena instrumentals, relative and correlative of place upasaṅkamiṁsu
    bhagavā m. nom. sg., the person arrived at yena
    upasaṅkamiṁsu 3 pl. aorist, inside the junction tenupasaṅkamiṁsu the first finite verb
    upasaṅkamitvā absolutive, taking up the verb before it nisīdiṁsu
    bhagavantaṁ m. acc. sg., the object abhivādetvā
    abhivādetvā absolutive nisīdiṁsu
    ekamantaṁ frozen accusative, ‘to one side’ nisīdiṁsu
    nisīdiṁsu 3 pl. aorist the second finite verb
    nisinnā past participle, m. nom. pl. bhikkhū, with which it agrees
    bhagavantaṁ m. acc. sg., the person spoken to avocuṁ
    etadavocuṁ etaṁ the thing said + avocuṁ, 3 pl. aorist the third finite verb

    There are three finite verbs, and every other word in the passage hangs from one of them, directly or through something that does. Chapter 16 began from the same observation: the finite verb is the scarce resource a long sentence is organised around. So the way into a sentence like this is to find the finite verbs first and then to ask of each remaining word what it is read against. Reading from left to right, as English is read, gets the words in the right order and their relations wrong.

    Not every word answers to a finite verb. Sāvatthiyaṁ and piṇḍāya answer to caritvā. The monks wandered in Sāvatthī for alms; they did not approach the Buddha in Sāvatthī for alms. An absolutive governs its own words as a finite verb does, and it is the governed words that tell a reader which action each phrase belongs to. Pacchābhattaṁ and ekamantaṁ are accusatives that have stopped being anything but adverbs, which is how the Digital Pali Dictionary files them, and yena … tena is the instrumental pair of the case-syntax chapter, with bhagavā in the nominative because, as this chapter put it, the thing arrived at stands in the nominative.

    The links are Chapter 16’s lion over again. Upasaṅkamiṁsu is taken up by upasaṅkamitvā, and nisīdiṁsu by nisinnā, the second time by a past participle instead of an absolutive, but to the same effect. Each action is the starting point of the next, so the passage recites as one movement and not as three. And two of the three finite verbs are inside junctions, tenupasaṅkamiṁsu and etadavocuṁ, so Chapter 8 found these verbs printed alone so seldom.

    Nothing in the sentence is rare, and so the chapter ends here. Tenupasaṅkami has 1,671 occurrences in the root texts. Account for every word of this passage and you can account for most of the narrative prose in the canon.

    Where the rest of this is

    The conditional sentence is a relative clause, and Warder classes sace, ce and yadi among the relatives on that ground: they open a bound clause and are answered by the main one. The three types and their tenses are in the chapter on clause syntax, which also treats the concessive clause, the only subordination in this family with no relative word at all, marked only by pi or api on a word inside it.

    The simile is a relative clause too. Seyyathā (which DPD resolves as se + yathā, the first element an old form of the ta- stem answering to Sanskrit tam) opens the comparison, and evameva closes it, often several sentences later. The construction is treated with the other sentence types in the chapter on clause syntax. Three forms of the word have to be kept apart: seyyathā alone stands 35 times in the root texts, seyyathāpi 2,289, and seyyathidaṁ (“as follows”, introducing a list, not a comparison) 686.

    The pronominal stems’ oblique forms as adverbs (tasmā, tato, yena … tena) are treated as case uses in the chapter on case syntax, and their forms in the chapter on pronouns. What this chapter has added is only that each of them can open a clause.

    And one form is a fossil. The ye- of yebhuyyena, “for the most part”, is an eastern nominative singular neuter of ya-, a Māgadhī form surviving inside a common adverb long after the dialect it belongs to stopped being written.27 Wijesekera and Oberlies note it independently. It is a small thing, and a reminder that a language keeps its oldest material in the closed classes.
