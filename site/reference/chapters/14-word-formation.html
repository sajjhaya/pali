    Chapter 14

    Word Formation: Prefixes and Suffixes

    The last chapter was about words joined together. This one is about words built up: the pieces that go on the front and the back of a stem, and what they do to it. The two are the same operation seen from different distances, so the tradition treats them side by side, and this book does too.

    The reader’s problem here differs from the last chapter’s. A compound is long and looks difficult and is in no dictionary; a derivative is short, looks easy, and is in the dictionary under a spelling one would not have guessed. Nepuññaṁ ‘skill’ is nipuṇo ‘skilful’ with a suffix, and it is filed under ne-. Ākiñcaññaṁ ‘nothingness’ is akiñcano ‘having nothing’ with a suffix, and it is filed under ā-. Derivation moves the front of the word, and a dictionary is looked up by the front of the word. That fact matters more to a reader than any list of suffixes, and this chapter is arranged to reach it early.

    Before any of it, though, there is something the tradition says about words that a Buddhist grammar has no business leaving out.

    There are no roots in reality

    All the schools of Buddhism hold that language is conventional: vohāra, agreement, usage. Sounds mean nothing in themselves; meanings are assigned to groups of sounds by social convention, and those meanings then drift through use, which the tradition calls rūḷhi. The Brahmanical view current at the time was the opposite: the Vedic language is eternal, and existed before anyone used it. On the Buddhist view, as Warder puts it, “there are no roots in reality, but we find it convenient to group words around them for descriptive purposes.”1

    This has a practical consequence and a doctrinal one, and they point the same way. Practically: a derivation describes a word and does not decode it, and to take it for a decoding is to be wrong regularly. Doctrinally: the Buddha’s own etymologies in the suttas are teaching devices, not claims about origins, and the grammar here gives no warrant for treating a word’s parts as its truth. Derivation is good for recognition: seeing that a word one does not know is built out of two one does, and knowing where to look it up.

    Two divisions, and a residue

    The tradition sorts derivation in two.2

    A kita suffix is added directly to a root and makes a stem: √kar plus -aka gives kāraka ‘doer’. The words so formed are the kitakanāma, the primary derivatives, and the class is larger than it looks: participles, absolutives and infinitives are all kita formations, so three chapters of this book have been teaching kita suffixes without using the name.

    A taddhita suffix is added to a stem that already exists, whether that stem is itself a primary derivative or an ordinary noun: sīla plus -vant gives sīlavant ‘virtuous’. These are the secondary derivatives, the taddhitanāma.

    The division is real and it matters less than a grammar’s table of contents suggests. Perniola states the reason flatly: “The distinction between primary and secondary suffixes is not of great importance since the same suffixes may be used both as primary and as secondary”: -a is primary in one word and secondary in the next.3 Thānuttamo, whose whole chapter is organised by the division, opens it by quoting that sentence. So the two names are needed because the grammars use them, and they are not a reason to memorise a suffix inventory twice.

    There is a third class, and how the sources handle it is more instructive than the class itself. Kaccāyana recognises the uṇādi affixes (named for the first of them, uṇ, plus ādi ‘and the rest’) for words whose connection to a root is, in Duroiselle’s words, “not clear either in meaning or in form”. He then declines to give them a chapter, folding them in with the kita suffixes and marking each with an asterisk.4 That is a frank way of saying that the class is a residue: what is left when the analysis has done what it can. A reader meets its members constantly and never needs the label.

    Prefixes

    Pāli has a closed class of about twenty prefixes, and closed classes are good news: they can be learnt once. The tradition calls them upasagga, and Aggavaṁsa’s etymology of the term is itself the definition: they “approach and adhere to” nouns and verbs, “specifying their meaning”.5

    Prefix Sense Example
    ati- beyond, across, excessively atikkamati goes beyond
    adhi- over, onto, superior to adhigacchati attains
    anu- after, along, in accordance with anugacchati follows
    apa- away, off apeti goes away
    api- on, over, close upon apidhānaṁ a lid
    abhi- towards, facing, over abhikkamati goes forward
    ava-, o- down, off okkamati descends
    ā- to, near, as far as; reversal āgacchati comes
    u-, ud- up, out, forth uggacchati rises
    upa- to, near, alongside upagacchati approaches
    du-, dur- badly, hard, wrongly dukkara hard to do
    ni- down, into nisīdati sits down
    nī-, nir- out, away, without nikkhamati goes forth
    pa- forth, forward pabbajati goes forth
    parā- away, opposed parābhavati comes to ruin
    pari- around, completely parijānāti fully understands
    pati-, paṭi- back, against, in return paṭipucchati asks in return
    vi- apart, variously, without vigacchati departs
    saṁ- together, fully saṅgacchati meets
    su- well, easily, thoroughly sugati a good destination

    Five of them also work as free words governing a case (anu, apa, abhi, ā and paṭi), and only those; the rest are bound.6 Chapter 12 gives the wider picture, which is that Pāli has almost no prepositions left and uses adverbs, nouns in oblique cases, and absolutives instead.

    What a prefix actually does

    Here the tradition is more useful than any modern grammar, because it names three effects a prefix can have, not one. A prefix may be dhātvatthavisesako, “that which modifies or enhances the meaning of the root”; or dhātvatthabādhako, “that which debars the meaning of the root”, reversing it; or dhātvatthānuvattako, “that which keeps on alongside the meaning of the root”: a prefix that adds nothing at all.7

    The third category saves a reader time. Vigacchati is vi- plus gacchati, and it does mean ‘goes away’; vipassati is vi- plus passati, and it means ‘sees clearly’, with the vi- contributing what one would not have predicted; and there are dozens of verbs where the prefix contributes a colouring so faint that no gloss can catch it. The tradition’s answer is that this is a fact about the language, not a failure of the reader.

    Bodhi arrives at the same place from a sutta-reader’s side and states the consequence: “often one cannot deduce the meaning of a verb simply by adding the nuance of the prefix to the meaning of the basic verb. It is thus advisable always to learn the meaning of a prefixed verb from a dictionary or glossary and to interpret it in context.”8

    The clearest demonstration is a single root run through the prefixes. √ñā ‘know’ gives jānāti ‘knows’, and from it:

    Verb Sense Noun Sense
    abhijānāti knows directly abhiññā direct knowledge
    ājānāti understands — —
    pajānāti discerns, understands paññā wisdom
    paṭijānāti acknowledges, claims paṭiññā a claim
    parijānāti fully understands pariññā full understanding
    vijānāti knows distinctly viññāṇa consciousness
    sañjānāti perceives saññā perception

    Seven prefixes, seven verbs, six nouns, and the nouns are six of the most doctrinally loaded words in the canon. Nothing in the prefixes would have told anyone that saññā is ‘perception’ while viññāṇa is ‘consciousness’; the senses were fixed by use long before any grammarian looked at them. Bodhi notes that viññāṇa may once have carried the distinctness its prefix suggests, and that the nuance “has faded into the background” by the time of the Nikāyas.9 This is rūḷhi, drift through usage, happening in front of us, and it is the best argument in the chapter for learning words instead of deducing them.

    What happens at the seam

    Chapter 2 divided sandhi into junction inside a word and junction between words, and deferred most of the first kind to this chapter and the last. The join inside a compound was Chapter 13’s. What is left is the join between a prefix and what follows it, and there are only a few things to know.

    Some prefixes have a hidden final consonant which appears when a vowel follows and assimilates when a consonant does. Warder writes these with the consonant in brackets (u(d), du(r), puna(d), saki(d)), a good habit to borrow.10 So ud- plus gacchati gives uggacchati, the d assimilating to the following g; dur- plus labha gives dullabha ‘hard to get’, the r assimilating to the l; nī- plus √kam gives nikkhamati ‘goes forth’; puna(d) plus bhava gives punabbhava ‘renewed existence’. If you do not know the consonant is there you will look for nikkhamati under a root beginning with kh.

    Learn saṁ- in full, because it changes form more than any other and is everywhere. Before a vowel the m stays: samaya, samādhi, samudaya. Before a stop it becomes the nasal of that stop’s own class: saṅkhārā, sañjānāti, saṇṭhāna, sandiṭṭhika. Before a labial it stays m: samphassa, sambodhi. Before y, v, s and h it becomes niggahīta: saṁyojana, saṁvara, saṁsāra, saṁhita. And before l it assimilates outright: sallekha.11 Seven forms, one prefix. Recognise them and you recover a great deal of vocabulary at a stroke.

    Prefixes stack, and when they do they behave as one: adhi plus ā plus vasati gives ajjhāvasati ‘inhabits’; saṁ plus anu plus passati gives samanupassati ‘regards’; saṁ plus ud plus ā plus carati gives samudācarati ‘behaves towards’.12 Three prefixes on one root is not rare.

    Prefixes that are not upasagga

    Three common word-initial elements are not in the traditional class, and the reason matters, because two of them behave differently.

    The negative a-, an- before a vowel, is a particle, not a prefix, in the tradition’s terms, and unlike an upasagga it attaches to almost anything: nouns, adjectives, participles, whole compounds. Anāgata ‘not come, future’, avitatha ‘not unreal’, appaṭivedha ‘non-penetration’. Where the following word begins with a consonant cluster the vowel is often absorbed and the consonant doubles, which is how paṭivedha becomes appaṭivedha.13

    Sa- ‘with, having’, and vīta- ‘devoid of’, are nouns and participles used as prefixes, and they turn what follows into an adjective, that is, into a bahubbīhi. Sadevaka ‘with its gods’, samāraka ‘with Māra’, sabrahmaka ‘with Brahmā’; vītarāga ‘free of lust’. Chapter 13 treats these under the bahubbīhi, which is where they belong.14

    Primary derivation: building on a root

    The kita suffixes go on a root, and mainly they change its vowel. So the grades come first, and the two kinds of noun the suffixes make, the name of an action and the name of its doer, come after.

    Three grades of the root vowel

    A root can appear in a derivative in any of three grades, and the grade is often the only thing distinguishing two words that share a root and mean different things. Chapter 7 introduced the ladder for the verb; it is the same ladder here.15

    Grade Root Derivative Sense
    weak √dis disa seeing (only bound)
    guṇa √budh bodha understanding (only bound)
    guṇa √gam gama going (only bound)
    guṇa √muh moha delusion
    guṇa √gah gaha grasping
    vuddhi √vad vāda speech, doctrine
    vuddhi √caj cāga giving up, generosity
    vuddhi √raj rāga passion
    vuddhi √har hāra carrying

    The pair that makes the rule teachable is Warder’s. From √kar, the plain suffix -a gives kara ‘making, causing’, with no change to the root; the suffix the grammarians write ṇa gives kāra ‘maker, doer’, with vuddhi. Two suffixes of identical shape, distinguished only by what they do to the vowel in front of them.16 Kumbhakāro is a potter; dukkara is ‘hard to do’.

    Two further pieces of notation from the same source explain a whole family of short words: the suffix the grammarians call ra elides the root’s final consonant and its vowel, leaving -ga from √gam and -ja from √jan; and a zero suffix, called kvi, does the same with nothing added, so that √gam yields the -ga of urago ‘snake’, literally ‘chest-goer’.17 These are the bound final members Chapter 13 listed and could not explain: -ga, -ja, -da, -ṭṭha, -pa, -ññū, -vidū, -bhū. They cannot stand alone. They exist only at the end of a compound, and this is where they come from.

    Action nouns

    An action noun names the doing of the thing. English makes them with -ing, -ment, -ation; Pāli has four, and between them they account for an enormous share of the doctrinal vocabulary.

    -ana, neuter, and -anā, feminine, are the commonest and the most transparent. Dassana ‘seeing’, sāsana ‘instruction’, ṭhāna ‘standing, place’, karaṇa ‘doing’, bhājana ‘vessel’, vāhana ‘carrying’; vedanā ‘feeling’, cetanā ‘intention’, codanā ‘reproof’. After a root ending in r or h the n is retroflex: kāraṇa, maraṇa.18

    -ti, feminine, is the second, and it misleads beginners, because a noun in -ti looks like a third-person singular verb. Gati ‘going, destination’, mati ‘thought’, sati ‘mindfulness’, khanti ‘patience’, vimutti ‘liberation’, patti ‘attainment’, cuti ‘passing away’, ñāti ‘relative’. The root is normally in its weak grade, and the consonant meeting the t often assimilates, so vimutti does not look like muñcati and ñatti does not look like ñāpeti.19

    -a with vuddhi, already met above, makes a mass of them: vāda, cāga, rāga, hāra, vihāra, uppāda. And -tā makes them from stems, not roots, which puts it in the next section.

    Agent nouns

    An agent noun names the doer. Four suffixes cover nearly all of them.

    -tar, declined as at Appendix A.1, gives the agent nouns closest to the verb: satthā ‘teacher’ (√sās), kattā ‘maker’, netā ‘leader’, dātā ‘giver’, bhāsitā ‘speaker’. These are Chapter 3’s r-stems, and a reader meets the nominative in -ā.20

    -aka, which the grammarians call ṇvu and which therefore carries vuddhi where the root allows, gives adjectival agents that decline like ordinary -a stems: kāraka ‘doer’, sāvaka ‘hearer, disciple’, dāyaka ‘giver’, yācaka ‘beggar’, upāsaka ‘lay follower’. The feminine is in -ikā: upāsikā, dāyikā, paricārikā ‘attendant’.21

    -in, feminine -inī, is at once an agent suffix and a possessive one, and the difference is only in what it is added to: added to a root it makes a doer, added to a noun it makes a haver. Gavesī ‘seeker’, dāyī ‘giver’, brahmacārī ‘one who lives the holy life’, musāvādī ‘one who speaks falsely’; and on the possessive side saññī ‘having perception’, pakkhī ‘winged, a bird’, hatthī ‘having a hand, an elephant’. The stem is -in and the nominative singular is -ī, so a dictionary’s hatthin and a text’s hatthī are the same word.22

    -ū, finally, makes agent nouns straight from a verbal root: pāragū ‘gone to the far shore’, vedagū ‘one who has attained knowledge’, kālaññū ‘knowing the right time’, atthaññū ‘knowing the meaning’, abhibhū ‘conqueror’. Four of the five are the bound finals Chapter 13 met, and the last is not: abhibhū is a prefix on a root, and it is a word in its own right. The suffix is not tied to compounding.23

    Secondary derivation: building on a word

    The taddhita suffixes go on a stem that already exists, and they strengthen a vowel too. But not the same one, and that difference makes a derivative hard to look up.

    The vuddhi is at the front

    Here is the rule this chapter exists to teach. When a secondary suffix is added, the first vowel of the word is strengthened, not the vowel next to the suffix.24 The ladder is the ordinary one: a becomes ā, i becomes e, u becomes o. A vowel already long, or long by position because a cluster follows, stays as it is.

    Base Derivative Sense
    kusalaṁ ‘skilled’ kosallaṁ proficiency
    nipuṇo ‘skilful’ nepuññaṁ skill
    paṇḍito ‘wise’ paṇḍiccaṁ erudition
    puthujjano ‘ordinary person’ pothujjaniko of the ordinary person
    punabbhavo ‘renewed existence’ ponobhavikā leading to renewed existence
    gilāno ‘sick’ gelaññaṁ sickness
    upa- + √nī ‘lead on’ opaneyyika leading onwards
    nigamo ‘town’ negamā townsfolk
    janapado ‘country’ jānapado countryman
    garu ‘heavy, venerable’ gāravo respect
    adhipati ‘lord’ ādhipateyyaṁ lordship, dominion

    So the dictionary entry is where it is. Meet nepuññaṁ, look under ni-, and you will not find it; the word is filed under ne-, and the rule turns a dead end into a lookup.

    It does not apply to everything, and one of the two exceptions needs stating. The possessive suffix -in does not take the front vuddhi: from vicikicchā ‘doubt’ the canon makes vicikicchī ‘doubting’, not vecikicchī.25 Nor do the primary suffixes generally, which strengthen the root itself if they strengthen anything.

    When the suffix is -ya, a second thing happens on top of the vuddhi: the y assimilates to whatever consonant precedes it, and the results are so unlike their bases that the words look unrelated. A dental gives -cca, ṇ or n gives -ñña, l gives -lla, d gives -jja.

    • paṇḍito ‘wise’ + -ya → paṇḍiccaṁ ‘erudition’
    • samaṇo ‘ascetic’ + -ya → sāmaññaṁ ‘the ascetic life’
    • kusalaṁ ‘skilled’ + -ya → kosallaṁ ‘proficiency’
    • bahussuto ‘much-learned’ + -ya → bāhusaccaṁ ‘learning’
    • su- + mano ‘mind’ + -ya → somanassaṁ ‘gladness’
    • akiñcano ‘having nothing’ + -ya → ākiñcaññaṁ ‘nothingness’

    Sāmaññaṁ and ākiñcaññaṁ are two of the most familiar words in the canon, and neither is recognisable from its base without the rule.26

    Abstract nouns

    Pāli makes abstract nouns freely, from any noun or adjective, and with the two commonest suffixes you can read a great many words never met before.

    -tā is always feminine and -tta, or -ttaṁ, almost always neuter, and the two are often both available from the same stem.27 Suññatā ‘emptiness’, devatā ‘deity’, lahutā ‘lightness’, thiratā ‘firmness’, kataññutā ‘gratitude’; manussatta ‘the state of being human’, ekatta ‘oneness’, sāvakatta ‘discipleship’, dukkhatā ‘painfulness’.

    -tā is distinctive in attaching to things much larger than a stem. It goes on a future passive participle (karaṇīyatā ‘the being-to-be-done’, bhabbatā ‘possibility’) and on a whole compound, where a reader meets it most: appicchatā ‘having few wishes’, anāgāmitā ‘the state of non-return’, kammaññatā ‘workability’, katapuññatā ‘the fact of having made merit’.28 English does the same thing with -ness, and just as unrestrictedly.

    The suffix -ya, with the front vuddhi and the assimilations above, is the third abstract and the one that produces the least recognisable results. Sāmañña, bāhusacca, ākiñcañña, ārogya ‘health’, dussīlya ‘unvirtuousness’, kāveyya ‘poetry’.

    Having, belonging, descending

    The remaining secondary suffixes sort by sense, not by form, and the classes overlap; what follows is the working set.

    Possession, ‘having X’, is the largest class. -vant and -mant decline like the (m/v)ant stems at Appendix A.1 and are the commonest: sīlavā ‘virtuous’, bhagavā ‘fortunate, the Blessed One’, paññavā ‘wise’, balavā ‘strong’, cakkhumā ‘having eyes’, satimā ‘mindful’, hirimā ‘having a sense of shame’. Note the nominative singular in -ā, the form a text prints; the -vant and -mant of a dictionary entry are stems.29 -in serves the same purpose, as above. -ika and -ita and a plain -a also form possessives (daṇḍika ‘having a staff’, saddha ‘having faith’, pañña ‘having wisdom’ in compounds), and -ālu expresses a settled disposition: abhijjhālu ‘covetous by nature’.

    Belonging, connection, origin are expressed by -ika, which Thānuttamo lists under sixteen distinct senses with a Kaccāyana rule for each, and of -iya and -eyya.30 The senses read as a spread, not a list: nāvika ‘sailor’ (living by a boat), kālika ‘temporal’, mānasika ‘mental’, kāyika ‘bodily’, dhammika ‘righteous’, cetasika ‘mental factor’, māgadhika ‘Magadhan’, nerayika ‘hell-bound’, attaniya ‘belonging to a self’, upādāniya ‘liable to clinging’, saṁyojaniya ‘liable to fetter’, dakkhiṇeyya ‘worthy of offerings’, pātheyya ‘provisions for a journey’. What they share is a relation, and what the relation is has to come from the base and the context.

    Descent, the patronymic, uses -āyana, -āna, -era, -eyya and a bare vuddhi. Kaccāyana and Kaccāna are both ‘descendant of Kacca’ and both are canonical names; sāmaṇera ‘novice’ is samaṇa plus -era, literally the ascetic’s own; Vāseṭṭha is of the Vasiṭṭha clan; Māgadha is of Magadha.31 The class matters less for reading grammar than for reading names, where a reader will mostly meet it.

    The suffix that changes nothing

    -ka, and its longer form -aka, is the suffix a reader most needs to be warned about, because it does five different things and one of them is nothing at all.32

    It makes agent nouns, as above. It makes diminutives: puttaka ‘little son’, kumāraka ‘boy’, kuṭikā ‘hut’. It makes contemptuous forms, which is the diminutive turned sour: samaṇaka ‘a wretched little ascetic’, muṇḍaka ‘shaveling’. It turns adverbs and pronouns into adjectives: adhika ‘exceeding’, māmaka ‘mine, devoted to me’. And it attaches to nouns and adjectives and changes nothing whatever: khuddaka is khudda ‘small’, jeṭṭhaka is jeṭṭha ‘eldest’, ācariyaka is ācariya ‘teacher’, mahallaka is ‘old’.

    The last of these trips a reader up, because a -ka that means nothing looks like a -ka that means something. There is no test. When a word in -ka does not make sense as a diminutive or an agent, try it as the plain word, and it usually is one. Chapter 13’s -ika on a finished compound (ponobhavikā, anulomikaṁ) is a sixth use, on the far side of the boundary between the two chapters.

    Verbs made from nouns and adverbs

    One formation remains, and it is common in the canon and absent from every source consulted for this chapter except one.33

    An adverb or a noun may be compounded with karoti or bhavati to make a fixed expression that behaves as a single verb. Pātukaroti ‘makes manifest’ and pātubhavati ‘becomes manifest’; āvikaroti ‘makes clear’; purakkharoti ‘puts in front, honours’; antaradhāyati ‘disappears’, where antara ‘interval’ stands unchanged in front of the verb.

    More often the first member takes a final -ī or -i before the verb, and this small formation carries a surprising amount of doctrinal vocabulary:

    Compound verb Sense
    bahulīkaroti makes much of, cultivates
    sacchikaroti realises for oneself
    tuṇhībhūto fallen silent
    sītibhūto become cool
    vasībhūto become a master

    Bahulīkaroti occurs 134 times in the root texts, almost always in the phrase bhāvitā bahulīkatā, ‘developed and cultivated’, which closes the description of every practice the Buddha teaches. Sacchikaroti is the verb of realisation itself.34

    A verse made of derivatives

    The Maṅgala Sutta is among the first things a Buddhist learns by heart, and almost every substantive in it is a derived noun. Three of its verses show four of this chapter’s formations at work in eight lines.

    Asevanā ca bālānaṁ, paṇḍitānañca sevanā; Pūjā ca pūjaneyyānaṁ, etaṁ maṅgalamuttamaṁ. (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 46)

    asevanā< a- not + √sev + -anā — non-association f nom sgcaandbālānaṁwith foolsm gen plpaṇḍitānaṁwith the wisem gen plcaandsevanāassociationpūjāhonouringcaandpūjaneyyānaṁof those worthy of honourm gen pletaṁthismaṅgalamuttamaṁ< maṅgalaṁ blessing + uttamaṁ highest

    Not associating with fools, and associating with the wise, and honouring those worthy of honour: this is the highest blessing.

    Asevanā and sevanā are one action noun in -anā, the same word twice with and without the negative particle, and the verse rests on the pair. Pūjaneyyānaṁ is the future passive participle in -aneyya, ‘to be honoured’, in the genitive plural.

    Bāhusaccañca sippañca, vinayo ca susikkhito; (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 47)

    bāhusaccaṁ< bahu much + suta heard + -ya — learning n nom sgcaandsippaṁcraftcaandvinayodisciplinecaandsusikkhito< su- well + sikkhita trained

    Learning and a craft, and discipline well trained in;

    Bāhusaccaṁ is the whole of the last two sections in one word: a compound base, the suffix -ya, vuddhi of the first vowel, and the assimilation of ty to cc. Nothing of bahussuta survives on the surface.

    Mātāpitu upaṭṭhānaṁ, puttadārassa saṅgaho; (Snp 2.4 — Sn p. 47)

    mātāpitumother and fatherdvanda, gen dual senseupaṭṭhānaṁ< upa- near + √ṭhā + -ana — attending on n nom sgputtadārassaof children and spousem gen sgsaṅgaho< saṁ- together + √gah + -a — support m nom sg

    Attending on mother and father, supporting children and spouse;

    Upaṭṭhānaṁ is the -ana action noun on a prefixed root, and saṅgaho is the -a action noun on another, with the prefix in its assimilated shape. Between them the two verses have used the -anā, -ana, -a, -ya and -aneyya suffixes and three prefixes, and there is nothing unusual about them; this is simply what Pāli verse is made of.

    The same is true of the formula the reader met in the last chapter. Sandiṭṭhiko akāliko ehipassiko opaneyyiko: four epithets of the Dhamma, all four adjectives in -ika, and the last of them shows the front strengthening clearly: the prefix upa- has become opa-. The other three do not, and each for a different reason, which is a fair sample of how the rule behaves in practice: saṁ- has no vowel to strengthen, the negative a- is not one of the affixes that trigger it, and ehi- is an imperative, not a stem.35

    Where word formation is treated elsewhere

    The comparative and superlative suffixes (-tara, -tama, -iya, -iṭṭha, -ma) are taddhita affixes and are taught in the chapter on gender and adjectives, where a reader meets them. The numeral suffixes (-ma and -tiya for the ordinals, -khattuṁ, -dhā, -so, -guṇa) are in the chapter on numerals. The adverb-forming suffixes (-to, -tra, -ttha, -dā, -thā) are taddhita too, and are in the chapter on indeclinables, since what a reader needs about them is what they do in a sentence.

    The participle, absolutive and infinitive suffixes are all kita, and have their own two chapters. The denominative, causative, passive and desiderative stems are in the chapter on derived conjugations: they build verbs, not nouns, and the tradition sorts them separately. The join inside a compound is in the chapter on compounds; this chapter has taken only the join after a prefix. And the full inventory of suffixes and prefixes, as Ñāṇatusita tabulates it, is at Appendix A.4.

    What derivation will not tell you

    Three cautions, in ascending order of discomfort, and all three are the sources’ own.

    Words from one root diverge. Warder is blunt about the consequence: “In the case of nouns, however, it is usually more convenient to learn the separate words without much attention to derivation, especially as the meanings of nouns derived from a common root are often widely divergent and it is these distinctions of meaning, rather than any similarity, which it is essential to discover and remember.”36 The ñā family above is the demonstration: seven prefixes on one root give seven words no arithmetic would predict.

    A prefixed verb is a word, not a sum. This is Bodhi’s warning, quoted in full above, and so a good glossary is worth more than a good rule.

    And a derivative may look like its base. Gotamo the clan name is Gotamo the ancestor plus a suffix that leaves no trace, and only the context says which is meant. So the absence of a visible suffix is not evidence that no derivation has taken place, and that sums up fairly why this chapter claims to help a reader recognise words, not generate them.

    None of this makes the chapter useless; it makes it a chapter about reading more than parsing. Know that the vuddhi is at the front, that -tā attaches to anything, that -ka often means nothing, and that a -ti noun is not a verb, and you will get through a page of canon markedly faster. The chapter claims no more.
