    Appendices

    Appendix D: Bibliography and Sources Consulted

    Every grammar, dictionary, coursebook and text this book draws on, so that the synthesis can be traced to its sources.

    A word about what this list does and does not tell you. Where two reputable grammars differ only in how they classify or present a point, this book picks the clearest treatment and states it as fact, without a footnote, as any textbook does. That silence is deliberate, and it would leave a reader unable to tell what was synthesised from what, so two things close the gap: this list, and the short Principal sources line that closes each chapter, naming the one to three works it leans on most. Where the grammars disagree about a point of substance, or where a form’s attestation is in question, the chapter says so in a footnote and names who says what.

    The entries are grouped by what kind of work each is and run alphabetically within each group. They are not ranked.

    How the citations work. A page number is always the printed page of the edition listed here, and where an entry names the copy consulted, it is that copy’s page. A grammar that numbers its sections is cited by section and page, as in Oberlies §41, p. 178. A dictionary is cited by headword (s.v.). The indigenous grammars are cited by rule number, as the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition numbers them. A canonical text is cited by the PTS volume and page of the passage itself.

    Edition details are those of each work’s own title page. Where the copy consulted could not settle the edition, the entry says so instead of guessing.

    Grammars and reference works

    • Ānandajoti Bhikkhu (version 3.5, January 2006). An Outline of the Metres in the Pāḷi Canon. Cited by its own section numbers (§1.1). The principal source for the metre chapter.
    • Ānandajoti Bhikkhu (version 2, September 2014). A Practical Guide to Pāḷi Grammar. Tables and notes for the beginner; cited once, for its present-participle paradigm, by section and page (“Verbs”, p. 10).
    • Bomhard, Allan R. (2012). An Introductory Grammar of the Pali Language. Consulted, but only where a point is corroborated elsewhere: the author’s scholarly reputation lies in the Nostratic hypothesis, which mainstream historical linguistics rejects, and there is no evidence of Buddhist-studies peer review for this grammar specifically. It has nonetheless supplied at least one thing no other grammar here did, ātumā as a reflexive beside attā, so it is kept, with the caution attached.
    • Duroiselle, Charles (1906; 3rd ed. 1921). A Practical Grammar of the Pali Language. Rangoon: British Burma Press. The copy consulted is the third edition of 1921, and its pages and sections are the ones meant. Thānuttamo and Bodhi cite the online re-edition of 1997, which is paginated and sectioned differently. Dated but still in active use, and unusually well organised. His paradigms sometimes carry forms derived by the indigenous grammarians without marking them as such, so a form taken from him is checked against the canon before it is taught.
    • Geiger, Wilhelm (1916; rev. ed. 1994). A Pāli Grammar. Translated by Batakrishna Ghosh, revised and edited by K. R. Norman, with a foreword by Richard Gombrich. Oxford: Pali Text Society; the copy consulted is the 2005 printing. Geiger marks rare forms by his own declared notation, and so he and Oberlies can appear to disagree when they do not.
    • Geiger, Wilhelm (1943; Eng. trans. B. Ghosh). Pāli Literature and Language. Distinct from his grammar, and consulted for a different job: its introduction surveys the whole nineteenth- and early twentieth-century argument about where Pāli came from (Māgadhī, Ujjayinī, the Vindhya region, Kaliṅga), and then declines the modern consensus and returns to the tradition. Used in the introduction for the history of the question and for the case against the position this book takes.
    • Hinüber, Oskar von (1996). A Handbook of Pāli Literature. Consulted on questions of transmission and dating, not on morphology.
    • Norman, K. R. (1997). A Philological Approach to Buddhism. School of Oriental and African Studies. The Bukkyō Dendō Kyōkai lectures of 1994. Lecture IV is the fullest treatment in this collection of the dialect question and of the sakāya niruttiyā passage, and it is careful where the tradition and the older handbooks are not: it sets out how many ways the two decisive words can be read before drawing anything from them.
    • Oberlies, Thomas (2001). Pāli: A Grammar of the Language of the Theravāda Tipiṭaka. Indian Philology and South Asian Studies 3. Berlin: de Gruyter. The current scholarly standard, and the work this book relies on most heavily for the forms. Its distinguishing virtue is that it records attestation per form and flags what the grammarians derive but no text uses, a discipline no other grammar here practises, and the source of several of this book’s footnotes. Every citation of the form “Oberlies §N” in this book is to this edition, whose section numbering is its own.
    • Oberlies, Thomas (2019). Pāli Grammar: The Language of the Canonical Texts in Theravāda Buddhism. Bristol: Pali Text Society. His later and much longer grammar, not consulted directly here. It is named only in footnotes, each time reporting a page Thānuttamo cites, and is listed separately so that those page numbers are not read against the 2001 edition, where they do not exist.
    • Perniola, Vito (1997). Pali Grammar. Pali Text Society. Exceptionally well organised: it states its inventory up front, which makes it the fastest way to find out what a topic contains. A separate diachronic chapter carries the history of the forms.
    • Thānuttamo Bhikkhu (2021). Māgadhabhāsā: A Compendious Grammar. Version of May 2021. Cross-references Kaccāyana against the modern grammars throughout, and is this book’s route to how the tradition itself analyses a point. Its own page references to other works are unreliable and are not followed without checking.
    • Karpik, Stefan (2019). “The Buddha taught in Pali: A working hypothesis”. Journal of the Oxford Centre for Buddhist Studies 16, 10–86. The fullest recent statement of the case that the traditional account has not been disproved. Used in the introduction for three things that are checkable and check out: that Māgadhī is absent from the canon and the commentaries, that the commentary on the disrobing rule glosses ariyaka as māgadhabhāsā and contrasts it with Dravidian, and that bhikkhave is effectively absent from verse.
    • Levman, Bryan (2008–09). “Sakāya niruttiyā revisited”. Bulletin d’Études Indiennes 26–27, 33–51. The article the sakāya niruttiyā section leans on: it assembles the roll-call of who has read the phrase which way, restates Geiger’s 1922 grammatical argument, and argues from the canonical occurrences that nirutti means neither “dialect” nor “language”.
    • Levman, Bryan (2020). Pāli: The Language of the Medium of the Message. On Pāli as an oral and rhetorical medium.
    • Levman, Bryan (2023). Pāli and Buddhism: Language and Lineage. The single most useful work in this collection on the linguistic situation the Buddha taught in: the koine thesis, the Dravidian and Munda substrate, and the comparison with Gāndhārī. Its diacritics are not always reliable in the printed text, so every form quoted from it has been checked against the primary text.
    • Wijesekera, O. H. de A. (1936; Ānandajoti’s proofread digital edition, 2016). Syntax of the Cases in the Pāli Nikāyas. The principal source for the case-syntax chapter, and for questions of what a construction does anywhere in the book.

    Coursebooks and readers

    • Bodhi, Bhikkhu (2020). Reading the Buddha’s Discourses in Pāli. Wisdom Publications. The copy consulted is Bodhi’s own PDF of December 2021, which is paginated differently from the printed book. So citations give his section title, which finds the passage in either, and the page of that PDF. The model for this book’s three-layer example presentation (Pāli, literal gloss, idiomatic rendering), not a grammar authority in its own right.
    • Buddhadatta, A. P. The New Pali Course. Colombo. The edition could not be confirmed from the copy consulted.
    • Collins, Steven (2006). A Pali Grammar for Students. Chiang Mai: Silkworm Books. Consulted for how a point is best sequenced for a learner, and for bridging European and Saddanīti-derived categories.
    • De Silva, Lily (2008). Pali Primer. A beginner’s course, and useful here for that reason: where a form appears in a book for absolute beginners, its currency is not in doubt.
    • Gair, James W. & W. S. Karunatillake (1998). A New Course in Reading Pāli: Entering the Word of the Buddha. Motilal Banarsidass. The copy consulted is a digitised text, so this book cites it by lesson and section and gives the printed page that text records. One further page reference, p. 127, is Thānuttamo’s.
    • Meiland, Justin (2010). Additions to Warder’s Introduction to Pali.
    • Sunyo Bhikkhu. Pali Course (draft). The course handouts the first fourteen chapters of this book were built out of: lessons in plain text and PDF, with answer keys, mapping onto this book’s Parts I to VI. Little of the wording survives the redrafting; the sequencing largely does.
    • Warder, A. K. (1963; 3rd ed. 1991). Introduction to Pali. Pali Text Society.

    Learning and pedagogy

    Cited in the front matter, where the book says how to study it, and nowhere else. These are meta-analyses of second-language learning and of study technique, so they answer a question no Pāli grammar can: what works for an adult learning to read a language, as against what the language does. They study modern languages learned partly for conversation, and no comparable synthesis of classical-language teaching exists, which is stated where they are used.

    • Al-Hoorie, A. H. (2018). “The L2 Motivational Self System: A Meta-Analysis”. Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching 8(4), 721–754. 39 samples, 32,078 learners. Cited for the finding that motivation predicts effort far more strongly than achievement.
    • Donoghue, G. M. & Hattie, J. A. C. (2021). “A Meta-Analysis of Ten Learning Techniques”. Frontiers in Education 6:581216. 242 studies, 1,619 effects, 169,179 participants.
    • Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J. & Willingham, D. T. (2013). “Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology”. Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4–58. The source of the ten-technique list Donoghue and Hattie later re-measured; both put practice testing and distributed practice first.
    • Jeon, E.-Y. & Day, R. R. (2016). “The Effectiveness of ER on Reading Proficiency: A Meta-Analysis”. Reading in a Foreign Language 28(2), 246–265.
    • Nakanishi, T. (2015). “A Meta-Analysis of Extensive Reading Research”. TESOL Quarterly 49(1), 6–37.
    • Nation, I. S. P. (2006). “How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?”. Canadian Modern Language Review 63(1), 59–82. The source of the 98% coverage threshold, on which Appendix B’s grading rests.
    • Norris, J. M. & Ortega, L. (2000). “Effectiveness of L2 Instruction: A Research Synthesis and Quantitative Meta-Analysis”. Language Learning 50(3), 417–528. Cited for the finding that explicit instruction outperforms implicit, which is the evidence that a grammar-forward book is the right instrument for this reader.

    Paradigm tables

    • Ñāṇatusita, Bhikkhu (2005). Pāḷi Noun Declension, Pronoun Declension and Verb Conjugation Tables. Forest Hermitage, Kandy, Sri Lanka. © 2005 Bhikkhu Ñāṇatusita, freely distributable for non-commercial purposes. The principal source for this book’s paradigm tables. They are themselves a synthesis, compiled, by his own note, against Ānanda Maitreyya, Buddhadatta, Duroiselle, Fahs, Geiger, Perniola, Warder and Wijesekera, and they give the full set of attested endings per cell, marking the rare, poetic and post-canonical forms in italics, a distinction preserved in the tables here. Every paradigm is cross-checked against Oberlies before it lands.

    Dictionaries

    Where the dictionaries differ on a sense, this book follows Cone, then the Critical Pāli Dictionary within its completed range, then the PED, then Buddhadatta; the Digital Pāli Dictionary is the practical first port of call and the printed dictionaries are the court of appeal. Any contested gloss gets a footnote naming who says what.

    • Buddhadatta, A. P. Concise Pali-English Dictionary. 2nd ed., preface dated 1958.
    • Cone, Margaret. A Dictionary of Pāli. Pali Text Society. Part I, a–kh (2001); Part II, g–n (2010).
    • Critical Pāli Dictionary (CPD). Rhys Davids, Trenckner, Andersen, Smith et al. The highest authority within its completed range, a–kāreti.
    • Digital Pāli Dictionary (DPD). Consulted for every form this book teaches, not only for glosses: it carries construction, Sanskrit cognate and a canonical example per headword, and so it can serve as a first check on whether a form is real. DPD is a living database and is revised continually; this book consulted it between July and October 2026, most lookups against a copy taken in August 2026. Where DPD has two headwords of one spelling, the citation gives its number, santa 2.1.
    • Rhys Davids, T. W. & William Stede. Pali-English Dictionary (PED). Pali Text Society.

    The canon: text and translation

    • Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition (Vipassana Research Institute). The source of every Pāli passage this book quotes, and the text every quotation was checked against. It records the page on which each sutta opens in the Pali Text Society edition, marks the page breaks of four printed editions, and carries its own apparatus of variant readings, which is what lets a variant be quoted from the edition that records it and not from a secondary report of it. It includes the aṭṭhakathā and ṭīkā as well as the root texts, which makes it this book’s instrument for two jobs the translations cannot do: checking a point that needs commentarial support, and establishing whether a form the grammars print is one the canon actually uses. The edition spells the niggahīta ṃ; this book normalises it to ṁ. Every printed count of how often a form occurs was measured over this text.
    • The commentaries, sub-commentaries and post-canonical works: the aṭṭhakathā (the Samantapāsādikā, Sumaṅgalavilāsinī and the rest), the ṭīkā, and the Visuddhimagga, read in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana above. Cited by the PTS volume and page where that edition records one, as it does for the aṭṭhakathā and the Dīgha ṭīkā (Sp VII 1399); otherwise, as for the Visuddhimagga and the other ṭīkā, by work, the section it comments on, and the edition’s own paragraph number (Visuddhimagga-mahāṭīkā, Paññābhūminiddesa, ¶580), which finds the passage in the digital edition. The sigla are the Critical Pāli Dictionary’s: Sp Samantapāsādikā (Vinaya), Sv Sumaṅgalavilāsinī (DN), Ps Papañcasūdanī (MN), Spk Sāratthappakāsinī (SN), Mp Manorathapūraṇī (AN), Dhp-a the Dhammapada commentary, Ud-a the Paramatthadīpanī on the Udāna, Nidd-a the Saddhammapajjotikā on the Niddesa, Paṭis-a the Saddhammappakāsinī on the Paṭisambhidāmagga, As the Dhammasaṅgaṇī commentary, Vibh-a the Sammohavinodanī (Vibhaṅga), and Sv-pṭ the Dīgha ṭīkā.
    • The indigenous grammars and lexicon: Kaccāyana’s grammar, Aggavaṁsa’s Saddanīti, Buddhappiya’s Padarūpasiddhi, Moggallāna’s grammar, the Payogasiddhi and the Abhidhānappadīpikā, read in the same edition and cited by its rule numbers. Older editions number Kaccāyana differently: Wijesekera follows Guṇaratana’s edition (Colombo, 1913), and so his rule numbers do not always match the ones given here.
    • Bodhi, Bhikkhu, The Middle Length Discourses, The Connected Discourses, The Numerical Discourses; Walshe, Maurice, The Long Discourses of the Buddha. Wisdom Publications, in copyright. Quoted in a few footnotes as comparators, where a translator’s reading of a form is evidence about the grammar, and never as this book’s own renderings.

    Style

    • Style Manual for Authors, Editors and Printers (Australian Government). First authority for mechanics: spelling, punctuation, capitalisation, abbreviation. Australian and British spellings throughout.
    • The Oxford Guide to Style / New Oxford Style Manual. For anything the Australian manual leaves open. Where a style manual and a Pāli convention collide (diacritics, italicisation, citation form), the Pāli convention governs.
