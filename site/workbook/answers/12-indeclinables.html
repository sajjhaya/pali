    Answers: Indeclinables and Particles

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Tesaṁ divā ca ratto ca, sadā puññaṁ pavaḍḍhati. (SN 1.47 — S i 33)

      Divā is an old instrumental; ratto is an old locative. The pairing is unremarkable because neither is behaving as a case any more. Both have frozen into indeclinable adverbs, and once a form has stopped taking part in the case system there is nothing left for a mismatch to be a mismatch in.

      To make the two agree is to apply a rule to words that have left its jurisdiction, and that is the difficulty of this whole chapter in one line: from here on, a word’s form no longer shows its function. Item 21 asks for divā again, out of its sentence, and the answer is the same.

      Ca has its ordinary joining function here (samuccaya), between two words that are not the same case. That is a second small demonstration of the same point, since ca joins two adverbs, not two nominatives.

    2. Ruppatī’ti kho, bhikkhave, tasmā ’rūpa’nti vuccati. (SN 22.79 — S iii 86)

      The first iti is causal (hetu); the second is the ordinary quotative. The first turns the clause ‘it is deformed’ into the reason for what follows; the second marks rūpaṁ as the name being applied.

      The first answers to tasmā. ‘Therefore’ picks the reason up and delivers the conclusion, and if the first iti is read as a quotation mark, tasmā answers to nothing at all. So when a sentence has a tasmā in it, look back for what supplies the ‘because’.

      The “quotation mark” account of iti cannot reach this sense. Set 09 read the same sentence for its two passives, ruppati and vuccati; that reading and this one concern the same eight words and have nothing else in common.

    3. Kiṁ nu kho, āvuso gotama, taṁ jīvaṁ taṁ sarīraṁ, udāhu aññaṁ jīvaṁ aññaṁ sarīran’ti? (DN 7 — D i 159)

      The two particles are nu, which marks the whole utterance as a question, and udāhu, which introduces the second of exactly two alternatives. Together they build what logic calls an exclusive disjunction: either the soul and the body are one thing, or they are two, and no third answer is on offer.

      So the question is one of the undeclared ones, a classic case, and the Buddha’s refusal to answer it is a refusal of the frame. Here the frame is built out of two words that do not inflect. Kiṁ is a third: it is the interrogative pronoun, but standing at the head with nu kho it does little more than open the question.

    4. Selo yathā ekaghano, vātena na samīrati; Evaṁ nindāpasaṁsāsu, na samiñjanti paṇḍitā. (Dhp 81 — Dhp p. 12)

      Yathā and evaṁ carry the simile, and the verse has no verb of comparison in it at all. English needs one (“as … so”, or “is like”, or “resembles”); Pāli sets the two clauses side by side with a correlative pair holding them together. Neither word inflects and neither refers to anything, yet between them they make the whole analogy.

      The two nas could not have been reduced to one, because they negate two different verbs in two different clauses: the rock is not shaken, the wise are not moved. Take the second away and the second clause says the opposite of what the verse means. Na is the word on the page a reader most easily skips, and here the sense depends on it twice.

      DPD gives samīrati as saṁ + īra + ti from √īr and samiñjanti as saṁ + iñja + ti from √iñj, two different roots of nearly the same meaning, one for the rock and one for the wise. The verse avoids repeating itself.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Ayaṁ kho me, samma, sāṇabhāro dūrābhato ca susannaddho ca, alaṁ me tvaṁ pajānāhī’ti. (DN 23 — D ii 351)

      Alaṁ refuses. It means ‘enough of that’, a declining of the offer, and not ‘enough’ in the sense of a quantity being sufficient; the dative me names the person for whom it is enough. English needs a clause where Pāli needs a syllable.

      The chapter files alaṁ under paṭisedha, denying and forbidding, alongside na, no and mā, because it closes something down instead of modifying anything. What follows it here, tvaṁ pajānāhi, “you know for yourself”, is the polite equivalent of “suit yourself”, and the two together are one of the canon’s most human exchanges.

      Two smaller things for the rebuilding. Me appears twice in eleven words, genitive the first time and dative the second, with nothing in the form to tell them apart; and ca … ca joins two past participles, the simplest kind of samuccaya.

    2. “Na sūpaṁ vā byañjanaṁ vā odanena paṭicchādessāmi bhiyyokamyataṁ upādāyā”ti sikkhā karaṇīyā. (Bu Sk 36 — Vin iv 192)

      Three of the four jobs are here, and the fourth is absent because a rule has nothing to join. Na is paṭisedha, denying; the pair vā … vā is vikappana, separating, in the arrangement ‘A vā B vā’ this chapter gives for ca; and ti is the quotative, closing what the monk is to say to himself. There is no samuccaya particle here and no filler, since a training rule has no line of verse to fill out and nothing to add.

      The ā of upādāyā is the quotative at work: upādāya + iti lengthens the final vowel and swallows the i, giving upādāyāti, which editors print with the mark inside the join. There is no form upādāyā, and to take the long vowel for part of the absolutive is to look for a stem that does not exist. The same junction makes every sekhiya rule end in a lengthened vowel, since all seventy-five close a first-person future with ti.

      Upādāya is itself an absolutive serving as a postposition. It is not one of the six this chapter names but belongs to that class, and it governs the accusative bhiyyokamyataṁ, as the rule for the class says. Item 35 sets the two chapters’ accounts of that class against each other.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. Samuccaya, joining: ca, pi, atha. Vikappana, separating: vā, udāhu. Paṭisedha, denying and forbidding: na, no, mā, alaṁ. Pūraṇattha, filling: kho, hi, vata, sudaṁ, khalu. The scheme is the Saddanīti’s, and the notable thing about it is that the fourth category exists at all: a Western list of parts of speech has nowhere to put a word whose job is to fill out a line.

    2. Both Oberlies and Geiger are historical-comparative grammars, organised by inflection. Their method tracks sound changes through sets of endings, and an indeclinable has no endings for it to work on. So a word class with no morphology has no place in a description built entirely out of morphology. The gap belongs to the genre and is no oversight, and it meant that every form in this chapter had to be checked against the corpus instead of a grammarian.

    3. It has kiriyāvisesana, ‘specification of the action’, with the subclass bhāvanapuṁsaka, the neuter accusative used adverbially. The expectation changes: in Pāli an adverb is a use of a word, not a kind of word, so there is no ending to look for, and the same string may be an adverb in one sentence and an ordinary case form in the next. You identify it by asking what it modifies. Item 1 is the shortest demonstration.

    4. Mā governs the aorist. It has to be known because it inverts the reading of a form the reader has already learnt as a past tense: mā avaca means ‘do not say’, not ‘you did not say’. Item 31 shows that even this is too narrow.

    5. Derivative adverbs (formed by suffix), case-form adverbs, and pure adverbs. A word you cannot analyse belongs to the third group by definition. Duroiselle’s “pure adverbs” are those which are neither derived nor case forms, and the inability to break them down is the criterion, not a failure of analysis.

    Identify the formation.

    The three traps are 14, 18 and 19.

    1. tato — adverb, ‘from there, thence, therefore’. Built on the distal pronominal stem ta- with the ablative-sense suffix -to. A derivative adverb; in most of its uses it has the force of a conjunction, drawing a consequence, as it does in item 36.

    2. sudaṁ — a filler (padapūraṇa), emphatic, with no reliable English rendering. Not analysable as anything else, so a pure adverb in Duroiselle’s terms. DPD records a second sudaṁ which is the sandhi of su + idaṁ, and the two are told apart only by what the sentence needs.

    3. Trap. kira — a reportive particle, ‘it is said, apparently’, marking the sentence as not the speaker’s own assertion. The trap is that kira is also an inflected form of the verb kirati ‘scatters’. Nothing in the shape distinguishes them. The second trap inside the first: some grammars print kila beside it as though it were a Pāli variant, but kila is the Sanskrit form and occurs nowhere in this corpus.

    4. cirassaṁ — ‘at long last’, a case-form adverb. Historically the genitive cirassa with a neuter accusative ending built on top of it, so it looks like two case endings at once. The plain genitive cirassa is also used adverbially but is rare; cirassaṁ is the form to expect.

    5. tikkhattuṁ — ‘three times’, a derivative adverb: the numeral stem ti- with the suffix -khattuṁ ‘times’. The suffix is fully productive: sattakkhattuṁ ‘seven times’ is built the same way. Set 06 met it as a numeral; item 34 sets it beside an adverb of the other kind.

    6. iṅgha — an interjection, ‘come on!, please!’. Analysable, and the item is here for that reason: DPD parses it as a second-person singular imperative of iñjati ‘stirs, moves’, frozen. It has stopped conjugating and become an indeclinable.

    7. Trap. bho — an address particle, ‘sir’, and not an indeclinable at all. It is the vocative of bhavant, a declined form used as an interjection. To list it among the nipātā is to classify it by behaviour instead of morphology, and for this class that is the wrong way round.

    8. Trap. ekopi — not a word to be looked up but two words written as one: eko ‘one’ with the enclitic pi ‘even’ fused onto it. The trap is that no dictionary carries it. This is the ordinary spelling of an enclitic in these editions, and the item tests whether you recognise the join.

    9. nanu — an interrogative particle expecting the answer yes. Analysable as na + nu, a negative folded into a question, as English ‘don’t you …?’ folds one in. A pure adverb by Duroiselle’s classification, a filler by nobody’s. Item 27 is it in a sentence.

    10. divā — ‘by day’, a frozen instrumental, so a case-form adverb. Its companion ratto ‘by night’ is a frozen locative, and neither is any longer functioning as a case. Item 1 is the line they stand in.

    Build the form.

    1. sabbattha.

    2. kadā.

    3. sattadhā. (Built as pañcadhā and bahudhā are; the doubling in sattakkhattuṁ belongs to the -khattuṁ suffix, not to this one.)

    4. The form the rule produces is mā gacchi, and the item is really about the corpus check. Mā gacchi occurs once in a commentary and once in a grammarian’s own conjugation table, which prints the whole run: mā agami, mā gamā, mā gamī, mā gametu. Tvaṁ mā gaccho, mā gacchi, mā gacchāhi. The canon has mā agamāsi ‘do not go’, in the Vinaya, with the augment kept.

      Two things follow. First, the rule is right: mā takes the aorist, and mā gaccha with an imperative, which is what an English speaker’s instinct produces, is not the construction. Second, a correctly built form may still be unattested, and you cannot see the difference until you look. The book runs this check on every form it teaches.

    5. ito. The proximal stem with -to, parallel to tato at item 12.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Read the sentence.

    1. nanu surely (interrog.) · tvaṁ you (nom. sg.) · sālavatikaṁ Sālavatikā (f. acc. sg.) · ajjhāvasasi you dwell in · ti (quotation)

      “Surely you live in Sālavatikā?”

      The questioner expects yes, and nanu shows it. The discourse bears it out: Lohicca answers Evaṁ, bho Gotama, ‘Just so, master Gotama’. Had the particle been nu kho, as in item 3, the question would have been open. The two particles mark the difference between asking and checking.

    2. The word is kudāssu, and the particle inside it is ssu, an emphatic that never stands as a free word in the root texts. Printed apart the two would be kudā ssu, and kudā ‘when’ does not stand alone in the root texts either, so a reader will meet either half only in the compound. The whole means ‘when, oh when’.

      kudāwhenssu(emphatic)nāmaindeedimassaof thisn gen sgdukkhassaof sufferingnissaraṇaṁescapen nom sgpaññāyissatiwill be discerned3 sg fut passjarāmaraṇassaof ageing-and-deathti(quotation)

      “When, oh when will an escape be discerned from this suffering, from ageing and death?”

      This is the Bodhisatta before the awakening, and the force of the question lies in the emphatic. Item 19’s ekopi is the same join in a less consequential place.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. Mā evaṁ, āvuso sāti, avaca, mā bhagavantaṁ abbhācikkhi. (MN 38 — M i 256)

      Watch the mood: both verbs are aorists, avaca and abbhācikkhi, and the prohibition is carried by mā alone. An imperative here would be wrong.

    2. Handa dāni, bhikkhave, āmantayāmi vo. (DN 16 — D ii 155)

      Handa the interjection, dāni the adverb of time, and vo the enclitic second-person plural pronoun. Only āmantayāmi and the vocative bhikkhave inflect. Set 08 took this sentence for the two forms of its eighth-class stems; here, once the verbs are accounted for, almost the whole sentence is left.

    Read at length.

    1. “Come, Kālāmas. Do not go by oral tradition, by lineage of teaching, by hearsay, by a collection of scriptures, by logical reasoning, by inferential reasoning, by reasoned consideration, by acceptance of a view after pondering it, by the appearance of competence, or because ‘the ascetic is our teacher’.”

      • There are ten mās and no aorist at all. Mā here governs an instrumental, and the verb it would have governed is left to be supplied: ‘do not [go / be led] by …’. The chapter taught mā with the aorist because a reader meets that construction most; this passage shows that the particle forbids, and that what it attaches to varies.
      • Anussavena, paramparāya, ākāraparivitakkena are all instrumental, and the case expresses means: these are the grounds by which one might be persuaded. The sentence rests on the instrumental of means, and mā refuses every instance of it.
      • Takkahetu and nayahetu are not instrumentals at all. They are compounds ending in hetu ‘cause’, which this chapter listed among the nouns used prepositionally: ‘by reason of logic’, ‘by reason of inference’. DPD parses takkahetu as takka + hetu and glosses it literally ‘because of logic’. So the series is less uniform than it first looks. Check before assuming a pattern holds across a long list.
      • Mā samaṇo no garū’ti: “nor because ‘the ascetic is our teacher’.” The iti is causal, as in item 2: it closes a quoted reason and hands it to mā. Read as a plain quotation mark, it leaves the clause with no verb to attach to.
      • The repetition makes each item a separate refusal. Written once with a list, the sentence would say ‘do not go by [this whole set]’, leaving open the possibility that some smaller combination would do. Ten mās close each door individually. The repeated form is also easier to recite and to remember, and in an oral literature that counts for as much.

    Reaching back.

    1. The rule is that the aorist after mā is prohibitive, not past. Remove the particle from item 29 and evaṁ, āvuso sāti, avaca becomes ‘you spoke thus, friend Sāti’, a report of what happened and the opposite of what the monks are saying. Here the particle in front of the form decides its tense, and that sums up this whole chapter fairly.

    2. Chapter 5 describes what tasmā and tato are: oblique case forms of the pronoun ta(d), sitting in its paradigm, ablative singular and -to form respectively. This chapter describes what they have become: words that no longer refer to anything and function as ‘therefore’ and ‘thence’. Both are true because the freezing is a historical process, not a reclassification: the form kept its shape and lost its function. In practice tasmā in a real sentence usually needs no antecedent, and looking for one is the commonest way of misreading it. Item 2 is the exception, for there tasmā does answer to something, and the something is a particle.

    3. This chapter adds the other five oblique cases, and the traditional name for the whole phenomenon (kiriyāvisesana). The sentence showing both at once is DN 16 — D ii 163, tikkhattuṁ citakaṁ padakkhiṇaṁ katvā, where tikkhattuṁ ‘three times’ is an adverb built with a suffix and padakkhiṇaṁ ‘keeping to the right’ is an ordinary accusative used adverbially.

    4. Chapter 11 teaches what they are and how they are formed: absolutives of pacceti, nissayati, uddisati, which have stopped naming a prior action and become postpositions. This chapter teaches what case they govern, the accusative, and places them among the four things that took over the vanished preposition’s work. Neither chapter is complete without the other, so each cross-refers to the other instead of repeating it. Item 6’s upādāya is a fourth member of the same class, met in this chapter and not in Chapter 11.

    Say it aloud.

    1. “Mind precedes all things; mind is their chief, they are mind-made. If with a corrupted mind one speaks or acts, from that suffering follows, as the wheel follows the ox’s foot.”

      The four indeclinables are the verse’s whole logical apparatus. Ce ‘if’ marks the protasis, and it is enclitic, so it stands second in its line, not first as English ‘if’ does. Vā … vā offers two ways the condition may be satisfied, speech or act, without preferring either. Tato ‘from that’ draws the consequence and makes the second half an apodosis instead of a new sentence. And va, the reduced iva, makes the simile, as yathā and evaṁ do in item 4, here with one syllable.

      Strip them out and six lines remain that are almost entirely nouns and two verbs, with no relation stated between any of them. That is a fair measure of how much of a Pāli sentence depends on the words that do not inflect.
