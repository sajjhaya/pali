    Answers: Participles

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Idha, mahāli, bhikkhu tiṇṇaṁ saṁyojanānaṁ parikkhayā sotāpanno hoti avinipātadhammo niyato sambodhiparāyaṇo. (DN 6 — D i 156)

      Sotāpanno is the word, a past participle used as a noun. DPD resolves it sota + āpanna, and āpanna is the past participle of āpajjati, from √pad: ‘entered into, fallen into’. So “stream-enterer” is a description, one who has entered the stream, more than a title. The chapter’s “as nouns” section describes this pattern, and Buddho is its other famous instance.

      Niyato is the second real participle, ‘bound, fixed’, from niyameti. The other two are not participles, though they are built out of participial material: avinipātadhammo is “having the property of not falling away” and sambodhiparāyaṇo “having awakening as destination”, both possessive compounds of Chapter 13’s kind. A sentence describing a spiritual attainment leans on this chapter’s whole apparatus, and three of its four long words are formed from verbs.

    2. Tiṭṭhaṁ caraṁ nisinno va, Sayāno yāvatāssa vitamiddho; (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

      Three endings across four words. Tiṭṭhaṁ and caraṁ are present participles in -aṁ. DPD parses both as the masculine nominative singular of tiṭṭhanta and caranta, tiṭṭha + nta + aṁ and cara + nta + aṁ, so the -nt stem is there and the nominative has simply worn down to a niggahīta. Nisinno is a past participle of nisīdati, read actively, which is the chapter’s point about verbs of posture. And sayāno is the present participle in -āna, DPD’s saya + āna from √sī.

      Sayāno is the answer to item 11 as well. It is the older ending, kept by a handful of verbs, with no -m- in it at all, and it stands here beside two participles of the commoner type so that the difference can be heard as well as described. Karoti’s kurumāno/kubbamāno is the other place a reader will meet it.

      So the line puts a past participle between two present participles and reads all three the same way, actively, as descriptions of what the man is doing. None of them is passive, or could be, because standing, walking, sitting and lying down have no object for a passive to act on.

      Yāvatāssa is yāvatā + assa. DPD gives yāvatā as an indeclinable, the ablative singular of yāva used adverbially, ‘as long as, to the extent that’. And vitamiddho is vīta + middha, ‘with drowsiness gone’. Neither is a participle; both are here because the line does not divide where a reader expects.

    3. The four pairs, of which two are printed above. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)

      The difference is that one has happened and the other has not yet. Pariññeyyaṁ says the truth of suffering is something a person is to fully understand; pariññātaṁ says it has been fully understood. Same verb, same neuter singular, agreeing with the same ariyasaccaṁ, and the whole distance between a task and its completion lies in the suffix.

      Formally: pariññeyya is the gerundive, which DPD parses pari + √ñā + eyya, ptp of parijānāti; pariññāta is the past participle of the same verb. Pahātabbaṁ and pahīnaṁ are the same pair on pajahati, and pahātabbaṁ is item 21’s build target. The third and fourth truths run sacchikātabbaṁ against sacchikataṁ and bhāvetabbaṁ against bhāvitaṁ; and sacchikātabba, DPD says, is sacchi + kātabba, which is item 14’s word with a noun welded to the front of it.

      Two things come out of this beyond the parsing. The first is that the sermon’s architecture is grammatical. The turnings are twelve, not four, because each truth is stated, then given a task, then reported done, and the second and third of those are one participle each. The chapter calls the gerundive’s name misleading because it is about obligation and not futurity; here is the whole doctrinal weight of that distinction in one passage.

      The second is that bhāvetabbaṁ and bhāvitaṁ are built on the causative set 09 spent an item on. Bhāveti is ‘causes to become’, so what the fourth truth says is that the path is to be brought into being and then that it has been. The causative has not stopped meaning what it means; it has only stopped announcing it.

    4. Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 57)

      Paṇihito is a past participle of paṇidahati ‘directs, disposes’, DPD’s pa + ni + √dhā + ita, literally ‘placed down forward’. The verb is transitive and the participle is accordingly passive: the body is what has been positioned.

      A generalising sentence of this kind needs a participle, and the sentence before it shows why. At 6.1 the sutta lists four postures with a finite verb apiece (going, standing, sitting, lying down), and then this sentence replaces the whole list with yathā yathā … paṇihito hoti, “however it is positioned”. A finite verb has to name a particular action; a participle names a state, and a state can be left indefinite. So the summary of a list of verbs is a participle and not a fifth verb.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. ’Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṁ brahmacariyaṁ, kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ, nāparaṁ itthattāyā’ti pajānāti. (SN 22.79 — S iii 90)

      Karaṇīyaṁ alone is not a past participle. It is the gerundive of karoti, ‘what is to be done’, and it stands immediately beside kataṁ, the past participle of the very same verb. So kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ is item 3’s construction in miniature: the task and its completion, one verb, two participles, two words. Every one of the four pairs in the first sermon is built the same way, and a reader meets the construction most often in this formula.

      The other three are past participles: khīṇā from khīyati (DPD: √khī + na), vusitaṁ from vivasati (vi + √vas > us + ita), kataṁ from karoti. Item 28 asks what voice each carries.

      There is no finite verb in the declaration at all until pajānāti arrives to report it. Four participles carry what may be the most important sentence in the canon, and the reporting verb is outside the quotation. That is the chapter’s closing argument, and it is best met by having said the sentence forty times.

    2. Adinnaṁ nāma yaṁ adinnaṁ anissaṭṭhaṁ apariccattaṁ rakkhitaṁ gopitaṁ mamāyitaṁ parapariggahitaṁ. Etaṁ adinnaṁ nāma. (Bu Pj 2)

      All seven are past participles and all seven stand in the neuter nominative singular, agreeing with yaṁ and through it with adinnaṁ, the word being defined. They are from dadāti ‘gives’, nissajjati ‘lets go’, pariccajati ‘relinquishes’, rakkhati ‘guards’, gopeti ‘protects’, mamāyati ‘treats as one’s own’, and pariggaṇhāti ‘takes possession of’ with para- ‘another’ in front.

      The first three carry the negative prefix and the last four do not, and the list is built on that division. The definition says first what has not happened to the object (it has not been given, let go of, or relinquished) and then what has: someone is guarding it and treats it as theirs. Both halves have to hold, so the order is not arbitrary, and the list is hard to rebuild. An- appears before the vowel of anissaṭṭhaṁ and a- before the consonants of adinnaṁ and apariccattaṁ, which is the rule this chapter gives for the negated participle.

      The neuter needs a moment. Nothing in the sentence names a thing that is neuter; the gender comes from adinnaṁ itself, a past participle used as a noun, ‘the not-given’, and everything downstream agrees with that. A participle can be the head as well as the modifier, and when it is, it fixes the agreement for whatever follows. Item 1’s sotāpanno is the same move in the masculine.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. A participle carries tense from the verb and gender, case and number agreement from the adjective. It is a verb form that behaves grammatically like an adjective.

    2. A transitive verb’s past participle is passive; an intransitive verb’s is active. Desita ‘taught’ describes what was done to something; gata ‘gone’ describes what the subject itself did. Item 28 tests the rule and finds where it needs sharpening.

    3. The past active participle (-tavant/-āvin, as in vusitavā, bhuttāvī) and the bare--a gerundive (dukkara, sulabha). The first is built by adding -vant- or -in- to an ordinary past participle; the second is a direct, unproductive survival of the Sanskrit sukara-/duṣkara- pattern, with no -tabba or -(a)nīya affix at all.

    4. Because it names a shape most sources also call the “future” participle even though it is built on the base, not the future stem, and means obligation (‘to be done’), not futurity. A true active participle on the future stem exists (Oberlies’s marissaṁ ‘about to die’) and is not what the common label refers to. Item 3 shows the distinction carrying doctrine.

    5. Atthi. Sayāno ‘lying down’ and santo ‘being’ both belong to this pattern. Sayāno is saya-, the present stem of sayati (√sī), + -āna, and atthi’s own present participle is one of the handful of verbs that keeps the older ending. Karoti keeps it too, in kurumāno/kubbamāno. Item 2 has sayāno in a line.

    Identify the formation.

    1. gato — past participle, active voice, of gacchati ‘goes’. Not a trap by itself, but it is the chapter’s central example: an intransitive verb’s participle read actively, ‘having gone’, not ‘having been gone’.

    2. desito — past participle, passive voice, of deseti ‘teaches’. Deseti is transitive, so the ordinary rule applies without complication.

    3. kātabbaṁ — gerundive, -tabba type, of karoti ‘does’. ‘To be done.’ Item 3’s sacchikātabbaṁ is this word with sacchi welded to the front.

    4. vusitavā — past active participle, -vant type, of vivasati (via its past participle vusita ‘lived’, vi + √vas, as item 5 gives it). ‘One who has lived (the spiritual life).’ Trap: this looks like it might be a present participle in -vant/-mant on first sight, because the possessive-adjective paradigm shares that suffix — but it is built on a past participle, not on a present stem, and its sense is unambiguously past and active.

    5. karonto — present participle, active voice, of karoti ‘does’. Ordinary -nt type on the irregular present stem karo-.

    6. dukkaraṁ — gerundive, bare--a type, of karoti, with the prefix du- ‘hard, ill’ in the role a suffix fills everywhere else. ‘Hard to do.’

    7. asantaṁ — present participle, negated, of atthi ‘is, exists’. Trap: it looks like an ordinary negative adjective (compare asanta ‘bad, wicked’, a separate lexical item built the same way) and is, grammatically, the negation of the present participle santaṁ ‘being’. The two readings coexist in the dictionary, and only the sentence decides which is meant.

    8. sotāpanno — past participle, substantivised, of sotaṁ āpajjati ‘enters the stream’. A compound past participle used as a noun, the pattern the chapter’s “as nouns” section describes for Buddho. Item 1 has it in its own sentence and item 28 asks what voice it carries.

    Build the form.

    1. dinnaṁ — -na type past participle of dadāti, the root dā- taking -na directly. 323 root-text occurrences. The bare stem dinna occurs as a free word only eleven times and inside compounds nearly two thousand, as a citation form usually does beside its word.

    2. pahātabbaṁ — pajahati’s stem pajaha- loses its reduplication and prefix down to pahā- before the -tabba is added; the chapter’s example. 564 root-text occurrences, 499 of them the Abhidhamma’s dassanena pahātabbā formula; the bare stem pahātabba has ten. The canon’s most consequential use of it is in item 3.

    3. vijitāvī — the past participle vijita ‘conquered’ (from vijayati/vijināti, root √ji) plus -āvī (the -in type past active participle). Forty-three root-text occurrences, most of them the standard epithet of a righteous wheel-turning monarch, cāturanto vijitāvī ‘conqueror of the four quarters’.

    4. jānanto — ordinary -nt type on the present stem jānā-, with the stem vowel shortened before the ending as it is throughout the -a declension. Sixty-nine root-text occurrences.

    5. vedayamāno — vedeti’s alternative stem vedaya- (the chapter’s “vedayamāno” table entry) plus -māna. Fifty-five root-text occurrences.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. Gahakāraka diṭṭhosi. Diṭṭho + asi fused, as item 31 prints it. The verse opens with this line.

    2. Pāṇo na hantabbo. The chapter’s DN 17 quotation gives this precept’s neighbours; DPD’s own citation for hantabbo happens to be a different sutta’s identical line (the Mahāsudassanasutta), which is further evidence that the sentence is fixed, formulaic Pāli and not a paraphrase.

    Read at length.

    1. “‘Suppavāsā, when a female noble disciple gives food, she gives the recipients four things. What four? She gives life, beauty, happiness and strength. Having given life, she becomes a partaker of life, whether divine or human. Having given beauty, she becomes a partaker of beauty, whether divine or human. Having given happiness, she becomes a partaker of happiness, whether divine or human. Having given strength, she becomes a partaker of strength, whether divine or human. Suppavāsā, when a female noble disciple gives food, she gives the recipients these four things.’”

      • Dentī is a present participle, feminine, of deti ‘gives’, and its feminine ending (-antī, the strong stem the chapter’s grammar section names) shows before a single verb is read that the passage concerns a woman: Suppavāsā the lay disciple, addressed by name in the vocative in the same clause. The participle’s agreement shows what a finite verb’s ending could not have shown.
      • Datvā is an absolutive, ‘having given’, and not a participle at all, though it looks close enough to one that a reader might guess. Chapter 11 gives the absolutive a chapter of its own; here it is enough to notice that Pāli reaches for the absolutive, not a participle, when one action is finished before the next one starts.
      • Bhāginī ‘sharer, partaker’ looks like a past active participle in -in and is not. DPD roots it in bhāga ‘portion, share’ plus the ordinary possessive-adjective suffix -in, the device the chapter’s bhuttāvī/vijitāvī class uses, but built on a noun instead of a verb’s past participle. The resemblance is real, since both classes decline the same way, on the ī/in-stems of Appendix A.1. It is the kind of look-alike that Chapter 9’s “recognising a derived stem” taught you to suspect, transposed here to participles.
      • No gerundive appears because the passage reports what happens, not what ought to. A gerundive would turn “she gives four things” into “four things are to be given”, a rule instead of an observation, and this passage is built as a chain of consequence (datvā … hoti, ‘having given, she becomes’), which needs a narrative sequence, not an obligation. Item 3 is the opposite case: there the obligation is the subject, and every second turning is a gerundive.

    Test the voice rule.

    1. Five of the six sort the way the rule predicts, and the sixth makes the rule need stating more carefully.

      Passive, from transitive verbs: pariññātaṁ (from parijānāti, ‘has been fully understood’), pahīnaṁ (from pajahati, ‘has been abandoned’), kataṁ (from karoti, ‘has been done’). Each of these has a patient (the truth that is understood, the craving that is abandoned, the work that is done), and the participle describes the patient, not the doer.

      Active, from an intransitive verb: khīṇā (from khīyati, ‘has run out’). English says “birth is exhausted” and the passive is English’s, not Pāli’s: nothing exhausted birth, birth ran out. DPD’s gloss is ‘consumed, spent, finished’, and the verb takes no object at all.

      Sotāpanno looks like a counter-example and is not. Āpajjati takes an accusative (sotaṁ āpajjati, āpattiṁ āpajjati), so on a test of “does it take an object” it is transitive, and its participle is nevertheless active: a stream-enterer is one who has entered, not one who has been entered. The rule as the chapter states it survives, because the chapter states it in terms of the patient: “the thing described is the patient rather than the agent”. The stream is a destination, not a patient, and a verb of motion takes an accusative of goal without there being anything for a passive to act on. Gacchati is the same: gāmaṁ gacchati takes an accusative and gives the active gata.

      So the sharper form of the rule is to ask whether the verb has a patient, not whether it can take an accusative. DPD’s gloss for āpanna is ‘fallen into, entered into’, from √pad, the dictionary entry of a verb of motion.

      Vusitaṁ is hard, and it is better to say so than to force it. Vivasati ‘lives’ is not obviously transitive, and brahmacariyaṁ is a cognate accusative: the life that is lived, not a thing acted on. The participle agrees with brahmacariyaṁ in the neuter, as a passive participle would, and DPD glosses vusita as ‘fulfilled, completed, finished’. English has no choice but “has been lived”. Whether that makes the Pāli passive is a question the six-word formula cannot settle, and to leave it open is to read it correctly.

    Reaching back.

    1. It means ‘having something made, causing to be done’: the causative middle participle of karoti, built from kāreti + -māna the same way bhāveti + -anta gives bhāvento. Chapter 9 explains this. It taught kāreti as the causative and gave the rule that a causative’s stem, not its ending, carries the extra sense. So kārayamāna is the wrong word for ‘doing’ because it already says ‘causing to be done’, and the Vinaya uses it of a monk having a hut or a robe made for him.

      As for the lookup: the root texts print this participle only in oblique cases, and overwhelmingly in the instrumental, kārayamānena, the agent by whom the thing is had made. There is no nominative kārayamāno in them at all. A search made in the citation form a dictionary files a word under therefore returns nothing, and returns it for a reason that has nothing to do with whether the word exists. Chapter 10’s footnote records that this chapter’s first draft made that mistake.

    2. It inflects by the (m/v)ant-stems of Appendix A.1, the paradigm every other -nt present participle in this chapter uses, bhagavant- and gacchant- included. It is not the surprise it looks like, because atthi’s irregularity in Chapter 7 (asmi, amhi, amha) is confined to its finite conjugation, built on endings peculiar to a handful of ancient athematic verbs; its participle is derived the ordinary way, on the ordinary -nt stem sant-, and ordinary participles inflect by one paradigm regardless of how irregular the verb underneath them is.

    Say it aloud.

    1. “House-builder, you are seen! You will not build a house again. All your rafters are broken, the roof-peak demolished; the mind has gone to the unconditioned, it has reached the ending of craving.”

      Both words share the root saṅkhāra/saṅkharoti ‘put together, construct, condition’, the root that also names the fourth aggregate and the whole doctrine of conditioned existence. Visaṅkhataṁ ‘demolished’ describes an ordinary roof-peak, built and now unbuilt. Visaṅkhāragataṁ ‘gone to the unconditioned’ describes the mind at the moment of awakening, in the same vocabulary. The verse uses the same word twice on purpose, so that the literal demolition of a house and the doctrinal “unconditioning” of a mind read as one event, the house-builder’s trade supplying the vocabulary in which awakening itself is described. It is a striking piece of construction, and the tradition has been reciting it for two and a half thousand years.

      Set 08 took this verse for its tenses: kāhasi, the contracted future, and ajjhagā, the aorist with its augment showing. Coming back to it for its participles is the same discipline as the sajjhāya stages themselves: a line that has been said often enough has more in it than the thing you came for.
