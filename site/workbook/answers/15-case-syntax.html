    Answers · Chapter 15

    Answers: Case Syntax

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Atha kho bhagavā kismiñcideva pakaraṇe bhikkhusaṅghaṁ paṇāmetvā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya kapilavatthuṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi. (SN 22.80 — S iii 91)

      Four accusatives with three functions: the chapter’s opening demonstration. Bhikkhusaṅghaṁ and pattacīvaraṁ are objects. The Saṅgha is what was dismissed, the bowl and robe are what was taken. Kapilavatthuṁ is the goal of motion: nothing is done to Kapilavatthu, and the accusative marks it as the place the going is towards. Pubbaṇhasamayaṁ is adverbial, of time when.

      The fourth is not a participant in the action at all. It has the ending of an object and is not one; it names when, and no kāraka role is involved. Items 15 and 16 ask for those two cold, and item 16 is one of the five traps.

    2. Puttā matthi dhanaṁ matthi, iti bālo vihaññati; (Dhp 62 — Dhp p. 9)

      The literal shape is ‘of me there are sons, of me there is wealth’, and the possessor is carried by the genitive, or the dative, which is the same form. Pāli has no verb ‘to have’ and this is the gap the genitive fills: a form of atthi or hoti with the possessor in the genitive.

      At the join, me has lost its vowel before the a- of atthi and what is left of it is a single consonant welded to the front of the verb: me + atthi → matthi. So the possessor is the m-, not something at the front of the word. Scan for me and you will not see it, and a corpus search for me atthi will not find this line either. This is the mirror of the fifth caution: there a word’s initial vowel is eaten by the word before it, and here a word is reduced to its first consonant by the word after. Either way the search term differs from what stands on the page.

      Whether the me is genitive or dative cannot be settled, and need not be: the two are identical in form for this pronoun and the sense supports both. That is the merger described at the head of the chapter, met in a real sentence, and item 37 shows how the tradition handles it.

    3. Ajjeva rattiyā pacchime yāme tathāgatassa parinibbānaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 139)

      The genitive is rattiyā, in the ordinary partitive sense: the watch is a part of the night. It sits inside a locative phrase and belongs to the noun, not to the verb, as a genitive always does.

      Rattiyā settles nothing on its own because a feminine oblique in -iyā is shared out among the cases, as -āya is on an ā-stem. The proof is in this chapter and not outside it. At SN 1.1 — S i 1 the same chapter prints the same word and glosses it a locative absolute, “when the night was far gone”; here it glosses it a genitive. One word, one chapter, two cases, and the form is identical in both.

      Here the company decides it. Pacchime yāme is already the locative of time when and the sentence does not want a second one; and a watch is a part of a night, which is the relation a genitive states. The ending narrows and the sentence decides: the chapter says so in the abstract, and this line shows it.

    4. Ayaṁ, deva, pūraṇo kassapo saṅghī ceva gaṇī ca gaṇācariyo ca ñāto yasassī titthakaro sādhusammato bahujanassa rattaññū cirapabbajito addhagato vayoanuppatto. (DN 2 — D i 47)

      A string of nominatives all in apposition to one another, and a single genitive where an instrumental would ordinarily stand.

      The odd word is bahujanassa, where the instrumental would ordinarily stand. It is the agent of the past participle sādhusammato, ‘held in high regard’, and the agent of a passive goes into the instrumental, which is item 12’s rule. Here it is genitive instead. That is the merger working the other way round: where two cases have run together in function, each turns up in the other’s territory, and an agent in the genitive is one of the commonest places to meet it.

      Apposition alone holds the eleven together. There is no verb in the sentence for them to be governed by; they agree with pūraṇo kassapo in case, number and gender, and that agreement is the whole of the syntax. Pāli says “is” with a list of nominatives.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Musāvādā veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ samādiyāmi. (Kp 2)

      The ablative is governed by the veramaṇī buried inside the compound, not by samādiyāmi. What is undertaken is a training rule; what the false speech is separated from is the abstaining. Veramaṇī ‘abstaining’ takes an ablative of the thing abstained from, and the ablative here is the sense of separation, release and abstention in which the precepts are phrased throughout.

      That is also how the phrase survives being compounded. The word governing the ablative has been swallowed into veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ and has taken its ablative with it: the ablative stands outside the compound and points at a member inside it. An ablative governed by the verb could not have done that, because samādiyāmi does not take one.

      Thānuttamo lists ‘abstention from’ among the ablative’s senses and names ārati ‘abstinence’ as another noun that governs it. Wijesekera would add the caution that adnominal ablatives in the Nikāyas are few and mostly presuppose a verb that has dropped out, which is a fair description of what has happened here.

    2. Etaṁ nibbidāya virāgāya nirodhāya upasamāya abhiññāya sambodhāya nibbānāya saṁvattati. (DN 9 — D i 189)

      Five of the seven, not all seven. Virāga, nirodha, upasama and sambodha are masculine a-stems and nibbāna is neuter, so on those five the -āya can only be the dative ending, the only place in the language where a dative is visible as a dative (item 25’s rule). Nibbidā and abhiññā are feminine ā-stems, and on them -āya is the ending, instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive and locative at once. Two of the seven are as ambiguous as vedanāya in item 7.

      They are readable for the reason any ending in this chapter is. Saṁvattati governs a dative, and the two ambiguous words stand in a series of seven whose other five can only be datives. The list carries them. Abhiññāya has a further claim on a reader’s attention, because it is also the absolutive of abhijānāti, ‘having directly known’, a second word of the same spelling, and only the series rules it out.

      Do not take the seven to be datives merely because each looks like an a-stem. Sambodhāya is from sambodha, masculine (DPD’s saṁ + √budh > bodh + *a, from sambujjhati), and not from the feminine sambodhi, which is an i-stem and cannot give -āya at all. The trap is the chapter’s subject: a run of seven identical endings invites a reader to stop looking.

    3. Evaṁ passaṁ, bhikkhave, sutavā ariyasāvako rūpasmimpi nibbindati, vedanāyapi nibbindati, saññāyapi nibbindati, saṅkhāresupi nibbindati, viññāṇasmimpi nibbindati. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)

      Three of the five endings can only be locative; two could be five cases each. The three carry the two.

      So the chapter calls the sentence the whole chapter in one line. Rūpasmiṁ, saṅkhāresu and viññāṇasmiṁ are unmistakable. Vedanāya and saññāya are feminine ā-stems and say nothing on their own. They are locative because the other three are, and because nibbindati takes a locative. The construction decides, and the ending only narrows.

    4. Tiṭṭhatu, bhante, bhagavā kappaṁ, tiṭṭhatu sugato kappaṁ bahujanahitāya bahujanasukhāya lokānukampāya atthāya hitāya sukhāya devamanussānaṁ. (DN 16 — D ii 103)

      Six datives of purpose, an accusative of extent, and a genitive at the end governing the last three of them.

      The accusative is kappaṁ, and it is extent of time, not an object: nothing is done to the aeon, and the sentence asks the Blessed One to remain for one. It is the same adverbial use as pubbaṇhasamayaṁ in item 1, measuring time instead of locating it.

      Devamanussānaṁ reaches back over the last three datives, atthāya hitāya sukhāya, the benefit and welfare and happiness of gods and humans. It stops there because the first three already carry their own possessor: bahujana- is compounded into two of them and loka- into the third, so they have no room for a second one. A genitive reaches back until it meets a phrase that does not need it.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. Eight vibhatti, ‘divisions’: the sets of endings, numbered, not named: paṭhamā, dutiyā, and so on to sattamī, with the vocative counted as a variety of the first. Six kāraka, ‘factors of action’: the roles a participant can play in what the verb describes: kattā agent, kamma object, karaṇa means, sampadāna recipient, apādāna point of departure, okāsa place. The genitive and the vocative are the two that are left over, and the Saddanīti says so in one line: na chaṭṭhīvihitattho kārakaṁ, yathā mantanaṁ. The reason given is that neither has a connection to the action. A genitive relates one noun to another and reaches the verb only at second hand; a vocative has no action to be connected to yet, since being addressed makes a person available to be told to do something.

    2. Instrumental and ablative are identical throughout the plural (-ehi, -āhi, -ūhi) and in the -ā singular of the a-stems; they stay apart in the a-stem singular, -ena against -asmā and -amhā. Dative and genitive are identical everywhere, in both numbers, with one exception: the a-stem dative singular in -āya. The consequence is the chapter’s structural point. Where the forms merged, the functions bled into each other and Pāli now uses each of the pair in senses that belong to the other; where the forms stayed apart, the functions stayed comparatively clean. So a beginner finds the accusative and locative easy while the other four seem to shade into one another. They do, and the reason is morphological, not conceptual.

    3. First the verb, because most verbs take one case and not another: nibbindati takes a locative, tasati a genitive, saṁvattati a dative, viramati an ablative. Then the parallel: Pāli is a language of lists, and a form whose case is unclear is very often standing in a series with forms whose case is not. Where neither helps, say that the sentence is open.

    4. They come apart in the passive (and in the causative), which Pāli uses constantly. The agent then goes into the instrumental and the nominative is the thing acted on: svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo, where dhammo is nominative and bhagavatā is the agent. The tradition has a name for each half: abhihitakattā, the agent expressed by the verb’s own ending, and anabhihitakattā, the agent the ending does not express and which must therefore be named separately.

    5. It uses the genitive (or dative; the two are the same in form) with atthi or hoti, giving a sentence of the shape ‘of me there is X’. Puttā matthi dhanaṁ matthi (Dhp 62) is me + atthi twice: “of me there are sons, of me there is wealth”, which we render “I have sons, I have wealth.”

    Name the case and its job.

    1. Dative, of purpose. The -āya dative is the only true dative form left in the language, and purpose is overwhelmingly what it expresses.

    2. Accusative, goal of motion. Nothing is done to Kapilavatthu; the accusative marks it as the place the going is towards.

    3. Accusative, adverbial, of time when, and this is a trap. It has the ending of an object, but it is not a participant in the action at all. DPD in fact files pubbaṇhasamayaṁ twice, once as the noun pubbaṇhasamaya and once as an indeclinable adverb in its own right, “acc sg of pubbaṇhasamaya”, because the accusative has hardened into a word.

    4. Instrumental, of agent. The sentence is passive: the Dhamma is what was proclaimed, and the Blessed One is who proclaimed it.

    5. Instrumental, of agent again, this time plural, and governed by the gerundive veditabbo, not by a finite verb: it is to be known by the wise.

    6. Genitive, with a verb of fearing. English wants an object or a preposition here; Pāli puts the thing feared in the genitive. The parallel line, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno, does the same with a second verb of the same class.

    7. Locative, of contact, with a verb of seizing: the opasilesika type in the tradition’s fourfold division, contact between two separately established things. Where English says ‘by the hair’, Pāli puts the part taken hold of in the locative.

    8. Ablative singular, of cause, and this is a trap. Jātipaccayā looks like a feminine nominative or an a-stem nominative plural and is neither: it is the ablative singular of the masculine compound jātipaccaya, ‘the condition that is birth’. DPD, like the other examples in this set, files it as an indeclinable adverb which is an ablative singular. Getting it wrong turns the sentence about conditionality into a sentence about a list.

    9. Genitive plural, partitive: the whole out of which one is taken. Subhadda became one of the arahants, not the arahants’ something.

    10. Instrumental, and this is a trap, because English says “possible for me” and the reader reaches for a dative. The impersonals sakkā, labbhā, dujjānaṁ, sukaraṁ take an infinitive and put the person in the instrumental. The Buddha’s reply in the same passage does it in the second person: dujjānaṁ kho etaṁ … tayā.

    11. Genitive, objective, and this is a trap, on two counts. The ending -iyā on a feminine ā-stem could be any of five cases; and even once one has decided it is genitive, there are two genitives on the same noun, with opposite functions. Tathāgatassa is subjective, the one who sees; vesāliyā is objective, the thing seen. Nothing in the endings distinguishes them. What distinguishes them is that a city cannot look at a person.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Choose the case.

    1. Dative of purpose, ending -āya on an a-stem: bahujanahitāya bahujanasukhāya. In a feminine ā-stem you could not identify it with the same confidence, because there -āya is the ending, instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive and locative singular all at once. The masculine and neuter a-stems are the only place in the language where a dative is visible as a dative.

    2. The genitive. Tasati ‘trembles’ and bhāyati ‘fears’ are the two in Dhp 129; Perniola’s fuller list adds sarati and sumarati ‘remembers’, saddahati ‘believes’, pūrati ‘is filled with’, maccharāyati ‘is stingy about’, and the verb of envying, which occurs in the canon only in the plural, as pihayanti.

    3. The suffix -to, which is invariable and which no other case wears, so a word carrying it can only be ablative. Wijesekera declines to call it a proper case ending because it is not one even in Sanskrit, where -taḥ is likewise a suffix, not an inflection; in the Nikāyas it attaches to every kind of stem and is mostly adverbial in force. Three from this chapter: jātito, nāmato and gottato in the Mahāpadāna’s list of what the Buddha recollects (jātitopi anussarati, nāmatopi anussarati, gottatopi anussarati), where every one of them is printed with the enclitic pi welded on. The Dhammapada sequence adds piyato, pemato and kāmato.

    4. This is a trap, set by the source itself. There is no such verb as appamaññati. The canon has Māvamaññetha pāpassa (Dhp 121 — Dhp p. 18) and Māvamaññetha puññassa (Dhp 122 — Dhp p. 18), and the verb is avamaññati, which DPD gives as ava + mañña + ti, √man, ‘thinks lightly of’. The grammar’s form would have to be built on appa- ‘little’, which is not what the line says. What has happened at the join is that mā and avamaññetha have run together and the vowel has lengthened (mā + avamaññetha → māvamaññetha), so the verb is not visible at the front of the word, and a corpus search for avamaññ returns nothing because what stands there is āvamaññ. The form to search for is vamaññetha, stripped of its own initial vowel.

    5. Dative singular of the neuter abstract noun itthatta, which DPD gives as ittha ‘thus, here’ plus the abstract suffix -tta, Sanskrit itthatva: ‘the state of being thus’. A search returns almost nothing because the word almost never stands bare in the canon. It stands before the quotation marker ti, and the final vowel lengthens at the join, so what is printed is itthattāyāti, 114 times in the root texts against one for itthattāya.

    Read the sentence.

      • Seyyathāpi, bho gotama, puriso purisaṁ narakapapātaṁ patantaṁ kesesu gahetvā uddharitvā thale patiṭṭhapeyya. (DN 12 — D i 234)
      • seyyathāpi just as · bho gotama good Gotama (voc.) · puriso a man (m. nom. sg.) · purisaṁ a man (m. acc. sg.) · narakapapātaṁ into a pit (m. acc. sg.) · patantaṁ falling (m. acc. sg., pres. part.) · kesesu by the hair (m. loc. pl.) · gahetvā having seized · uddharitvā having pulled out · thale on firm ground (nt. loc. sg.) · patiṭṭhapeyya would set down
      • Just as, good Gotama, a man might seize by the hair another man falling into a pit, pull him out, and set him on firm ground.
      Both locatives are the opasilesika type of the chapter’s fourfold table, contact between two separately established things, the hand on the hair and the man on the ground. The two accusatives beside them differ too: purisaṁ is the object of gahetvā and narakapapātaṁ is the goal of patantaṁ, the four-accusative lesson of item 1 in a sentence with only two. The participle patantaṁ agrees with purisaṁ, and that shows whose falling it is.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṁ brahmacariyaṁ, kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ, nāparaṁ itthattāyā ti. The last clause is na + aparaṁ + itthattāya, and the whole weight of it is on the dative.

    2. Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno. The nouns are genitive, not accusative and not ablative.

    Read at length.

    1. On Ud 1.10. Diṭṭhe, sute, mute, viññāte are locative singulars of past participles used as nouns: ‘in the seen, in the heard, in the sensed, in the cognised’. Which of the tradition’s four kinds of locative they are is harder than it looks, because the fourfold division was built for physical supports: oil in sesame, a man on a mat, a dwelling near a city, fish in water. The seen is not a place, and this is the locative of reference, the nimitta-sattamī the grammarians name separately, which the fourfold division does not cover. The second inflected word in each clause, diṭṭhamattaṁ and its fellows, is a nominative singular neuter, the subject of bhavissati: literally “in the seen there will be merely-the-seen”. Bāhiya’s request at Ud 1.10 — Ud p. 7 is desetu me, bhante, bhagavā dhammaṁ: desetu imperative, bhante vocative, bhagavā nominative, dhammaṁ accusative, and me open between dative and genitive, the me the chapter on pronouns discusses at evaṁ me sutaṁ. The chapter would not force a choice: the two cases are identical in form for this pronoun and the sense supports both, ‘teach the Dhamma to me’ and ‘let there be a teaching of the Dhamma for me’. Finally, yattha and tattha are not case forms at all. They are the relative and demonstrative pronominal stems ya- and ta- with the adverb-forming suffix -ttha. The formation is in the chapter on indeclinables, and the correlative use of the pair is in the chapter on relative clauses.

    Reaching back.

    1. Because the fundamental sense of the instrumental is accompaniment, not the instrument: Brugmann’s Mit-Kasus, the with-case, and Wijesekera notes that some have preferred to call it the sociative for this reason. If ‘with’ is the case’s core sense, then ‘without’ is its negation and takes the same case; saddhiṁ and vinā are two ends of one relation.

    2. Bhikkhusaṅgho resolves as bhikkhūnaṁ saṅgho, a genitive relation: the assembly of the monks. Kāmataṇhā resolves as kāmesu taṇhā, a locative relation. The recovered cases differ because the case inside a tappurisa is decided by the second member, and saṅgha takes a genitive while taṇhā takes a locative. This chapter’s first rule, taken one step further, decides it: look at the governing word, here the compound’s second member, where the chapter’s wording is “look at the verb first”. English translation conceals this one, for ‘craving for sensual pleasures’ sounds like a dative, and Pāli words for desire and for views take their object in the locative.

    3. -āya in a feminine ā-stem singular is instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive and locative (with -āyaṁ as an alternative locative). What settles vedanāya in vedanāyapi nibbindati is both of the chapter’s tests at once: nibbindati governs a locative, and the word stands in a series of five whose other three members, rūpasmiṁ, saṅkhāresu, viññāṇasmiṁ, carry endings that can only be locative. The sentence decides, not the word.

    4. Buddhaghosa distinguishes three senses in which me is met (instrumental, dative, genitive) and then declines to choose for this passage: idha pana “mayā sutan”ti “mama sutan”ti ca atthadvaye yujjati, “but in this place both ‘heard by me’ and ‘my hearing’ fit.” This chapter recommends saying so. Where neither the verb nor a parallel settles an ending, the sentence is open, and to demand that Pāli be more determinate than its own commentators found it is to assert things the language does not say.

    A chain of genitives.

    1. aparikkhittassa unenclosed (m. gen. sg.) · gāmassa of an inhabited area (m. gen. sg.) · gharūpacāre at the access to a house (m. loc. sg.) · ṭhitassa standing (m. gen. sg. p.p.) · majjhimassa of middling stature (m. gen. sg.) · purisassa of a man (m. gen. sg.) · leḍḍupāto a stone’s throw (m. nom. sg.)

      “A stone’s throw of a man of middling stature standing at the access to a house of an unenclosed inhabited area.”

      The nesting is the same as in the chapter’s sentence, and the pivot word has changed. Aparikkhittassa gāmassa hangs on the locative gharūpacāre, and majjhimassa purisassa, with ṭhitassa agreeing with it, hangs on leḍḍupāto. Where the enclosed village was measured from its gate-post, an unenclosed one has no gate-post, so the measurement starts from the edge of the last house instead. One word moves and the whole definition changes its starting point, so the two sentences are printed one after the other.

      The endings cannot decide, being five identical -assa forms. What can sensibly own what decides it: a village has an access to a house, and a stone’s throw does not. Five genitives in seven words, at two depths, and the reader supplies the depth.

    Say it aloud.

    1. Ratiyā and taṇhāya. The company they keep makes them readable. Piyato, pemato and kāmato carry the -to suffix, which is not a proper case ending at all and can only mean ‘from’; the five verses are the same sentence five times over; and jāyati takes an ablative of the source in all five. Three unambiguous endings, one repeated frame, and one verb settle the two ambiguous words, though the words themselves say nothing. That is how a case is decided in practice.
