    Answers · Chapter 16

    Answers: Clause and Sentence Syntax

    Three items carry a trap: 17, 18 and 27. Each is named where it falls.

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Sīho, bhikkhave, migarājā sāyanhasamayaṁ āsayā nikkhamati. Āsayā nikkhamitvā vijambhati. Vijambhitvā samantā catuddisā anuviloketi. Samantā catuddisā anuviloketvā tikkhattuṁ sīhanādaṁ nadati. (AN 4.33 — A ii 33)

      The condition is that the subject does not change. The absolutive carries no person and no number, only priority, so it cannot say whose action it is, and the language may use it only where that question does not arise. One lion does all four things, so nikkhamitvā, vijambhitvā and anuviloketvā are available. Had somebody else been doing the coming out while the lion stretched, the absolutive would have been unusable and an absolute construction would have had to take its place: that is item 9’s rule, and this passage is the easy half of it.

      Four finite verbs, and each sentence opens by repeating in the absolutive what the sentence before ended with as a finite verb. This is Bodhi’s third pattern, and it is a fact about oral composition, not grammar: each sentence is joined to the last action of the one before, and that makes a long passage recitable. Whether one prints it as four sentences or as one is a decision about punctuation, and the Pāli has no punctuation to consult.

    2. Parinibbute bhagavati saha parinibbānā mahābhūmicālo ahosi bhiṁsanako salomahaṁso. (DN 16 — D ii 156)

      The locative absolute carries it, and no other word in the sentence shares the load. Parinibbute bhagavati is a clause of its own with its own subject, and the only thing marking it off is the case: the Blessed One stands in the locative, which he would not be if he were the subject of ahosi. Translate the two words as they stand, ‘in the extinguished Blessed One’, and you have read the case correctly and the construction not at all.

      The tense of the participle settles the order. Parinibbute is past, so the subordinate action is complete before the main one: the parinibbāna is over before the earth moves. A present participle there would have said the opposite, that the two ran together, and item 3 is that sentence. Neither contains a word meaning ‘when’, and the chapter states the rule once and never repeats it: past participle, subordinate action first; present participle, the two concurrent.

    3. Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

      Only the tense of the participle has changed, and it changes the timing entirely. A locative absolute with a passive present participle, so the two actions are concurrent: the discourse is still being delivered when Koṇḍañña’s insight breaks. The tense of the participle carries all of that, and no word in the sentence means ‘while’.

      Learn the pair together, because between them they exhaust the rule. Two sentences, one construction, one difference, two time relations; once the pair is fluent there is no more to remember about which is which.

    4. Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

      The added word is bhagavatā, and its case is the instrumental. A locative absolute with a past passive participle (so this time the subordinate action is complete before the main one), and with the agent added in the instrumental, bhagavatā. The grammatical subject stays in the locative and the logical subject stands beside it in a third case. Compare item 3: same construction, different participle, different time relation, and the pair is the clearest demonstration of the tense rule in the chapter.

      Follow the instrumental to find out who did the thing. Dhammacakke is the grammatical subject of the absolute and it did nothing; it is what was set rolling. The participle is passive, so the doer cannot be in the locative with it, and a passive puts its agent in the instrumental: Chapter 15’s rule, inside a subordinate clause. Item 2 has no agent at all because parinibbute is not passive: nobody extinguished the Blessed One.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Yā pana bhikkhunī lasuṇaṁ khādeyya, pācittiyaṁ. (Bi Pc 1 — Vin iv 259)

      The optative carries the ‘if’ and a case ending carries the ‘then’. There is no sace, no ce and no yadi; khādeyya carries the condition by itself. The consequence then arrives as a single noun in the nominative, pācittiyaṁ, with hoti understood, as in appamādo amatapadaṁ at the head of the chapter. A rule is a conditional with the ‘if’ left to a mood and the ‘then’ left to a case ending, and most of the Pātimokkha is built that way.

      Yā pana bhikkhunī gives the rule its generality: any nun there may be, not some particular nun. A name would have decided a case instead of making a rule, and a plural would have said something false, for the offence is incurred one nun at a time. The generic relative is Chapter 17’s material, and yā is its feminine nominative singular; that a rule of law opens with a relative pronoun and no antecedent states that chapter’s central point in one sentence.

    2. No cetaṁ, bhikkhave, abhavissa ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, nayidha jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyetha. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 80)

      An unreal condition. Abhavissa is the conditional, third singular, of bhavati; paññāyetha is a third-singular optative in the middle voice, built on the passive stem paññāyati. The protasis is contrary to fact and the apodosis draws the consequence that would follow if it were not.

      With indicatives the sentence would assert what it exists to deny. ‘There is no unborn, and no escape is discerned here’ is a well-formed Pāli sentence, and it says the opposite of what the passage exists to say. The vocabulary does not separate the two readings (the nouns, the negatives and the word order are the same), and two verb endings carry the entire difference. So the conditional tense, rare everywhere else in the language, is at home here: this construction needs it, and the chapter on tenses and moods had little occasion to show it.

    3. It denies the false premise and states the true one, using the causal correlative yasmā … tasmā: ‘but because there is an unborn … therefore an escape is discerned’. And the verbs change with it: the conditional and optative of 3.2 become the plain indicatives atthi and paññāyati of 3.3. Bodhi identifies this as the regular sequel to a counterfactual in the Nikāyas. Only the mood of the verbs marks the difference between the two sentences, and the whole force of the passage lies in that difference.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. The other verbs are in the absolutive, which is indeclinable and carries no person, number or tense, only priority. The condition is that the subject does not change. The absolutive cannot say whose action it is, so it can only be used where that question does not arise.

    2. If the prior action belongs to the subject of the main verb, use the absolutive; if it belongs to somebody else, use an absolute construction. That is Bodhi’s formulation, and nowhere else in the collection are the two set against each other instead of described separately.

    3. A present participle or a past passive participle may stand as the predicate of an absolute. The future passive participle may not, and Wijesekera gives the reason: it cannot agree with a substantive in an oblique case in the required way. He rules out the past active participle too, and there the rule is too strong: bhuttāvissa at DN 17 — D ii 195 is a past active participle in -āvin, standing in a genitive absolute with its own object, and it has thirteen root-text occurrences.

    4. The tradition has a case-substitution rule, sitting in the kāraka section beside the other substitutions. Kaccāyana 305, Anādare ca, and its vutti: anādare chaṭṭhīvibhatti hoti, sattamī ca, “in the sense of anādara the sixth case-ending occurs, and the seventh.” Both cases are named in one breath, as alternatives, and no construction is named at all.

    5. Real conditions put both verbs in the indicative (present or future), with an imperative sometimes standing in the apodosis. Possible conditions put the optative in both. Unreal conditions put the conditional in the protasis (usually abhavissa) and the optative in the apodosis, and only the third gives the conditional tense a regular home in the language.

    6. Hoti and atthi. When a substantive stands where the verb would be, concord of gender and number lapses: appamādo is masculine and amatapadaṁ neuter, and neither shifts to match the other. Where an adjective or a past participle is the predicate, concord holds.

    Whose action is it?

    1. The subject’s own. The Blessed One dresses, takes his bowl and robe, and enters, one actor throughout, so the first two verbs go into the absolutive and the third carries the tense and the person for all three. This is the absolutive chain.

    2. Somebody else’s, and it is a locative absolute. Ānanda leaves; Māra approaches. Two subjects, so the absolutive is unavailable and the case must carry the relation.

    3. Somebody else’s, and it is a genitive absolute: four words in the genitive making a clause with its own subject, the parents. Note that the same sentence then uses two absolutives, ohāretvā and acchādetvā, for the actions that do belong to the speaker. Every device in the chapter is in this one sentence.

    4. Somebody else’s, and it is not an absolute at all: this is a trap. Sandhānaṁ gahapatiṁ and āgacchantaṁ are both accusative, and together they are the object of addasā: “he saw that the householder Sandhāna was coming from afar.” This is Pāli’s real indirect statement, an accusative with a participle agreeing with it, and it is easy to mistake for an accusative absolute because the shape is the same.

    5. The subject’s own: a second trap. Daharova samāno, ‘while still young’, is nominative, agreeing with the so kho ahaṁ that is the subject of pabbajiṁ. Wijesekera calls this a semi-absolute: the temporal sense is real, but nothing is detached, because the participle’s subject and the sentence’s subject are the same person. It stands four words before the genitive absolute in item 16, in the same sentence, which makes that sentence the best single test in the chapter.

    6. The subject’s own. The absolutive again, this time in Bodhi’s second pattern, rhetorical repetition, the same absolutive chained across a list of four, with the four matching main verbs following.

    Name the construction and its sense.

    1. Locative absolute, conditional sense. No word in the sentence says ‘if’ or ‘because’; the construction says only that one state is the mark of another. Aggavaṁsa illustrates his own rule for the locative absolute with the parent formula, imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti: the tradition’s grammar and the tradition’s doctrine reach for the same sentence.

    2. Locative absolute, concessive. Five nouns and four participles in one clause, and the concessive force comes from none of them: it comes from the negative in the main clause. Rendered ‘when the rain is falling … he would not hear’ the sentence says nothing; rendered ‘even with the rain falling’ it says what it means.

    3. Locative absolute, causal. The disappearance of the wheel-treasure is why the king is displeased, and a verb of feeling in the main clause is the commonest signal for this reading.

    4. Locative absolute, attendant circumstance. The torches are not the cause of his setting out and not a condition of it; they accompany it. Note that the same sentence carries two absolutives, paṭiyādāpetvā and āropetvā, for the brahmin’s own prior actions: the division of labour is exact.

    5. Locative absolute, hypothetical, and the signal is that the main clause is a question. Wijesekera’s rendering for this class is ‘supposing’ or ‘now if’. The exchange runs: how are we to conduct ourselves towards women? — By not looking, Ānanda. — But supposing there is looking, how should one conduct oneself? — By not talking, Ānanda.

    6. Genitive absolute, and the sense shades between temporal and concessive: ‘even while my body is afflicted, my mind will be unafflicted’. Wijesekera notes that anādara is faintly implied here, and on Aggavaṁsa’s definition of anādara, the simultaneous occurrence of the two actions, the temporal and the concessive readings are one, not two.

    7. Genitive absolute with a past active participle, which is the exception to Wijesekera’s rule stated in answer 10. Bhuttāvissa is genitive singular, it has its own object bhojanaṁ, and the drowsiness belongs to the householder, not to the participle’s own clause.

    8. Locative absolute, conditional, and this is a trap. Hatthesu and pādesu are locative plural; sati is locative singular. The participle does not agree with its subject, and this is regular, not an error: sati stays masculine singular against feminine subjects and plural ones alike. The Digital Pali Dictionary has given up on it and files two entries for the same three letters, one singular and one plural.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    The sentence types.

    1. A possible condition, and the optative in both clauses is how you know. Sace by itself would not tell you: it introduces real conditions too. Meghiya is floating a request, not reporting a fact, and the two optatives say so.

    2. The locative absolute āditte … cele vā sīse vā carries it, with no particle at all. Translate it ‘if’, not ‘when’. Bodhi’s reason is the right one: the locative absolute with a past participle normally means ‘when X has happened’, but nobody’s turban is actually on fire, and the situation is offered as a hypothesis, not reported as an event.

    3. The verb is missing: hoti or atthi, understood, not written. And concord has lapsed: appamādo is masculine nominative, amatapadaṁ is neuter nominative, and they sit in apposition without either shifting. That lapse is regular whenever a substantive stands in the predicate; it is not a licence taken by the verse.

    4. It is marking purpose, not quotation. Read as a quotation the sentence is nonsense, because nobody is speaking; read as a purpose clause it is exact: the wise one guards his virtue so that praise may come to him. An imperative or optative closed with ti is the ordinary Pāli way of saying ‘in order that’, and it sits beside the infinitive and the dative of an action noun as the third means of expressing purpose.

    5. The simile frame seyyathāpi … evameva kho; a locative absolute tasmiṁ agāre āditte carrying a condition; and an infinitive of purpose, sotthiṁ kātuṁ. The verb is optative because the first term of a simile is imagined, not reported; that is the regular convention, and knowing it stops a reader hunting for the occasion on which a man with an eleven-doored house escaped a fire.

    6. The word order (atthi stands first) and the particle nu kho. Trust the particle. Fronting is real and Perniola is right that it can mark a question, but his own second example, anuññātosi mātāpitūhi agārasmā anagāriyaṁ pabbajjāya at MN 82 — M ii 60, has the verbal form first and is a statement, not a question: Raṭṭhapāla’s friends telling him he has his parents’ permission. Word order in Pāli is a rhetorical instrument, not a grammatical one.

    Adjudicate.

    1. An accusative absolute: santaṁ, the accusative singular of the present participle of atthi, with paraṁ lokaṁ in the accusative beside it. The evidence that the tradition recognised it is the commentary’s gloss on the parallel phrase at DN 9 — D i 186: evaṁ santan ti evaṁ sante. Bhummatthe hi etaṁ upayogavacanaṁ — “‘evaṁ santaṁ’ means ‘evaṁ sante’; for this is an accusative in the sense of the locative.” Buddhaghosa does what a grammarian does, identifying an unexpected ending by naming the one it stands for. It should be added that no premodern Pāli grammarian classified the accusative with the locative and the genitive here; the category is a modern Western one, which does not make it wrong but does mean a reader will not find it in Kaccāyana.

    2. Wijesekera reads it as a probable accusative absolute: ‘there being inward ill-will, he knows that there is inward ill-will’. Bodhi reads the parallel line as an ordinary direct object with a participle agreeing with it: ‘he understands the ill-will existing internally’. Both fit the Pāli, and the commentary supports each in a different place: bhummatthe at DN 9, but itthambhūtākhyānatthe upayogavacanaṁ, an accusative describing a state of being, at DN 4, where it resolves the phrase with a genitive instead. The main verb would settle it: where the accusative could be the verb’s object, it probably is. Pajānāti can take an object, so Bodhi’s reading is the safer one here, and saṅkappeti in item 34 cannot take paraṁ lokaṁ as its object, so that one is an absolute.

    3. The category is the nominative absolute, and the reader should conclude that it is not established for Pāli. Every case offered for it either has a competing reading in another edition or admits an ordinary analysis. This one turns on a single word in a single edition: saṁvaṭṭamāno occurs nowhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana in any of its four layers, and the Buddha Jayanti reads the locative with the Burmese. Collins’s other instance, DN 19, is not variation at all — every edition prints the locative absolute Govinde brāhmaṇe kālaṅkate, and the nominative Govindo brāhmaṇo kālakato is twenty lines away inside the king’s quoted lament, where it is an ordinary verbless sentence. And the tradition’s two verses do not show what they are offered for: in both, the nominative participle agrees with the subject of the main verb, which is a participle qualifying a subject and not an absolute of any kind. A claim about spelling or about a reading is settled against editions, not against a corpus, and this item really tests that.

    4. The first is a genitive absolute and the second a locative absolute, with the same subject, the same participle and the same sense: ‘he went forth although the child was crying’. They are printed together because Kaccāyana’s rule names both cases in one breath (anādare chaṭṭhīvibhatti hoti, sattamī ca), and the vutti illustrates each half. A reader should not take either as canonical Pāli because neither occurs in any root text: rudantasmiṁ dārake appears once in the whole Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, in this line of this grammar. The Saddanīti has the tradition’s word for illustrations of this kind: lokikappayoga, ‘worldly usage’, as against the suttapadāni it quotes elsewhere.

    Read the sentence.

    1. “That the Realised One should recollect, of past Buddhas who have attained final Nibbāna, even their birth, even their name, even their clan …” Yatra hi nāma with a future, carrying astonishment, not result; the future there is not future time. And inside it sits a second construction, the locative absolute atīte buddhe parinibbute, and inside that a run of ablatives in -to used adverbially. The sentence is the monks’ exclamation in the very conversation that supplied this chapter’s genitive-absolute example twelve segments earlier.

    The case law.

    1. tena kho pana samayena now at that time · aññataro a certain (m. nom. sg.) · bhikkhu monk (m. nom. sg.) · saṅghassa by the Sangha (m. gen. sg.) · odane rice (m. loc. sg.) · bhājīyamāne being distributed (m. loc. sg., pres. part. of the passive bhājīyati) · aparassa for another (m. gen. sg.) · bhāgaṁ a share (m. acc. sg.) · dehi give! (2 sg. imper.) · ti end of quotation · amūlakaṁ groundlessly (nt. acc. sg., adverbial) · aggahesi took (3 sg. aor.)

      “Now at that time a certain monk, while rice was being distributed by the Sangha, took a share groundlessly, saying ‘Give a share for another’.”

      The three constructions are a locative absolute, a quotation closed by ti, and an adverbial accusative. Saṅghassa odane bhājīyamāne is the absolute: its grammatical subject odane stands in the locative, its participle agrees with it, and its agent, the Sangha, is in the genitive, not the instrumental, which this chapter notes as the ordinary alternative. “Aparassa bhāgaṁ dehī”ti is direct speech with no verb of saying anywhere in the sentence, the ti alone marking it; Chapter 12 treats that omission, and a reader has to supply ‘saying’ from nothing but the particle. Amūlakaṁ is a neuter accusative used adverbially, which Chapter 15 sets out.

      The locative absolute shows that the monk was not distributing the rice. Had he been, the sentence would have used an absolutive, whose doer is always the doer of the main verb, and the whole offence depends on his not having been: he was a recipient claiming a second share under another monk’s name. The construction has a legal function before a grammatical one, and so the Vibhaṅga reaches for it so much more often than the suttas do.

    The reading passage.

    1. The exchange runs: “How, sir, are we to conduct ourselves towards women?” — “By not looking, Ānanda.” — “But supposing there is looking, sir, how should one conduct oneself?” — “By not talking, Ānanda.” — “But when talking, sir, how should one conduct oneself?” — “Mindfulness should be established, Ānanda.”

      The second and third questions are carried by locative absolutes (dassane … sati and, in the third, the instrumental ālapantena, with a comparable function), and the sense is hypothetical, because the main clause is a question: ‘supposing there is looking’. Wijesekera identifies the interrogative main clause as the regular signal for that reading, and it makes ‘when there is looking’ a thinner rendering than ‘supposing there is’.

      Sati is masculine locative singular and dassane is neuter locative singular, so they agree in case and number but not in gender. That is the regular behaviour set out in item 27: sati does not trouble to agree, and the reader should stop expecting it to.

      What the exchange demonstrates is how much a Pāli sentence can leave unsaid. Three of the four answers are single words in an oblique case with no verb at all (adassanaṁ, anālāpo), and the questions carry their subordination on the ending of one participle. There is not a conjunction in the passage. Trained to look for ‘if’, ‘when’ and ‘although’, you will find nothing to look at, and will have to do what this chapter has asked throughout: read the case, read the participle, and supply the relation.
