    Answers: Review — The Noun Phrase

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. The three nouns are ratti (f., an i-stem), yojanaṁ (n., an a-stem) and saṁsāro (m., an a-stem), and they hand dīgha the endings -ā, -aṁ and -o.

      Two of the three match and one does not. Dīghaṁ yojanaṁ and dīgho saṁsāro are the same ending on the same declension, adjective and noun in step. Dīghā ratti is not. Ratti is an i-stem whose nominative singular is the bare stem; dīghā is an ā-stem feminine, because an adjective in -a makes its feminine in -ā and that is a different declension altogether. The two words agree in gender, number and case and share no ending at all.

      Take that from the verse, for this is the commonest unnoticed error in reading Pāli. Agreement is agreement in categories, never in endings. Learn concord by matching the letters at the ends of words and you will read dīghā ratti as a mismatch and start looking for the mistake. Item 12 is the rule this depends on; here is the rule with a noun standing next to it.

    2. Three of the four nouns wear their gender and one does not.

      Patto, uttarāsaṅgo and antaravāsako all end in -o, which is the masculine nominative singular of the a-stems, and for these three the ending decides, with the standing qualification of item 13 that an -o in the nominative singular may also be an old -as stem on its way into the masculine. Saṅghāṭi decides nothing. It is an i-stem, and the i-stem nominative singular is the bare stem in both genders: aggi is masculine and ratti is feminine and the two look identical. It happens to be feminine, and nothing on this page says so.

      Ayaṁ is no help either, four times over. The nominative singular of ima- is ayaṁ in the masculine and ayaṁ in the feminine, so the only word agreeing with all four nouns cannot distinguish two of the three genders. It agrees correctly with a feminine and with three masculines and reports the fact in the same three letters.

      So gender is visible in three places in this sentence, all of them the -o endings, and nowhere for the robe. A reader must then do as item 13 recommends and look for something that agrees. Here nothing does, for the sentence has no adjective, no participle and no verb with a gendered form anywhere near saṅghāṭi, and when that fails there is only the dictionary. In general, Pāli marks gender far less often than a reader coming from a European language expects, and a fair proportion of the time there is nothing to read it off at all.

    3. Sahassa is a noun in sahassaṁ candānaṁ: neuter, nominative singular, with candānaṁ a genitive plural hanging on it. Literally “a thousand of moons”, which is the second of the four constructions item 9 lists. In mahāsamuddasahassāni it is the second member of a compound, mahāsamudda + sahassa, neuter nominative plural: the fourth construction.

      Cattāri agrees with mahāsamuddasahassāni, the thousands, and not with the oceans. It is neuter nominative plural because sahassāni is, and the oceans are inside a compound where nothing can reach them to agree with.

      Put the two together and the whole design of the higher numerals falls out. From nineteen up a numeral is a noun with a gender of its own, and a noun cannot agree with what it counts, because it is not describing it, so the thing counted has to be attached some other way, and Pāli has two: put it in the genitive, or compound it. Hence the genitive keeps appearing where an English reader expects a plain plural. And a number larger than a thousand is built by counting the thousands themselves, with a low numeral agreeing, as low numerals do, with a high one. Cattāri … sahassāni, four thousands. The two systems are working in the same phrase, one inside the other.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Tvaṁ and ahaṁ are not filling subject slots, because rakkha and rakkhissāmi have already said who is acting. They are there for contrast, as item 6 predicts of a pronoun subject that appears when nothing requires one.

      Take them out and the sentence still works: samma medakathālike, mamaṁ rakkha, taṁ rakkhissāmi is good Pāli and means the same thing. What has gone is the opposition, you against me, and with it the design of the line. The Pāli is a chiasmus, tvaṁ … mamaṁ, ahaṁ … taṁ, second person outside and first person in, then the reverse; the four pronouns are the four corners of it and the sentence is built so that a listener hears the two people being set against each other before the verbs arrive.

      That answers the recall question in concrete form: a Pāli pronoun subject is never required by the grammar and is always there for rhetoric. The acrobat is proposing a bargain, and the pronouns serve the bargain. The Buddha’s reply, a few lines later, refuses the terms of it.

    2. Cattāro is masculine nominative plural, agreeing with dhammā, which is masculine nominative plural. The four things it counts are āyu (neuter), vaṇṇo (masculine), sukhaṁ (neuter) and balaṁ (neuter): three neuters and one masculine.

      Had the numeral agreed with the list and not with the word that heads it, three of the four being neuter, a reader would expect cattāri. It is cattāro, and the reason is that the numeral never sees the list. Cattāro dhammā is a complete noun phrase and the four items stand in apposition to it, naming what the dhammā are. Agreement runs to the syntactic head and stops.

      So the general instruction is: when the members of a list disagree in gender with the word that names them, everything agrees with the word that names them, and the members agree with nothing: they are in apposition, and apposition requires case, not gender. Try to make a numeral or an adjective agree with the contents of a list and you will find sentences that cannot be parsed at all.

      The odd stem is āyu, historically a stem in -us, the sibling of the -as stems of item 13, with the same history: an old neuter consonant stem drifting into a vowel declension, so that the canon writes āyunā and āyuno where the older language would have had āyusā. The nouns chapter takes it as its clearest single case of that drift.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. Because the verb carries person, a pronoun subject is never structurally required, and it follows that when one appears it is there for emphasis, contrast, or the drawing of a distinction. The prediction holds: ahaṁ and tvaṁ turn up where a speaker is set against someone else, not where English would simply need a subject. Treat them as ordinary subject pronouns and half of them will be inexplicable and the other half translated flat.

    2. Up to eighteen a numeral is an adjective, with no gender of its own, taking the gender of what it counts and standing in that noun’s case. From nineteen upwards it is a noun, with its own gender, normally singular however many things it counts, and the thing counted attached by some means other than agreement. The boundary falls at nineteen, not twenty, because nineteen is ekūnavīsati, ‘twenty less one’, a word built on ‘twenty’: it belongs with what follows it.

    3. Eka ‘one’ declines as a pronominal adjective throughout. The form that goes further is the feminine genitive/dative and locative singular ekissā, which follows the ki- / ima- type and not the plain -assā the rest of the pattern would predict.

    4. Agreement; the counted noun in the genitive plural; no inflection at all; and a determinative compound. A reader assumes agreement is the only one, because the low numerals agree and a reader meets the low numerals first. That assumption founders on the genitive construction.

    5. Ubha ‘both’, whose nominative and accusative ubho is a true dual ending generalised across the rest of its inflection. Its -o looks like a masculine nominative singular and is not one. And ‘two’ has no proper paradigm because the dual exists above all for a numeral meaning ‘two’: when the category went, the word had to be reassembled. Dve is an old dual transferred to the masculine, and everything else in its column is borrowed from ‘three’.

    6. Nominative, vocative and accusative. Everywhere else (instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative, locative, in both numbers) masculine and neuter are identical, so the appendix gives them one table with two columns instead of two tables. What follows for a reader is blunt: an oblique ending cannot tell you the gender. Buddhassa and cittassa are the same ending on the same declension; purisesu and rūpesu likewise. If the gender matters, it has to come from the dictionary, or from an adjective agreeing with the noun, or from a nominative somewhere else in the passage; gender cannot be read off -ena.

    7. A noun in -a takes -ī: dāsa → dāsī. An adjective in -a takes -ā: dīgha → dīghā. The difference is grammatical because the same stem shape gives two different suffixes according to the word’s part of speech, which no rule about sounds could predict, since the phonetic environment is identical. The best evidence is the pair of words that do both, according to which they are being at the time: kalyāṇa gives the adjective kalyāṇā ‘beautiful’ and the noun kalyāṇī ‘a beautiful woman’. One stem, one sound, two suffixes, and the only thing that has changed is whether the word is naming or describing.

    8. Those words, manas, cetas, tapas and the rest, are historically neuter and are migrating into the masculine a-declension, because their old nominative mano looks like an a-stem masculine nominative in -o and attracts masculine agreement accordingly. So the grammars are not contradicting each other so much as describing different moments: Perniola and Ñāṇatusita record where the class came from, DPD where it has arrived. The sense-base that gives it away is the mind. At SN 35.2 the Buddha runs through the six, and five take the agreement their own gender requires (cakkhuṁ dukkhaṁ, jivhā dukkhā, kāyo dukkho), while the sixth is mano dukkho, masculine. The adjective is the witness, and that is the general moral of this whole group: when a noun’s own ending is ambiguous or contested, look at what agrees with it.

    Say what the form is, and what decides it.

    Say what the form is, and what decides it.

    1. ekissā — the numeral eka ‘one’, feminine singular, genitive/dative or locative. The form belongs to the numeral chapter and its shape belongs to the pronoun chapter: -issā is the ki- / ima- ending, not the -assā that the ordinary pronominal-adjective pattern would give. And the case cannot be recovered from the form, since genitive, dative and locative fall together here; whatever governs the word decides it. A verb of giving or a noun of possession takes the first two; a locative sense, or a locative absolute, the third.

    2. tissannaṁ — the numeral ti ‘three’, feminine genitive/dative plural. The masculine and neuter is tiṇṇaṁ, and the split matters: ti distinguishes all three genders in the nominative and accusative but only masculine-neuter against feminine in the genitive. The gender of the noun counted decides the reading, and the information exists nowhere else. The doubled -ss- is taken over from the feminine nominative tisso, so this one cell looks unlike its neighbours.

    3. paṭhamaṁ — the ordinal paṭhama ‘first’, and it has two readings that no ending will separate. As an adjective it is neuter nominative or accusative singular (or masculine accusative singular), agreeing with its noun. As a temporal adverb it is ‘the first time, first of all’, an ordinary neuter accusative used adverbially. Only the sentence decides, and sometimes not even that: sentence 22 below is a case where both readings are available and the sense comes out the same. An adverbial reading is wanted when there is no neuter singular for it to agree with.

    4. dvīhi — the numeral dvi ‘two’, instrumental or ablative, and all genders at once, since dvi makes no gender distinction anywhere. This is the form that teaches the general lesson: a numeral whose instrumental and ablative have fallen together shows nothing about the case of its phrase, and neither does a noun in -ehi, which is syncretic the same way. The case has to be read off whatever else the phrase governs. The chapter’s example is dvīhi dhammehi niyyāti abhijjhāya ca byāpādena ca, where the whole phrase is settled as instrumental by byāpādena alone.

    5. tāya — the distal pronoun ta(d) ‘that, she’, feminine singular, and then instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative or locative. Five cases in one form. It is here because this is the same five-way collapse the feminine ā-stem nouns have in kaññāya, and the same one the feminine ī-stems have in nadiyā — pronoun and noun behaving identically, for once. The same things decide it as for the nouns: the verb, any governing indeclinable, and whether there is something for a genitive to possess. Being a pronoun does not help at all, and that runs against the grain, because pronouns are usually more informative than nouns and here they are not.

    6. manasā — the -as stem manas ‘mind’, instrumental or ablative singular, with the old consonant-stem ending -asā in place of the a-stem -ena. It belongs to the stem class that is halfway into the a-declension, and manena exists beside it. Its ending is one of the four (mano, manasā, manaso, manasi) that mark the old declension still working. And instrumental and ablative have fallen together here as they do throughout the plural of every class, so the form does not choose between them. In practice the instrumental is far likelier, because manasā stands in the standing phrases of Dhp 1 and 2, manasā ce paduṭṭhena and manasā ce pasannena, where the adjective paduṭṭhena agreeing with it is itself an a-stem instrumental: one word in the old declension and its adjective in the new one, in the same phrase.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Read the sentence.

    1. Ime kho, bhikkhave, tayo niruttipathā adhivacanapathā paññattipathā asaṅkiṇṇā … (SN 22.62 — S iii 72)

      imethesem nom plkhoindeedbhikkhavebhikkhusm voc pltayothreem nom plniruttipathāways of speechm nom pladhivacanapathāways of designationm nom plpaññattipathāways of descriptionm nom plasaṅkiṇṇāuncorruptedm nom pl

      “These, bhikkhus, are the three ways of speech, of designation, of description, and they are uncorrupted …”

      The plainest case, and it is here to be the control. Ime and tayo are both masculine nominative plural and so are all four nouns: pronoun, numeral and noun in one case, one number, one gender. This is the agreement construction, the first of the four, and the reason a reader comes to expect it everywhere. Notice that the numeral does nothing a pronoun could not do, for both simply qualify the noun, and the next sentence is set for that reason.

    2. Yā kho, bhikkhave, imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ saṅgati sannipāto samavāyo, ayaṁ vuccati cakkhusamphasso. (SN 35.93 — S iv 68)

      yāwhichf nom sgkhoindeedbhikkhavebhikkhusm voc plimesaṁof thesem gen pltiṇṇaṁof threem gen pldhammānaṁof thingsm gen plsaṅgatimeetingf nom sgsannipātocoming togetherm nom sgsamavāyoconcurrencem nom sgayaṁthisnom sgvuccatiis calledcakkhusamphassoeye-contactm nom sg

      “The meeting, coming together and concurrence of these three things is what is called eye-contact.”

      Three genitive plurals in a row, imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ, and they agree. This is ordinary agreement that happens to be in the genitive, not the genitive construction of the numeral chapter, where a numeral-noun governs the thing counted. Be able to state the difference. In sahassaṁ candānaṁ the numeral is nominative and the noun genitive, and they cannot be a unit; here all three words share case, number and gender, and the whole phrase then depends on saṅgati. Suspicion of numerals with genitives will over-correct here.

      Then the two pronouns, which behave differently from each other. Yā is unambiguously feminine, and it agrees with saṅgati, the nearest of the three subjects, and not with the masculine sannipāto and samavāyo that follow it. Ayaṁ is the only form of ima- that serves masculine and feminine nominative singular alike, so it is untroubled by the mismatch and can answer to cakkhusamphasso without strain. A syncretic form is usually a nuisance; here it is a convenience, and the sentence would be harder to write without it.

    3. Mano tesaṁ dhammānaṁ paṭhamaṁ uppajjati, anvadeva akusalā dhammā. (AN 1.56 — A i 11)

      manomindn nom sgtesaṁof thosem gen pldhammānaṁof thingsm gen plpaṭhamaṁfirstn nom./acc. sg, or adverbialuppajjatiarisesanvadevaimmediately afterindakusalāunskilfulm nom pldhammāthingsm nom pl

      “Mind arises first of those things, and unskilful things follow immediately after.”

      This is item 16 in a sentence, and it does not resolve. Paṭhamaṁ can be a neuter nominative singular agreeing with mano, which is neuter: ‘mind is the first of those things to arise’. Or it can be the adverbial accusative the chapter met among the ordinal’s non-counting uses, ‘mind arises first’, which is how Bodhi renders it. Both are available, both are good Pāli, and the sense is the same either way, so nothing in the sentence forces a choice, and that is no defect in the exercise. Expect every ambiguity to be resolved by context and you will be puzzled by a great many sentences like this one.

      Tesaṁ dhammānaṁ is a genitive plural of the distal ta(d), agreeing with its noun and depending on paṭhamaṁ, ‘first of those things’, and this reading makes the adjectival analysis attractive. On the adverbial analysis it depends on nothing in particular and is simply the domain within which the arising happens first. The two readings differ in the function of the genitive, a cleaner way to see the ambiguity than arguing about the ordinal.

      One further thing, which was not visible when this review covered only two chapters. Mano is glossed neuter above, and item 13 has just established that the grammars divide over its gender. The adjectival reading of paṭhamaṁ depends on taking mano as neuter, because a masculine would want paṭhamo. So the two ambiguities are not independent: settle the gender and you have half-settled the ordinal. Nothing here settles either: the sentence has one reading in which mano is neuter and paṭhamaṁ agrees with it, and another in which the question of gender never arises because paṭhamaṁ is an adverb.

    4. Tīṇimāni, bhikkhave, titthāyatanāni yāni paṇḍitehi samanuyuñjiyamānāni … parampi gantvā akiriyāya saṇṭhahanti. (AN 3.61 — A i 173)

      tīṇithreen nom plimānithesen nom plbhikkhavemendicantsm voc pltitthāyatanānisectarian tenetsn nom plyāniwhichn nom plpaṇḍitehiby the wisem ins plsamanuyuñjiyamānānibeing cross-examinedn nom pl, passive present participleparaṁfurtherapievengantvāhaving goneakiriyāyain inactionf dat./loc. sgsaṇṭhahantithey come to rest

      “There are these three sectarian tenets, mendicants, which — when cross-examined by the wise and pressed even further — come to rest in inaction.”

      Four words in a row all carrying the neuter nominative plural, from four different chapters: the numeral tīṇi, the demonstrative imāni, the noun titthāyatanāni, and the relative yāni. Add the participle samanuyuñjiyamānāni and it is five. This is the agreement construction at full stretch, and it is here because it shows what agreement buys a reader: five words, and the case-number-gender of all five is established the moment any one of them is.

      Notice that tīṇi is the neuter form of ‘three’. It could not be tayo, which is masculine, and the choice is forced by titthāyatanāni. That is the numeral chapter’s first construction, behaving as the review’s item 9 said it does.

      Paṇḍitehi is the odd one out. It is masculine instrumental plural, agreeing with no other word in the sentence, and it is an adjective used as a noun: ‘the wise’, paṇḍita, standing on its own without a noun to describe, the pattern the gender-and-adjectives chapter set out. It is instrumental because it is the agent of a passive participle.

    5. Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ, kusalassa upasampadā; Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna sāsanaṁ. (Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27)

      sabbapāpassaof all eviln gen sgakaraṇaṁthe not-doingn nom sgkusalassaof the wholesomen gen sgupasampadāthe undertakingf nom sgsacittapariyodapanaṁthe cleansing of one’s own mindn nom sgetaṁthisn nom sgbuddhānaof the Buddhasm gen plsāsanaṁthe teachingn nom sg

      “Not doing any evil, undertaking the wholesome, cleansing one’s own mind — this is the teaching of the Buddhas.”

      Three subjects, then a predication, and every chapter of Part II is in it.

      Sabba ‘all’ is a pronominal adjective: it declines on the pronoun pattern, not the noun pattern, and here it is the first member of a compound with pāpa. Kusalassa is an adjective used as a noun and neuter, which is the gender such a noun takes when it names a quality and not a person, the same word that was an ordinary adjective in kusalaṁ ṭhānaṁ. Upasampadā is a feminine ā-stem nominative singular standing as one of three parallel subjects, two of which are neuter; nothing agrees with anything across the three, because they are a list, not a phrase. And etaṁ, neuter nominative singular, answers to sāsanaṁ and gathers all three.

      The form to stop on is buddhāna. It is a genitive plural, ‘of the Buddhas’, with the niggahīta dropped: buddhānaṁ without its -ṁ. This is a metrical licence, common in verse and rare in prose, and it is canonical: 23 occurrences in the root texts against 148 for the full form. If you have met only -ānaṁ you will not recognise it, yet it can be nothing else.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. buddhāna (item 24), imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ (item 21), sahassaṁ candānaṁ (item 3).

      The chapters behind them are not the same. Buddhāna draws on the nouns chapter alone, for the a-stem genitive plural, with the metre chapter accounting for the nasal it has dropped. Sahassaṁ candānaṁ draws on two: the numerals chapter for the construction, a noun taking a genitive, not an adjective agreeing, and then the same a-stem genitive plural again for the ending on the moons. The middle one draws on three at once. Imesaṁ is the pronouns chapter’s imesaṁ; tiṇṇaṁ is the numerals chapter’s tiṇṇaṁ; and dhammānaṁ is an ordinary a-stem genitive plural out of the nouns chapter. Three words in a row, in the same case, from three different paradigms. A Pāli noun phrase is routinely like this, and so the four chapters of Part II are one part, not four.

      The short one is buddhāna, for buddhānaṁ. To be sure it is not an error you would have to go outside Part II entirely. The line is verse, and a final niggahīta is regularly dropped there for the metre. That is the metre chapter’s business, and item 28 of that set is this very line, asked from the other side: what the missing niggahīta buys. The editions also agree on the reading: the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society all print buddhāna here. A form that looks short is a claim about what an editor printed before it is a claim about what a poet did, and the way to check that is the apparatus, not the grammar.
