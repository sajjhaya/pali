    Answers: Nouns

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Attā hi attano nātho. — The long vowel is the ā of attā, and again of nātho; the doubled consonant is the tt, which belongs to both attā and attano and has to be held in each. Said properly the line has a shape: two heavy words, a short particle, and a landing.

      Attano is the genitive singular of attan ‘self’, a masculine -an stem, the same class as rājan in item 9. Four words teach two points. The genitive and dative of this class fall together in -ano, so attano is equally ‘for oneself’; and the line puts one stem in two cases, attā nominative and attano genitive, which is the trick item 21 turns at greater length.

    2. Punappunaṁ vaccho upeti mātaraṁ. — The doubled pp is the whole point of punappunaṁ, ‘again and again’, which is puna doubled with the consonant assimilating at the join.

      Mātaraṁ is the accusative singular of mātar ‘mother’, a feminine -ar stem, and its nominative singular is mātā. Note the short a of mātaraṁ against the long ā of satthāraṁ in item 10. That is no spelling accident: it is the division between the relationship nouns (mātar, pitar, bhātar), which keep the short vowel here, and the agent nouns formed from verbs, which lengthen it. One vowel separates a family word from a job description.

    3. Te taṁ rattiṁ tikkhattuṁ paccuṭṭhāya pañjalikā aggiṁ namassanti. Rattiṁ is feminine and aggiṁ masculine, and the line does not show it. The i-stem accusative singular is -iṁ in both genders, so the ending cannot help; and the only word that might have helped does not, because taṁ is the accusative singular of ta(d) in all three genders alike.

      Only knowledge of the words decides it. Ratti is feminine and aggi masculine, as the dictionary and the paradigms have it, and a reader learns them one at a time. A sentence can supply, when it happens to, an adjective or a participle agreeing with the noun in a case where the genders differ, and this line supplies none. It shows fairly how much of Pāli gender is vocabulary, not grammar.

    4. Mātā yathā niyaṁ puttam āyusā ekaputtamanurakkhe. — Mātā is the nominative singular and mātaraṁ in item 2 the accusative singular, of one word.

      A search of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana for the word āyusā on its own finds nothing, and the form is here all the same: the edition has puttam and āyusā run together as puttamāyusā, so a search for the word looks straight past it. This is the caution the chapter draws out of this very line, and it is one to keep. A failed search is a question, not a verdict — look for the string inside longer words before believing it.

      Āyusā is an instrumental singular, and the form is the older one: āyu ‘life’ is historically a -us stem, and -usā is what such a stem does in the instrumental. Its ordinary canonical instrumental is āyunā, on the u-stem pattern the word has otherwise gone over to. The old form survives in verse, which is where this line is.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Idha pana, upavāṇa, bhikkhu cakkhunā rūpaṁ disvā rūpappaṭisaṁvedī ca hoti. — “Here, Upavāṇa, a monk, having seen a form with the eye, experiences the form.”

      Cakkhunā is the form of item 11, met in its own sentence: this line and its fellows are why the instrumental is the reading to reach for first. Look again at rūpaṁ while you have the line in your mouth, because -aṁ is the neuter nominative and accusative singular, and only disvā decides between them: one does not see with a form.

    2. Nibbindaṁ virajjati; virāgā vimuccati. Vimuttasmiṁ vimuttamiti ñāṇaṁ hoti. — Virāgā is an ablative singular of virāga ‘the fading of desire’, a masculine a-stem: an ablative of cause, ‘through the fading of desire’. Vimuttasmiṁ is a locative singular, marking the circumstance — ‘when he is freed’.

      Two a-stem obliques in consecutive clauses, and they show the two forms the declension offers. Virāgā takes the bare -ā, which is the ablative that stands beside -asmā, -amhā and -ato; vimuttasmiṁ takes the full -asmiṁ. Both are ordinary canonical forms, not paradigm fillers, and not every cell in the table can say as much.

    3. … pubbe ananussutesu dhammesu cakkhuṁ udapādi, ñāṇaṁ udapādi, paññā udapādi, vijjā udapādi, āloko udapādi. — Paññā and vijjā are feminine, cakkhuṁ and ñāṇaṁ neuter, āloko masculine; all five are nominative singular, and ananussutesu dhammesu is a masculine locative plural.

      The ending narrows the field in every case and settles it in none, and the set you are holding supplies the counterexamples. -ā here marks paññā and vijjā as feminine ā-stems, but attā in item 1 and mātā in item 4 are also nominative singulars in -ā, from consonant stems, and one of those is masculine. -o marks āloko as a masculine a-stem, but mano, whose locative is item 12, has the same nominative and is the word whose gender the sources cannot agree about. -aṁ is the least informative of the three: neuter nominative and accusative singular, and masculine accusative singular besides.

      So the ending gives you a short list and the sentence decides among them, which is the chapter’s whole argument in five words of one line. Here it is the verb that decides: udapādi, ‘arose’, takes no object, so every noun beside it is a subject.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    1. bhikkhūnaṁ — bhikkhu, masculine u-stem. Genitive/dative plural. The lengthened stem vowel is the plural marker: bhikkhu-naṁ becomes bhikkhūnaṁ by the same lengthening that gives bhikkhūhi and bhikkhūsu. One row, two cases, because the genitive and dative have fallen together everywhere except the a-stem singular.

    2. raññā — rājan ‘king’, masculine -an stem. Instrumental or ablative singular. Know this form cold, because nothing about it announces that it belongs to rājā: the -jan- of the stem has assimilated to -ññ-. The alternative rājinā exists and is very rare — one occurrence in the root texts against seventy-one for raññā.

    3. satthāraṁ — satthar ‘teacher’, masculine -ar stem. Accusative singular. The long stem vowel matters here: it marks satthar as an agent noun, one of the group formed from verbs (dātar ‘giver’, netar ‘leader’), as against the relationship nouns pitar, mātar, bhātar, which have a short vowel in this case and in the nominative, vocative and accusative plural. Satthāraṁ against pitaraṁ is the whole distinction in two words, and item 2’s mātaraṁ shows it in a real sentence.

    4. cakkhunā — cakkhu ‘eye’, neuter u-stem. Instrumental or ablative singular, and the form does not choose between them. In practice the instrumental is overwhelmingly the more likely, because cakkhunā is a standing phrase: cakkhunā rūpaṁ disvā, ‘having seen a form with the eye’, which is item 5.

    5. manasi — manas ‘mind’, an -as stem. Locative singular. The -asi ending is one of the four that mark this class as still declining the old way, not as an ordinary a-stem, alongside mano, manasā and manaso. Note that the same word also has a complete a-stem declension running beside this one (manaṁ, manena, manasmiṁ), which is the migration described in the chapter, and the same migration that has taken āyusā to āyunā in item 4.

    6. bhagavato — bhagavant, masculine -(n)t stem. Genitive/dative singular. It is built on the weak stem bhagavat-, not the strong bhagavant-, which is the class’s defining behaviour: strong stem in the accusative singular and throughout the plural (bhagavantaṁ, bhagavanto), weak stem in the other singular cases (bhagavatā, bhagavato, bhagavati).

    7. purisā — three possibilities. Nominative plural masculine (purisa-as > purisā); vocative plural, which is the same form; and ablative singular, from the bare -ā ending that stands beside -asmā, -amhā and -ato — the ending item 6’s virāgā carries. To settle it, look at the verb: a plural verb makes it a nominative plural. If the verb is singular, look for a verb of motion, separation, origin or fear, which is what an ablative attaches to — and for the possibility that a following word governs it.

    8. kaññāya — five: instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative and locative singular. This is the sharpest ambiguity in the nominal system, and so the feminine ā-stems deserve attention out of proportion to their difficulty. The form itself carries no information beyond “feminine, singular, oblique”. Everything is decided by the sentence: the verb, any preposition-like indeclinable, and whether there is another noun for it to depend on: a genitive needs something to possess, a dative someone to benefit.

    9. rūpāni — neuter nominative, vocative or accusative plural. The three are always identical in the neuter, in every stem class, which saves effort instead of adding difficulty. Settle it by looking for another nominative in the clause and by the verb: a transitive verb with its own subject makes rūpāni the object. The singular rūpaṁ has the same ambiguity in a shorter form, which is item 5.

    10. aggino — genitive or dative singular of aggi ‘fire’, built on the -ino ending this class shares with the -in stems. It is also listed in the paradigms as a nominative plural, an alternative to aggayo. Here the texts settle what the grammar leaves open: as a nominative plural aggino occurs nowhere in the root texts, only in the commentaries and the grammarians’ works. So the ambiguity is real on paper and, in the canon, almost always resolves to the genitive.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    1. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

      mano-pubbaṅgamāhaving mind as forerunnerm nom pldhammāstates, thingsm nom plmano-seṭṭhāhaving mind as chiefm nom plmano-mayāmade of mindm nom pl

      “Mind goes before all states; mind is their chief; they are made of mind.”

      Two stem classes, and one of them is disguised. Dhamma is an ordinary masculine a-stem, nominative plural. The three long words are compound adjectives agreeing with it, each built on manas, and manas appears in composition as mano-, its old nominative form pressed into service as a stem. So a reader meets mano- dozens of times in compounds and manas- almost never: the compounding form and the paradigm have parted company.

      Manoseṭṭhā also contains a superlative, seṭṭha ‘best, chief’, which is one of the irregular comparatives set out in the next chapter, not formed from any adjective it resembles.

    2. Attanā hi kataṁ pāpaṁ, attanā saṅkilissati. (Dhp 165 — Dhp p. 24)

      attanāby oneselfm ins sghiindeedkataṁdonepast participle, n nom sgpāpaṁeviln nom sgattanāby oneselfm ins sgsaṅkilissatione is defiled

      “Evil is done by oneself; by oneself one is defiled.”

      Attan is an -an stem, and attanā is its instrumental singular: the agent of a passive construction in the first clause and the means in the second. Pāpa is a neuter a-stem, nominative singular, the subject of the participle kataṁ, which agrees with it. Two stem classes, and the whole verse rests on repeating one word in one case. Item 1 is the same stem in two other cases, four verses earlier in the same collection.

      The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana’s own apparatus records a variant here: the Sinhalese, Syāmese and Pali Text Society editions read attanāva, not attanā hi, and the printed PTS text has it so. Nothing in the grammar changes, and it is a fair illustration of the kind of variation the chapter on the alphabet described — real, recorded, and of no consequence for reading.

    3. Ārocemi kho te, mahārāja, paṭivedemi kho te, mahārāja, adhivattati kho taṁ, mahārāja, jarāmaraṇaṁ. (SN 3.25 — S i 101)

      ārocemiI announcekhoindeedteto youdat./gen. sgmahārājagreat kingm voc sgpaṭivedemiI informadhivattatibears down, rolls intaṁupon youacc sgjarāmaraṇaṁold age and deathn nom sg

      “I announce it to you, great king; I inform you, great king: old age and death are bearing down upon you, great king.”

      Three stem classes. Mahārāja is the -an stem rājan as the last member of a compound, in the vocative singular, and the vocative is -a, as in the simple word rāja, while the nominative of the compound is mahārājā. Jarāmaraṇaṁ is an a-stem, neuter nominative singular; it is a dvanda compound of two nouns, ‘ageing and death’, and like several such compounds it is treated as a single neuter singular, not as a plural. Te and taṁ are pronouns and belong to a later chapter; te here is the enclitic dative, ‘to you’.

      The rhetorical shape matters, since this book’s premise is that the grammar serves the reading. The vocative is repeated three times in one sentence, which ordinary Pāli does not do. This is the Pabbatūpama Sutta, the simile of the mountain: a trusted man arrives from the eastern direction reporting a mountain as high as the clouds advancing and crushing every living thing, and then another from each of the remaining directions. Old age and death are the fifth report. The grammar of address carries the insistence.

    4. tena bhikkhunā by that monk (m. instr. sg.) · saṅghaṁ the Sangha (m. acc. sg.) · upasaṅkamitvā having approached (absol.) · ekaṁsaṁ over one shoulder (m. acc. sg., adverbial) · uttarāsaṅgaṁ the upper robe (m. acc. sg.) · karitvā having arranged (absol.) · vuḍḍhānaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ of the senior monks (m. gen. pl.) · pāde the feet (m. acc. pl.) · vanditvā having paid homage (absol.) · ukkuṭikaṁ squatting (adverbial) · nisīditvā having sat (absol.) · añjaliṁ joined palms (m. acc. sg.) · paggahetvā having raised (absol.) · evaṁ thus · assa by him (m. gen./dat. sg.) · vacanīyo is to be said (m. nom. sg., fpp.)

      “That monk should approach the Sangha, arrange his upper robe over one shoulder, pay homage at the feet of the senior monks, sit down squatting, raise his joined palms, and say this.”

      Bhikkhunā is the instrumental singular and bhikkhūnaṁ the genitive plural, both of the u-stem bhikkhu. The first is the agent of the whole sentence: a gerundive construction puts its agent in the instrumental, so the monk is nowhere in the nominative. The second is possessive: the feet belong to the senior monks. The stem vowel lengthens before the plural ending and not before the singular one, the difference to hold on to, since bhikkhunā and bhikkhūnaṁ are otherwise easy to confuse by eye. Said aloud they are different words, and that is the reason for saying them.

      Pāde is the accusative plural of the a-stem pāda ‘foot’, the object of vanditvā. It cannot be a locative singular, though the form is identical, because vandati takes an accusative: one pays homage to the feet, and Pāli treats that as a direct object, not as a place. The verb decides it, not the ending. That is the chapter’s general rule in a real sentence: an ending narrows the possibilities and the context decides among them.

    5. The four phrases, with the line each came from:

      1. attano nātho (Dhp 160 — Dhp p. 24): attano, genitive singular of the -an stem attan; nātho, nominative singular of the masculine a-stem nātha. English needs three words and a possessive; Pāli needs two.

      2. cakkhunā (SN 35.70 — S iv 41): instrumental singular of the neuter u-stem cakkhu. One word for “with the eye”, and no preposition anywhere: the case is the preposition, the hardest habit to acquire in producing Pāli.

      3. āyusā (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26): instrumental singular of āyu, in the older -us form. If you produced āyunā, you produced the canonical instrumental of the word and the wrong one for this line; the exercise is set to show that both exist.

      4. vuḍḍhānaṁ bhikkhūnaṁ pāde (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 196): genitive plural, genitive plural, accusative plural. The adjective vuḍḍhānaṁ agrees with its noun in case and number, so it looks like a second genitive plural and not like an adjective; and the whole phrase, three words, is what English needs six for.

      If (a) and (b) came out and (c) and (d) did not, that is the expected result, not a failure: a phrase of one word can be produced long before a phrase with agreement in it. Come back to (d) on day 30.
