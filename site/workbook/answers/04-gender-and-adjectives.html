    Answers: Gender and Adjectives

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Dīghā jāgarato ratti, dīghaṁ santassa yojanaṁ; Dīgho bālānaṁ saṁsāro, saddhammaṁ avijānataṁ. (Dhp 60 — Dhp p. 9)

      The three forms in order are dīghā, dīghaṁ, dīgho: feminine with ratti, neuter with yojanaṁ, masculine with saṁsāro. The stem dīgha is the same in all three, and only the ending moves. This is the chapter’s whole claim in one verse, and a good line to have by heart for that reason as much as for its sense.

      Two smaller features of the verse. Jāgarato and avijānataṁ are present participles in -(n)t, genitive singular and genitive plural, the row of the feminine table whose strong stem gives gacchantī. Here they are masculine and so decline like bhagavant, on the weak stem: jāgarat-, avijānat-. And each of the three long things is long for somebody, in the genitive: jāgarato, santassa, bālānaṁ. The pattern is exact, and stage 1 asks mainly that you hear it.

      The three printed editions read bālānaṁ here, and the Mahāsaṅgīti prints bālāna. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society all keep the niggahīta, so it is what stands above. The short form is this same genitive plural with its final nasal dropped for the metre, a form altered to fit the line, which the metre chapter accounts for under metre as the cause and keeps apart from the three cases where the spelling and the sound have merely come apart. It is not a different case and the sense does not depend on it. But a search for the genitive plural under its usual spelling will miss it.

    2. Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, bālo abyatto akusalo sūdo … (SN 47.8 — S v 149)

      Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, paṇḍito byatto kusalo sūdo … (SN 47.8 — S v 151)

      Six adjectives, one noun, and all six masculine nominative singular in -o, because sūdo is masculine nominative singular. Two of the three pairs are made with the privative a- (byatto / abyatto, kusalo / akusalo), and the third, paṇḍito / bālo, is two separate words. The sentence teaches both facts at once: Pāli negates a quality freely with a prefix, and then keeps a lexical opposite anyway where it has one.

      The second and the fifth are past participles. DPD parses byatta as the past participle of byañjayati, literally ‘distinguished’, and abyatta as the same with the negative in front. Bāla, paṇḍita and kusala are plain adjectives with no verb behind them. The surface does not show it (all six end in -o and all six agree the same way), and that is the point: a participle declines like an adjective and agrees like an adjective, and its history shows only in the dictionary. The same observation returns at item 20, on nissaggiya and nissaṭṭha, and at item 18 on abhikkantataro.

      Two points about the sentence itself. The two versions stand four sections apart in one sutta, which is how a great deal of the canon is built: a simile stated in the negative, then the same words in the positive. And nānaccayehi sūpehi is an ordinary adjective-plus-noun agreement in the instrumental plural, in the middle of a sentence about nominatives.

    3. Tena kho pana samayena sukkā bhikkhunī mahatiyā parisāya parivutā dhammaṁ deseti. (SN 10.9 — S i 212)

      Mahatiyā and parisāya are the two that are not nominative. They are instrumental singular, as shown by their ending -āya, which the ā-declension and the feminine ī-declension use for the oblique cases and never for the nominative. A feminine nominative singular would be parisā. The other three (sukkā, bhikkhunī, parivutā) are nominative, and between them they carry the two feminine declensions the chapter’s table produces: sukkā an ā-stem, bhikkhunī an ī-stem from the -u row.

      Parivutā is nominative and not instrumental because it describes the nun, not the assembly: she is surrounded. The instrumental mahatiyā parisāya is what she is surrounded by. The two stand next to each other and the agreement reaches past them, the point item 20 makes at greater length: agreement in Pāli is not a matter of adjacency, and pairing each word with its neighbour gets this sentence backwards.

      Mahatiyā is the feminine of mahant, the last row of the feminine table, and it is the same form as item 16 below. Meeting it in a sentence first and parsing it cold afterwards is the order this workbook is arranged in.

    4. Yo pana bhikkhu … (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 195) and Yā pana bhikkhunī … (Bi Np 1 — Vin iv 243)

      The two words that change are yo → yā and bhikkhu → bhikkhunī. The first is the relative pronoun taking the feminine; the second is the -u row of the feminine table, bhikkhu → bhikkhunī, in the sentence that put it into the language.

      The four words that close both rules did not have to move because they do not agree with the monk or the nun. Nissaggiyaṁ pācittiyaṁ is a separate verbless clause stating the penalty, and it agrees within itself: nissaggiya is a gerundive, ‘to be relinquished’, and it takes the neuter of pācittiyaṁ. Whose offence it is has nothing to do with what gender the offence is. This is item 20’s lesson from the other side: there, four words that had to agree; here, two that did not.

      The offences themselves differ, on purpose: a surplus robe for the monks, a stash of bowls for the nuns. The two codes are two codes on one frame, not the same rules with the genders switched, and so the frame shows clearly when they are read side by side.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Tāvadeva chāyā metabbā, utuppamāṇaṁ ācikkhitabbaṁ, divasabhāgo ācikkhitabbo, saṅgīti ācikkhitabbā, cattāro nissayā ācikkhitabbā. (Kd 1 — Vin i 95)

      The four shapes of ācikkhitabba are neuter singular ācikkhitabbaṁ with utuppamāṇaṁ, masculine singular ācikkhitabbo with divasabhāgo, feminine singular ācikkhitabbā with saṅgīti, and masculine plural ācikkhitabbā with cattāro nissayā. If rebuilding from the gloss produced all four, the chapter has done its work; if it produced ācikkhitabbaṁ four times, the exercise has found what it was built to find.

      The two spelled alike are the third and the fourth. Ācikkhitabbā itself cannot decide between feminine nominative singular and masculine nominative plural; the two endings are simply the same. Saṅgīti on one side and cattāro nissayā on the other decide it, and both of those are unambiguous: saṅgīti is a feminine i-stem, and cattāro is a masculine plural numeral. So the noun settles the adjective here, having been settled by the adjective two clauses earlier: the chapter’s remark that agreement runs in both directions, visible inside a single sentence.

      Metabbā at the front is the same construction on a different verb, feminine singular with chāyā. Five gerundives, five nouns, and not one of the gerundives could have been guessed from the verb alone.

    2. Sahassamapi ce vācā, anatthapadasaṁhitā; Ekaṁ atthapadaṁ seyyo, yaṁ sutvā upasammati. (Dhp 100 — Dhp p. 15)

      According to the chapter’s table of irregulars, the positive of seyyo is sant ‘good’, and its superlative is seṭṭha. Item 22 goes on to complicate that table; recite it in this form first.

      Two features of the verse to notice while it is being rebuilt. Anatthapadasaṁhitā agrees with vācā, feminine nominative plural: a whole compound serving as one adjective, the chapter on compounds arriving early. DPD carries atthapada ‘a beneficial saying’ as a compound of attha and pada, and the verse puts saṁhitā ‘connected with’ on the end of it and the privative an- on the front. And seyyo stands beside ekaṁ atthapadaṁ, neuter nominative singular.

      What seyyo itself is remains open, and the verse does not settle it. DPD registers it as an indeclinable, ‘better, it is better’, and the chapter’s footnote records that these comparatives were historically -yas-stems, which would make seyyo a neuter nominative singular of the same form as mano, agreeing with atthapadaṁ. The two accounts give the same rendering, and the second explains why the form looks as it does. No choice is forced here, and it is better to know that than to have picked one.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Give the feminine.

    1. dāsa → dāsī ‘female servant’. A noun in -a takes -ī, which replaces the stem vowel. The result declines as a feminine ī-stem: dāsiyā, dāsiyo.

    2. dīgha → dīghā ‘long’. An adjective in -a takes -ā, and the result declines as an ordinary feminine ā-stem like kaññā. This row is the most easily missed, and the contrast with 7 explains it: the same stem ending, two different suffixes, sorted by whether the word is a noun or an adjective. Item 1 is this row in a verse — dīghā ratti.

    3. upāsaka → upāsikā ‘laywoman’. Stems in -aka change -aka to -ikā. Compare sāvaka → sāvikā, kumāraka → kumārikā. In practice this is the -a → -ā rule with a vowel change in front of it, but hold it separately, because -aka is such a productive suffix.

    4. hatthin → hatthinī ‘she-elephant’. Stems in -in add -ī, giving -inī. Compare musāvādin → musāvādinī ‘a woman who lies’.

    5. bhikkhu → bhikkhunī ‘nun’. Stems in -u and -ū add -nī. Compare abhijjhālū → abhijjhālunī ‘covetous’. Item 4 is this row carrying an entire second code of monastic law.

    6. mahant → mahatī ‘great’. An adjective in -(n)t adds -ī to the weak stem, the one without the nasal: mahat- gives mahatī, sīlavant gives sīlavatī. A present participle in -(n)t adds -ī to the strong stem instead: gacchant- gives gacchantī ‘going’. The same ending attaches to different stems according to the word’s function, the only complication in this table, and it is a real distinction, not a spelling variant.

    What does the adjective tell you about its noun?

    1. kusalā — three readings, in two genders. Masculine nominative plural (the noun is masculine, plural, and the subject); feminine nominative singular (the noun is feminine and singular); or neuter nominative or accusative plural, since the a-stem neuter has a rare plural in -ā beside -āni. So the ending narrows the noun to “not masculine singular, not any oblique case” and no further. The verb usually settles the number, and the noun itself the gender.

    2. dukkhaṁ — neuter nominative or accusative singular, or masculine accusative singular. Note what this excludes: it cannot be feminine, and it cannot be plural or oblique. If the sentence has hoti or no verb at all, a neuter nominative is likeliest and the phrase is a predication — ‘X is suffering’. If there is a transitive verb with another subject, it is an accusative.

    3. sīlavato — masculine or neuter, genitive or dative singular, built on the weak stem sīlavat-. Two points follow. The noun is singular and is being possessed, or benefited, or is the person about whom something is said. And it is not feminine: a feminine would be sīlavatiyā. Item 1 has two forms of this kind, jāgarato and santassa, with the second of those functions.

    4. mahatiyā — feminine singular, and then the same five-way ambiguity every feminine ī-stem oblique has: instrumental, ablative, genitive, dative or locative. The adjective has given the gender and the number but not the case. Compare 15, where the -(n)t stem in the masculine narrows the case to two: the feminine of this class, having gone over to the ī-declension, inherits that declension’s ambiguity along with its endings. In item 3 it is the noun parisāya and the verb that settle it as instrumental.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Read the line.

    1. Maggānaṭṭhaṅgiko seṭṭho, saccānaṁ caturo padā. (Dhp 273 — Dhp p. 40)

      maggānaṁof pathsm gen plaṭṭhaṅgikothe eightfoldm nom sgseṭṭhobestm nom sgsaccānaṁof truthsn gen plcaturofourpadāsayings, statementsn nom pl

      “Of paths the eightfold is best; of truths, the four sayings.”

      Learn seṭṭho as vocabulary; do not derive it: it is irregular, paired with the comparative seyya, and it is not formed from any adjective it resembles. It agrees with aṭṭhaṅgiko, masculine nominative singular, which is itself an adjective (‘having eight factors’) standing as a noun: the chapter’s point about the thin line between the two.

      The construction of the comparison is not what a reader schooled on English would expect. There is no word for ‘than’. The field within which something is best goes into the genitive plural (maggānaṁ, ‘among paths’), and the superlative simply agrees with its own noun. The full verse does it four times over: dhammānaṁ, dvipadānaṁ.

      Two smaller points. Padā is a neuter nominative plural in -ā, the alternative to padāni that Appendix A.1 records for this class; DPD cites this very line under pada in the sense ‘word, phrase, saying’. And caturo is the form the numeral chapter gives as masculine nominative and accusative, standing here with a neuter noun. Verse does not always hold strictly to gender agreement, and this looks like an instance; it is hard to be certain, and the sense does not depend on it.

    2. Atthi kho, bhikkhu, sakko nāma devānamindo amhehi abhikkantataro ca paṇītataro ca. (DN 11 — D i 216)

      atthithere iskhoindeedbhikkhumendicantm voc sgsakkoSakkam nom sgnāmaby namedevānaṁof the godsm gen plindolordm nom sgamhehithan usinst./abl. plabhikkantataromore excellentm nom sgcaandpaṇītataromore sublimem nom sgcaand

      “There is, mendicant, Sakka, lord of gods by name, more excellent and more sublime than we are.”

      Two comparatives in -tara, both masculine nominative singular, agreeing with sakko. Both are formed the ordinary productive way, and abhikkantataro is the type the chapter noted as built on a past participle: abhikkanta ‘surpassing, excellent’ is itself the past participle of abhikkamati. It is the observation item 2 made of byatto, one step further along: there a participle standing as an adjective, here a participle standing as an adjective and then being compared.

      Here the standard of comparison is expressed, by amhehi, and that is the case-syntax question the chapter deferred. The form is ambiguous: instrumental and ablative plural fell together in Pāli and share one ending, so amhehi is either. On the ablative reading it is ‘from us’, the ordinary Indo-European construction for comparison, and that is the likelier account; but the form itself does not decide, and this is a good instance of a place where morphology hands the question to syntax and stops.

    3. Cakkhuṁ, bhikkhave, dukkhaṁ. … jivhā dukkhā … kāyo dukkho … mano dukkho. (SN 35.2 — S iv 2)

      cakkhuṁthe eyen nom sgbhikkhavemendicantsm voc pldukkhaṁsufferingn nom sgjivhāthe tonguef nom sgdukkhāf nom sgkāyothe bodym nom sgdukkhom nom sgmanothe mindnom sgdukkhom nom sg

      “The eye, mendicants, is suffering … the tongue is suffering … the body is suffering … the mind is suffering.”

      One adjective, dukkha, taking three genders from three nouns without any change to its stem. This is agreement at its plainest, and the sentence proves the chapter’s claim that the adjective has no gender of its own: the same four letters are neuter, feminine and masculine in one paragraph. Item 1 does the same thing in verse and in one line; this does it in prose and across a whole sutta opening.

      The last clause is the interesting one. Mano is an -as stem, historically neuter, and a neuter would take dukkhaṁ. It takes dukkho, masculine. That is the migration described in the chapter on nouns, in progress: a word whose nominative singular looks like a masculine a-stem nominative has attracted masculine agreement, and the canon itself supplies the evidence. Told flatly that the -as stems are neuter, you would have to call this an error. It is the language moving, not an error.

    Agreement across a long sentence.

    1. idaṁ this (nt. nom. sg.) · cīvaraṁ robe-cloth (nt. nom. sg.) · itthannāmassa of such-and-such a name (m. gen. sg.) · bhikkhuno of a monk (m. gen. sg.) · nissaggiyaṁ to be relinquished (nt. nom. sg.) · āyasmantānaṁ to the venerables (m. dat. pl.) · nissaṭṭhaṁ has been relinquished (nt. nom. sg., p.p.)

      “This robe-cloth, to be relinquished by the monk so-and-so, has been relinquished to the venerables.”

      The four that agree are idaṁ, cīvaraṁ, nissaggiyaṁ and nissaṭṭhaṁ, all neuter nominative singular. Two of them are not adjectives to begin with: nissaggiya is a gerundive and nissaṭṭha a past participle, and both decline like adjectives and agree like adjectives, so they can sit at either end of the sentence and still be found.

      The two that do not agree are itthannāmassa bhikkhuno and āyasmantānaṁ, because they do not describe the cloth. The first is a genitive naming whose the offence was, the second a dative naming who now receives; both are masculine, and one is plural. They stand between the cloth and the participle that describes it without disturbing the agreement, and that is the point to notice: agreement in Pāli is not a matter of adjacency.

      Cīvara, which is neuter, fixes the gender of the four. No other word in the sentence could: bhikkhu and āyasmant are both masculine, and if agreement worked by proximity the last word would have followed them.

    Put it back into Pāli.

      1. Dīghā … ratti (Dhp 60 — Dhp p. 9): dīghā feminine nominative singular, agreeing with ratti, a feminine i-stem nominative singular. The trap is dīghaṁ, which is the form the verse uses one pāda later and with a different noun.

      2. mahatiyā parisāya parivutā (SN 10.9 — S i 212): mahatiyā and parisāya feminine instrumental singular, parivutā feminine nominative singular. Three feminines and two cases, and the case boundary falls where the sense does.

      3. saṅgīti ācikkhitabbā (Kd 1 — Vin i 95): both feminine nominative singular. If this came out as ācikkhitabbaṁ, the neuter has been taken as a default, which it is not.

      4. Yā pana bhikkhunī pattasannicayaṁ kareyya (Bi Np 1 — Vin iv 243): yā and bhikkhunī feminine nominative singular, pattasannicayaṁ masculine accusative singular, kareyya third person singular optative. Note that the object is masculine and agrees with no other word; only the two feminines are in step.

      Producing these is a different skill from parsing them, and the failure it exposes is a particular one: the masculine and the neuter come to hand and the feminine does not. If (c) and (d) were the hard ones, the table in 7–12 is recitable and not yet usable, and day 30 is where that gets fixed.

    Where the ending is not the word you think it is.

    1. They are two different words that have fallen together in this case, and the table’s sant is not the word in the verse.

      DPD carries both. The sant of the irregular table is the present participle of atthi ‘to be’ (sa + nta, from √as), which as a masculine noun means ‘a good person, a virtuous man’ and as an adjective ‘true, real’. The santa of dīghaṁ santassa yojanaṁ is the past participle of sammati, √sam + ta, literally ‘calmed’ and in this sense ‘tired, wearied’. DPD gives Dhp 60 itself as its illustration of that sense. Two roots, two formations, one genitive singular.

      And the other word is in the same verse. DPD builds saddhamma ‘the true teaching’ out of sat and dhamma, and that sat is the participle of atthi: the table’s word, in a compound, one pāda below the santassa that is not it. Dhp 60 carries both, three words apart, and prints them so differently that nobody would look twice.

      Had the table’s word been read into the verse, the line would have come out as “long is the league for the good man”, which is grammatical, scans, and is wrong. That is the nature of the error: a form parsed correctly and assigned to the wrong word, not a form the reader cannot parse. The ending will never warn you. Only the dictionary does, and only the sense not quite fitting sends you to the dictionary.

      A note for anyone who looks seyya up while doing this. The chapter’s table pairs sant → seyya → seṭṭha, which is the traditional grammarians’ arrangement and records which positive the comparative does duty for. DPD’s entry instead derives seyya from sirī ‘glory, fortune’, literally ‘luckier’, matching Sanskrit śreyas. These answer different questions. The table is about a suppletion in the living language (as English pairs good with better, which is not built from it either), and the dictionary is about where the form came from. Both can be held at once, and noticing the difference means reading the table correctly, not doubting it.
