    Answers: Review — The Sentence and the Verse

    Sotānugata — met by ear.

    1. Nisinno is the odd one. Tiṭṭhaṁ, caraṁ and sayāno are present participles (the first two active, the third middle), and nisinno is a past participle. The difference of kind is a difference of aspect and not of time: standing, walking and lying down are postures being held, while sitting down is an act completed, and Pāli says “having sat” where English says “sitting”. Render all four alike and nothing of the sense is lost, but the reason the fourth word looks unlike the other three is lost entirely.

      What all four have in common is that not one of them is the subject of anything. There is no finite verb in this half of the verse at all. All four are masculine nominative singulars, and they wait for a clause that has not arrived yet.

      It arrives in the other half: Etaṁ satiṁ adhiṭṭheyya, “he should undertake this mindfulness”. The four participles are adverbial to that optative, and yāvatā assa vitamiddho is the further condition on it. So the whole verse is one clause with four alternative circumstances stacked in front of it, and the stacking costs four words. English cannot do it that way: “whether standing, walking, seated or lying down” needs a subordinating conjunction and a copula that Pāli simply does without, because a nominative participle agreeing with the subject already says “while being thus”.

      That is the method of this whole stretch in its plainest form. A participle is a clause that has given up its verb, and what it gives up in exchange is the need for anything to join it on.

    2. The three compounds are of three different classes, and the line is set for that reason. Tirokuṭṭesu is an avyayībhāva, a compound governed by an indeclinable, and it is inflected here as a locative plural instead of standing frozen in the usual -aṁ. That is useful to see: the class is defined by its first member and not by its ending. Sandhisiṅghāṭakesu is a dvanda, junctions and crossroads. Dvārabāhāsu is a genitive tappurisa, the posts of a door.

      All three are locative plural, and the job is the plainest the case has: place where. Three compounds, three classes, one case, and the case does what English would need a preposition for three times over, without ever being repeated.

      Āgantvāna is an absolutive, “having come”, and the ordinary form is āgantvā. The longer -tvāna is the same absolutive with a syllable more, and here it fills out a line of verse. At this point the absolutives chapter and the metre chapter have to be held together: a form that looks like a dialect variant or a scribal fullness is very often a metrical choice, and the way to find out is to scan the line and see whether the shorter form would fit. Here it would not.

    3. Everything after kassapo is in the nominative, and the relation is apposition: each epithet renames the same man and depends on nothing. Apposition holds a verbless sentence together: ayaṁ pūraṇo kassapo is the subject and every word that follows is another way of saying it, so no verb is needed and none is supplied. The courtier is piling up names for one person, and the case shows that they all belong to him.

      The epithets sort into three kinds. Past participles: ñāto, sādhusammato, cirapabbajito, addhagato, vayoanuppatto, five of them, and every one of the last three is a compound with a participle as its second member, so it belongs to two chapters at once. Possessive suffixes: saṅghī, gaṇī, yasassī, rattaññū, words meaning “having a community”, “having a following”, “having renown”, and the suffix is the word-formation chapter’s business. Compounds proper: gaṇācariyo, titthakaro, and the compound halves of the participial epithets.

      Bahujanassa is the word to get right, and it is a genitive. It looks like a possessive, “of many people”, and a reader will attach it to whatever noun is nearest. In fact it is attached to no noun: it is the agent of the past participle sādhusammato, “held in high regard by many people”. The case syntax chapter treats that construction. Pāli uses a genitive where an instrumental would seem the obvious choice because a past participle is not quite a passive verb, and it takes the agent of a noun, not the agent of a verb.

      So one sentence of flattery needs four chapters and has no verb in it, and that is a fair description of what reading canonical prose is actually like.

    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud.

    1. Jarāmaraṇaṁ is a dvanda, “old age and death”, and sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā is a dvanda too, of five members, “sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and distress”. A dvanda makes the list one thing, as a string of separate words would not. Jarāmaraṇaṁ is singular and neuter: old age and death are a single condition with two names here, and the ending says so.

      Jātipaccayā is an ablative singular, and the job is cause: birth being the condition, this comes to be. Spelt out, the relation is jātiyā paccayā, “from the condition that is birth”, and compounding it removes the second ending and joins the condition to what conditions it. Twelve links, each stated in two words, make the formula recitable, and this matters for more than its sound: the chain has to be held in the memory forwards and backwards, and the compound makes each link short enough to be a single hand-hold.

      Note also that sambhavanti is plural where jarāmaraṇaṁ is singular. The verb is agreeing with the five-member dvanda, not with the two-member one, which is the ordinary Pāli habit when a verb has two subjects of different number: it takes the nearer, or the larger, and here it is both.

    2. “Not doing any evil, undertaking the wholesome, cleansing one’s own mind: this is the teaching of the Buddhas.” Read it aloud, holding the long vowels of -pā-, -pa-dā and -sā- and the closed syllables -raṇaṁ, -sam- and -naṁ to their full weight, and sounding the doubled consonants of sabbapāpassa as two each, not one. Note that pariyodapanaṁ, for all its length, has not a single double consonant in it: the third pāda is one whole word, nine syllables of it, and six of the nine are light — ⏑ — ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ — ⏑ ⏑ ×. Read aloud beside the heavy opening of the fourth, it is the most audible thing in the verse.

      Akaraṇaṁ is the negative prefix a- (a particle, not an upasagga, in the tradition’s terms, and treated in Chapter 14) on the action-noun suffix -ana, built on the root kar- ‘to do’. Literally ‘not-doing’, the same -ana type as dassana and ṭhāna. Sacittapariyodapanaṁ is a genitive tappurisa. Chapter 13 advises trying an unfamiliar tappurisa as a genitive first, and this one answers to it: sacitta ‘one’s own mind’ stands to pariyodapana as dukkha stands to samudaya in dukkhasamudayo, ‘the origin of suffering’. So: ‘the cleansing of one’s own mind’, with the same -ana action-noun suffix on the final member as akaraṇaṁ carries.

      The last word’s full form is buddhānaṁ, the genitive plural taught in Chapter 3 and displayed in Appendix A.1: it had to keep its case ending as far as the grammar goes, since nothing else in the sentence marks whose teaching this is, and it had to lose its niggahīta as far as the metre goes, since the Siloka’s even-line cadence has no room in its final four syllables for the extra weight a closed -naṁ would carry. This verse closed a question in the second review of this book, chapters ago, as a plain sentence to be read and translated. It took the whole of Chapter 18 to say why its last four syllables scan the way they do, and the fact needed to say so had been in the declension table of this book’s third chapter all along.

    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind.

    Recall.

    1. A transitive verb’s past participle is passive; an intransitive verb’s is active. Desita ‘taught’ and kata ‘done’ describe what was done to something; gata ‘gone’ and bhūta ‘been’ describe what the subject itself did, and there is no patient for a passive reading to act on. English uses the same -en/-ed form for both (“a broken pot” is passive, “a fallen leaf” is active, not “a leaf that fell upon”), so the rule has to be stated and cannot be left to be noticed.

    2. Ordinarily the absolutive’s agent is the main verb’s subject, and it stops being so the moment the main clause’s own subject cannot plausibly have performed the prior action, typically because that subject is an abstract noun and the sentence reports a consequence, not a sequence. The commentators’ name for it is asamānakattuka, ‘having a different agent’, and Dhammapāla’s gloss is sharper than any Western rendering: the construction marks two actions occurring at the same time, one the mark of the other, and so it can carry both a causal sense (‘because he saw’) and a temporal one at once.

    3. Samuccaya (joining), vikappana (separating), paṭisedha (denying and forbidding), and pūraṇattha (filling). The fourth changes how a page is read, because it names a class of word (kho, hi, vata, sudaṁ, khalu) that carries no translatable meaning at all. Calling kho “emphatic” says what it feels like; calling it a filler says what it is for, and permits the only sound response to it: leave it untranslated and watch its effect on the movement of the sentence instead.

    4. Ekapadatta, ‘the fact of being one word’, and ekavibhattitā, ‘the fact of having one inflection’: only the last member of a compound takes an ending. The second catches beginners out, because most compounds encode a case relation, and the ending that would state it is not written anywhere. Dukkhakkhandha means ‘mass of suffering’, but the genitive that says so has to be recovered, not read off the word.

    5. It strengthens the first vowel of the whole word, not the vowel beside the suffix: a becomes ā, i becomes e, u becomes o. That fact decides where a dictionary entry sits: nipuṇo ‘skilful’ gives nepuññaṁ ‘skill’, and a reader looking for it under ni- will not find it. The word is filed under ne-, because the dictionary is alphabetised on the written form and the written form has moved.

    6. The genitive and the vocative. The tradition’s line is na chaṭṭhīvihitattho kārakaṁ, yathā mantanaṁ: “what is expressed by the sixth case is not a factor of action, nor is address.” A genitive relates one noun to another and reaches the verb only at second hand, if at all; a vocative has no connection to the action at the moment it is uttered, since calling someone makes them available to be told to go or to eat, and only then can the action begin.

    7. When a run of actions shares a subject, every verb but the last goes into the absolutive. It stops working the moment the prior action belongs to somebody else, for the absolutive carries no person, number or case of its own and so cannot say whose action it is. Pāli then reaches for the absolute construction, which has a subject of its own because the absolutive cannot supply one.

    8. A relative pronoun takes its number and gender from the antecedent, and its case from its own clause. Number and gender are agreement: they say what the relative is pointing at. Case is function: it says what the relative does inside the clause it opens. The two halves answer different questions, so they can pull in different directions in the same short sentence and both still be correct.

    9. A syllable with e or o counts heavy whether it is open or closed, because the two vowels are long by origin. Before a conjunct they are pronounced short (upekkhā’s e is shorter than upekhā’s), but the syllable is heavy either way, once for the vowel and once for the conjunct if there is one. There is no position in which e or o is light by rule, and every other vowel in the language differs: a, i and u are light in an open syllable and heavy only when closed, and ā, ī and ū are simply long and heavy throughout.

    10. Kita: suffixes added directly to a root. Participles, absolutives and infinitives are all kita formations, so the chapter on word formation can say that three chapters of this book have been teaching kita suffixes without using the name. What unites them is that each lets a single non-finite word carry an action or a state without inflecting for person at all (a participle agreeing like an adjective, an absolutive and an infinitive not inflecting even for that), and that is how a long Pāli sentence can run on one finite verb.

    11. It is any compound of any of the preceding classes used as an adjective describing something outside itself, and no separate kind of compound at all. That is Collins’s formula, and Bodhi’s and Thānuttamo’s independently. The suffix with the same structural function, turning a finished unit into a word that describes something else, is -ika (or its variant -iya) added to a whole compound, as in anuloma ‘with the hair’ giving anulomika ‘in accordance with’. And it is treated in the chapter on compounds, not the chapter on word formation, because it operates on a finished compound and not on a bare stem. It is the only piece of suffix machinery this book files by what it attaches to, not by which chapter usually owns suffixes.

    12. The genitive is outside the system because it “relates one noun to another and reaches the verb only at second hand, if at all”: it is read against the noun beside it, not against the verb. The vocative is outside it because at the moment of address there is no action yet for it to be connected to. An absolute construction, by contrast, is read against a verb of its own, its own participle, and is named after its case for a related reason: only the case marks the construction off. Strip the case and nothing is left to say a locative absolute is a clause and not an ordinary phrase.

    13. Resolution replaces one heavy syllable with two light ones, and it is common, particularly at the start of a line. Replacement replaces two light syllables with one heavy, and it is much rarer, occurring only after a word break. Both obey the same constraint in mirror image: resolution operates across the first two syllables of a word, and replacement across the first two syllables of a word standing right after a break, which shows that the two light syllables being replaced were themselves the opening of a word.

    Say what the form is, and what decides it.

    1. Disvā: an absolutive, ‘having seen’, and the question is whose seeing it is. The main clause’s subject is āsavā, ‘the defilements’, and defilements do not see with wisdom; the seer is the monk, present in the sentence only as assa. This is the asamānakattuka construction of item 7. The main clause’s subject cannot plausibly have performed the action, and that forces the absolutive’s agent outward, onto whoever is grammatically present only in an oblique case. Dhammapāla’s commentary calls this reading causal, ‘because of seeing’, and not merely sequential.

    2. Sati: the locative singular of the present participle of atthi, ‘there being’, standing beside the plural noun pādesu and not agreeing with it in number. This is Chapter 16’s one standing exception to ordinary participle agreement: sati is the workhorse of the whole absolute construction and is used in the singular “even when the corresponding locative noun is plural,” as Bodhi puts it of the same word beside saṅkhāresu. Nothing settles it; a reader has to know the idiom, not derive it, and the Digital Pali Dictionary’s two separate entries, one locative singular and one locative plural, record the same fact from the lexicon’s side.

    3. Santaṁ: the accusative singular present participle of atthi, and the question is whether it is an ordinary participle agreeing with an accusative noun or an accusative absolute in its own right. Here saṅkappeti already has its object: the quoted thought, ‘natthi paro loko’ ti. Since the verb’s object slot is taken, santaṁ yeva … paraṁ lokaṁ cannot also be filling it, and the test Chapter 16 gives for this construction generally decides it: where the accusative could be the main verb’s own object, it probably is; where it could not, it is absolute. Here it could not, so the reading is ‘although the other world does exist, he forms the thought that there is no other world’, the concessive sense the commentary’s gloss of the parallel formula, bhummatthe upayogavacanaṁ, ‘an accusative in the sense of the locative’, supports.

    4. Taṇhāya: a feminine ā-stem ending that could be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locative. Chapter 15‘s stated method settles it in two steps. First, the verb: jāyati ’is born’ takes an ablative of source, as tasati takes a genitive and saṁvattati a dative. Second, the parallel: this line is the fifth in a run of five near-identical verses in the same chapter of the Dhammapada, and three of the other four carry an ending that can only be ablative: piyato, pemato and kāmato, all in -to. Framed by three unambiguous ablatives, the two ambiguous forms (ratiyā and taṇhāya) are read as ablative too.

    5. Bhuttāvissa: a past active participle in -āvin, genitive singular, standing in a genitive absolute. This is doubly exceptional: Wijesekera’s stated rule restricts the absolute construction to a present participle or a past passive one, ruling out both the future passive participle and the past active one. Yet here a past active participle stands in the genitive, taking its own object (bhojanaṁ) and marking a state (having eaten) that belongs to someone other than hoti’s subject, bhattasammado, ‘drowsiness after a meal’. The case is genitive, not instrumental or dative, because the participle has its own object and its own logical subject, as a genitive absolute does. It is rare (thirteen root-text occurrences), and Thānuttamo had to go looking for it, so it is stated here and not merely noted.

    6. Yena has two different functions, and in each sentence what governs it decides which. In SN 1.1 — S i 1, yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami, it is the instrumental of ya(d)- in the fixed idiom “yena X, tena Y”, ‘to where X is, there Y’, and it is governed by a verb of motion, upasaṅkami. Chapter 15 files the whole idiom under the instrumental, not as a relative clause proper. In AN 4.80 — A ii 82, yena mātugāmo … na kammantaṁ payojeti, there is no verb of motion at all; yena follows the nouns hetu and paccayo, ‘cause’ and ‘condition’, and works as a connective, ‘whereby, in that, such that’: Wijesekera’s term, and the function the empty accusative yaṁ has elsewhere. The test is whether a verb of motion governs it or it attaches a clause to a noun asking for a reason.

    7. Every grammar in the collection but one treats yato and yasmā as interchangeable synonyms for ‘because’. Warder alone calls yasmā “a rarely used synonym of yato”, and the corpus shows he is right about the canon specifically: root-text yato stands 1,370 times against yasmā’s 374. But the ratio reverses, and reverses harder, outside it — commentaries 3,317 against 590, sub-commentaries 1,605 against 327. Yasmā … tasmā is the exegetes’ connective; the canon, which argues less and narrates more, reaches for yato. Which a reader should expect depends on the layer of the text, and a grammar written mainly from commentarial Pāli will teach yasmā as ordinary and will be describing accurately the language it read.

    8. Ye te is one unit, not two levels: a relative phrase, masculine nominative plural, ‘those who are the Vajjian elders’. What makes the sentence hard is what is around it. There are two relative clauses here, one inside the other, and their answering words are of different kinds.

      The outer clause is opened by yāvakīvañca, ‘and so long as’, and it is answered by nothing at all. No tāva arrives; the main clause simply states what is to be expected, vuddhiyeva … pāṭikaṅkhā. The inner clause is ye te vajjīnaṁ vajjimahallakā, and it is answered, by the te that follows, accusative plural this time, the object of the four verbs sakkarissanti garuṁ karissanti mānessanti pūjessanti. So one pair is complete and the other is not, in the same sentence.

      A correlative can be answered by silence, and that decides it. Look only for yāva … tāva and you will hunt for the missing tāva, find the nominative ye te and the accusative te instead, and conclude the sentence is broken. It is not: the inner pair behaves as it should, and the outer one uses the language’s freedom to leave the answering word out.

    9. Both are the quotation particle ti, with two different functions, only one of which is quoting. The first, after ruppati, is hetu, causal, and it is the first of the seven senses Aggavaṁsa distinguishes for iti over and above the ordinary quotation account: it attaches to the clause and makes it the reason for what follows, and it is answered by tasmā, ‘therefore’: ‘because it is deformed’. The second, after rūpaṁ, is the ordinary naming sense that closes an act of calling something something: ‘it is therefore called “form”’. The tasmā directly after the first tells them apart: only a causal iti-clause is answered by a ‘therefore’, and the ordinary quotation-naming use never is.

    Take the compound or the word apart.

    1. Sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā is five members (soka ‘sorrow’, parideva ‘lamentation’, dukkha ‘pain’, domanassa ‘dejection’, upāyāsa ‘distress’), of which only the last, -upāyāsā, is inflected: masculine nominative plural, agreeing with sambhavanti alongside jarāmaraṇaṁ. Four of the five members cannot be found independently in that form in the root texts; when they stand free the canon has soko, paridevo, domanassaṁ, upāyāso. Dukkha is the exception, and an instructive one: it stands free three hundred times, because it is an adjective and a neuter noun as well as a member of this compound, and its bare stem is therefore a real word in its own right. The other four are stems used inside a compound and nowhere else. Jātipaccayā is not part of the compound at all; it is the ablative of cause Chapter 15 gives for the whole formula of dependent origination, paccayā ‘as condition’. The same sentence is quoted in both the compounds chapter and the case-syntax chapter, for a different purpose in each: one reads the long compound, the other reads the ablative in front of it.

    2. Read from the right, as the compounds chapter’s method requires. Ñāṇāya is the outer layer: pubbenivāsānussati + ñāṇa, a genitive tappurisa, ‘knowledge of recollection’, in the dative of purpose (cittaṁ abhininnāmeti, ‘he directs the mind towards’). Pubbenivāsānussati is the middle layer: pubbenivāsa + anussati, another genitive tappurisa, ‘recollection of the former abode’. Pubbenivāsa is the innermost layer, and the one to pause on: pubbe + nivāsa, an alutta compound. The first member keeps the locative ending it would have had as a separate word, pubbe ‘in the past’, and does not reduce to the bare stem pubba-. That is Chapter 13’s exception to the rule that only the last member of a compound is inflected, sitting on top of the ordinary locative sense (dwelling in the past) that Chapter 15 gives that case.

    3. Both are masculine nominative singular in -vā and both decline as the (m/v)ant-stems of Appendix A.1, but they are built two different ways. Vusitavā is Chapter 10‘s past active participle: -vant added to an already-formed past participle, vusita ’lived’. Sīlavā is Chapter 14‘s ordinary possessive: -vant added directly to a noun, sīla ’virtue’. The base decides which is which: is the element in front of -vant itself a verbal past-participle shape (a root already carrying -ta or -na, an event already completed) or a plain noun naming a quality? Vusita is a pp.; sīla is not.

    4. Both end in nominative -iko, and the -ika has a different function in each. Ehipassiko is Chapter 13‘s closing formation: -ika added to a finished compound (here an unusual one, two second-person imperatives, ehi ’come!’ plus passa ‘see!’) to make it an adjective, ‘a come-and-see thing’. That whole operation is filed in the chapter on compounds, not the chapter on word formation, on the explicit ground that it works on a compound already assembled and not on a bare stem. Dhammiko is Chapter 14‘s ordinary taddhita suffix, -ika attached directly to the noun dhamma, in one of the sixteen senses Thānuttamo catalogues for it after Kaccāyana, here ’righteous’. So the same three letters mean two different operations depending on what stands in front of them, and the more surprising of the two locations is where the compound-final use is taught.

    5. Four operations, in order. (1) A compound base, Chapter 13‘s material: bahu ’much’ + suta ‘heard’, the same shape as bahussuta ‘much-learned’. (2) Chapter 14’s taddhita suffix -ya, added to that compound base to make an abstract noun. (3) Chapter 14’s front-vuddhi rule, strengthening the first vowel of the whole word, not the vowel next to the suffix: ba- becomes bā-. (4) Chapter 14’s assimilation rule for -ya: the -ty- produced where the suffix meets the base’s final -t (from suta) assimilates to -cc-. The result, bāhusaccaṁ, carries nothing of bahussuta recognisable on its surface, and the word-formation chapter uses this example to make that point.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view.

    Read the sentence.

    1. So kho ahaṁ I, that one (m. nom. sg.) · akāmakānaṁ unwilling (m. gen. pl.) · mātāpitūnaṁ mother and father (m. gen. pl.) · assumukhānaṁ with tear-stained faces (m. gen. pl.) · rudantānaṁ weeping (m. gen. pl., pres. part.) · kesamassuṁ hair and beard (nt. acc. sg.) · ohāretvā having shaved off · kāsāyāni vatthāni ochre robes (nt. acc. pl.) · acchādetvā having put on · agārasmā from home (nt. abl. sg.) · anagāriyaṁ to homelessness (nt. acc. sg.) · pabbajiṁ I went forth (1 sg. aor.). “And I, though my mother and father were unwilling and were weeping with tear-stained faces, shaved off my hair and beard, put on ochre robes, and went forth from home into homelessness.”

      Ohāretvā and acchādetvā are ordinary absolutives, sharing their subject with pabbajiṁ: the going-forth is what “I” did, in three stages, one finite verb at the end. The four genitives (akāmakānaṁ mātāpitūnaṁ assumukhānaṁ rudantānaṁ) are a genitive absolute: they report the parents’ weeping, an action belonging to somebody else entirely, running at the same time as the going-forth, not before it. It could not have been an absolutive, because an absolutive has no case, gender or person of its own to say whose action it reports. Once the action belongs to someone other than the main verb’s subject, the absolutive cannot mark it, and Pāli reaches for the case-substitution rule instead. This one sentence demonstrates Chapter 16’s whole argument, with every device in the chapter at once.

    2. Evaṁ passaṁ thus seeing (m. nom. sg., pres. part.) · bhikkhave monks (voc. pl.) · sutavā ariyasāvako a learned noble disciple (m. nom. sg.) · rūpasmiṁ regarding form (nt. loc. sg.) · pi also · nibbindati becomes disenchanted · vedanāya regarding feeling (f. loc. sg.) · saññāya regarding perception (f. loc. sg.) · saṅkhāresu regarding volitional formations (m. loc. pl.) · viññāṇasmiṁ regarding consciousness (nt. loc. sg.). “Seeing thus, monks, a learned noble disciple becomes disenchanted with form, with feeling, with perception, with volitional formations, with consciousness.”

      Rūpasmiṁ, saṅkhāresu and viññāṇasmiṁ carry endings that can be nothing but locative. Vedanāya and saññāya are feminine ā-stems whose ending could be instrumental, ablative, dative, genitive or locative. Chapter 15’s two-step method settles them, in order. Look at the verb first: nibbindati takes a locative of what one is disenchanted with. Then look for the parallel: the two ambiguous forms stand in a series between three unambiguous locatives. The verb alone would have been enough; the parallel confirms it.

    3. Seyyathāpi just as · purisassa of a man (m. gen. sg.) · agāraṁ a house (nt. nom. sg.) · ekādasadvāraṁ with eleven doors (nt. nom. sg., bahubbīhi) · so he (m. nom. sg.) · tasmiṁ agāre āditte that house being ablaze (nt. loc. sg., past part.) · ekamekena dvārena by any one door (nt. ins. sg.) · sakkuṇeyya would be able (3 sg. opt.) · attānaṁ sotthiṁ kātuṁ to make himself safe. “Just as, sir, if a man had a house with eleven doors, and that house were ablaze, he would be able to get himself to safety by any one of the doors.”

      Three constructions. The simile frame, seyyathāpi opening the comparison (Chapter 16, “The simile”). A locative absolute carrying a condition with no particle at all, agāre āditte, ‘if that house were ablaze’ (Chapter 16, the locative absolute’s hypothetical sense, the past participle āditte marking the state where no word means ‘if’). An infinitive of purpose, sotthiṁ kātuṁ, ‘to make safe’, sharing its agent with the main verb, formed in Chapter 11 and put to use in Chapter 16, so the chapter on clause syntax can call all three of these its own. Chapter 16 calls this sentence “a good measure of how much of the grammar a reader has to have available to follow one ordinary sentence of the canon at reading speed”, and this review takes it at its word.

    4. First sentence: no cetaṁ … abhavissa ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ ‘if there were no unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned’ (abhavissa, 3 sg. conditional) · nayidha … nissaraṇaṁ paññāyetha ‘no escape … would be discerned’ (paññāyetha, 3 sg. mid. optative, passive stem). Second sentence: yasmā ca kho … atthi ajātaṁ … ‘but because there is an unborn …’ (present indicative) · tasmā … nissaraṇaṁ paññāyati ‘therefore … is discerned’ (present indicative).

      The first sentence is an unreal condition: the false premise goes into the conditional, its consequence into the optative, Chapter 16‘s third type. The second sentence is two plain indicatives. The shift changes the function of yasmā: in the first sentence’s shape it would answer to tasmā as an ordinary ’because … therefore’ stating a real reason; here it opens the sentence that corrects the counterfactual just entertained, restoring the true state of affairs: ‘but because [in fact] there is an unborn …, therefore [in fact] an escape is discerned’. The whole doctrinal weight of the passage, that an escape from the conditioned is possible because the unconditioned exists, rests on this contrast of mood between the two sentences alone.

    5. Ito so, bhikkhave, ekanavutikappe yaṁ vipassī bhagavā … loke udapādi ‘ninety-one eons ago, monks, the Blessed One Vipassī … arose in the world’.

      Buddhaghosa resolves the empty relative instead of leaving it: Tattha yaṁ vipassīti yasmiṁ kappe vipassī, “‘yaṁ vipassī’ means ‘in which eon’ Vipassī [arose]”, assigning yaṁ the locative sense (his bhummatthe, the fourth of four uses he distinguishes by case), even though on its face the word looks accusative and appears to modify nothing at all. For a reader to accept this as a method rather than a one-off convenience, the empty relative would always have to carry a case, recoverable from the sentence, and never be case-less. Buddhaghosa’s four-way classification makes that claim, each use cited to a real passage (nominative, accusative, instrumental, locative). It is a stronger and more demanding position than the shrug that such relatives merely “do not pose a problem” for translation; it says that recovering the case is what reading the sentence consists of.

    6. Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. “Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease.”

      There is no finite verb; hoti is understood, and the two compounds (samudayadhammaṁ, nirodhadhammaṁ) stand as predicate to their subjects with nothing supplying tense. Chapter 16 calls this a nominal sentence, one of the four kinds of predicate Duroiselle lists, and here concord holds, since taṁ and nirodhadhammaṁ are both neuter singular in agreement, not two nouns merely placed in apposition. In place of a yo … so pair, the sentence is held together by Chapter 17‘s device for generalising a relative clause by adding an indefinite: yaṁ kiñci ’whatever’ opens a class with no member named, and sabbaṁ taṁ ‘all that’ closes it and states what holds of every member. Every word in the sentence is either a pronoun or a compound of dhamma, and the whole of its content lies in the relation between the two halves, so the chapter on relative clauses calls it the canon’s shortest statement of insight and a pure specimen of the construction at once.

    Scan the line.

    1. The full, un-elided word is buddhānaṁ, the genitive plural of the -a stem masculine noun buddha, the -ānaṁ ending given for every masculine -a stem in Chapter 3’s declension and displayed across Appendix A.1. Dropping the niggahīta, which is Chapter 2’s material generally, leaves the syllable -na light where -naṁ would have closed it and made it heavy; the Siloka’s even-line cadence needs ⏑—⏑× and no other pattern, so this one dropped nasal keeps the most-quoted verse of the Dhammapada inside the metre it is written in.

    2. The third pāda, Ākaṅkhamānā sotthānaṁ, scans ——⏑—¦———×, which is the mavipulā: syllables two to four run —⏑—, and the cadence is four heavy syllables running straight through where the pathyā would require ⏑——. It even carries the mavipulā’s normal word break after the fifth syllable, at Ākaṅkhamānā | sotthānaṁ. Someone who had learnt only the pathyā would take the opening line of the Maṅgalasutta, one of the best-known suttas in the canon, for a corrupt text.

    3. The first pāda, Tapo ca brahmacariyañca, needs two accommodations at once: br- fails to make position, so the syllable in front of it (ca) stays light; and the sarabhatti vowel of cariya is dropped, so it scans as the two syllables car-ya, not three. Together these turn what a literal reading gives as nine syllables in no recognisable shape into the regular eight-syllable Siloka opening ⏑—⏑—¦⏑——×. The second pāda, ariyasaccāna’ dassanaṁ, needs two more, and each does separate work. Ariyasaccānaṁ has a sarabhatti vowel of its own, as cariya does, so it scans ar-ya-sac-cā- and not a-ri-ya-sac-cā-; keep it and the line has nine syllables where eight are wanted. And the niggahīta of ariyasaccānaṁ is dropped, as in Dhp 183 above; keep it and the line has its eight syllables but closes ——⏑× where the Siloka’s even line requires ⏑—⏑×. Neither accommodation can replace the other, so they are named separately. That makes four separate departures from the written text in two lines, in an ordinary verse, not a difficult one.

    4. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana’s reading, Chaccābhiṭhānāni abhabba kātuṁ, needs no licence at all: abhabba has a short final -a, light by its own nature, and the line scans ——⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—× as a regular Tuṭṭhubha. Ānandajoti’s text prints abhabbŏ instead, which requires the final -o to be read light against its own weight: a true licence, and one that changes the line’s opening from heavy to light in that position. This is the same question the book has raised twice before: once in Chapter 1, on aneḷagalāya/anelagalāya, where a variant recorded only in the apparatus turned out to be the reading the corpus carries; and once in Chapter 16, on saṁvaṭṭamāno/saṁvaṭṭamāne, where the nominative absolute rested on a single word in a single edition against two others. In all three cases, a claim about the language turned out on inspection to be a claim about which edition one had opened.

    5. Line a, Satto guhāyaṁ bahunābhichanno, has the normal break, —⏑⏑ (opening ——⏑—, break —⏑⏑, cadence —⏑—×). Line b, Tiṭṭhaṁ naro mohanasmiṁ pagāḷho, has the break —⏑— instead, one of the documented alternatives to the ordinary —⏑⏑, and otherwise identical to line a in every other position. Hold the three-part pattern (opening, break, cadence) in mind and this is a variation within the metre, not an error; a reader scanning by gaṇas alone would have no ready name for what has changed.

    6. Line a, Appaṁ vata jīvitaṁ idaṁ, has nine syllables (—— ⏑⏑ ¦ —⏑—⏑×) totalling fourteen measures: 2+2+1+1 for the opening, then 2+1+2+1+2 for the cadence. Line b, Oraṁ vassasatāpi miyyati, has ten syllables and totals sixteen measures by the same arithmetic: 2+2+2+1+1 for the opening, then the identical cadence. The cadences are the same eight measures in both; the whole difference sits in the opening, which is two measures heavier in the even line. So the two lines are not equal to each other, and the metre never asked them to be. The Vetālīya fixes a total per position, fourteen measures in every odd line and sixteen in every even one, and because it counts mattā, not syllables, a poet can reach that total by any arrangement of lights and heavies they please. So line a can spend nine syllables and line b ten while both fit the metre. The last piece of the arithmetic is the pādantagaru rule: the even line’s final syllable is light and is counted two anyway, without which every even line in every measure metre would come out one measure short.

    Read at length.

    1. Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. “And while this exposition was being spoken, the dust-free, stainless eye of the Dhamma arose in the venerable Koṇḍañña.” Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. “Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease.” Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. “And when the wheel of Dhamma had been set rolling by the Blessed One, the earth-dwelling gods raised a cry.”

      The first sentence’s construction is a locative absolute, and its participle is present, not past: bhaññamāne, ‘being spoken’, not bhaṇite, ‘having been spoken’. Chapter 16’s rule for the tense of the participle in this construction is exact: a past participle where the subordinate action precedes the main one, a present participle where the two run together. So Koṇḍañña’s insight is stated as arising while the discourse was still being delivered, not after it closed.

      The second sentence has no finite verb (the predicate is a bare compound, nirodhadhammaṁ) and no yo … so pair. The generalising is done by Chapter 17‘s device of adding an indefinite to the relative: yaṁ kiñci ’whatever’, closed by sabbaṁ taṁ ‘all that’, which opens a class with no member named and then says what holds of every member of it.

      The third sentence’s case would be an ordinary locative of place, ‘in the wheel of Dhamma’, if the sentence stopped at dhammacakke. What turns it into something else is pavattite … bhagavatā: a past participle agreeing with the locative noun, and an instrumental naming who did the setting-rolling. This is Chapter 16’s account of the passive locative absolute: the grammatical subject stays in the locative, and the logical subject, the agent, is added in the instrumental, which is very often the first sign that the locative is absolute and not merely local. The agent named is bhagavatā, the Blessed One: it is his own turning of the wheel, reported in the case that names an instrument, not the case that names a place.

    Reaching back.

    1. The compound-seam rule, from Chapter 13: the ordinary rules of sandhi that would flatten a consonant cluster are “partly annulled at the seam of compounds in order not to obscure the initial sound of the posterior member”, Oberlies’s own words for it. Duccaritaṁ keeps its c, duttaro its t, uggacchati its g. Had the assimilation rules of Chapter 2 applied without restraint at these seams, none of the second members could be recognised, let alone looked up. The rule solves the reader’s problem: the language protects the beginning of the second member so that it stays findable, the very difficulty a compound poses in every other respect.

    2. The suffix is -to, marking an ablative sense (‘by birth’, ‘by name’, ‘by clan’), and it is introduced in Chapter 3, on the nouns. Chapter 15 reaches back to it for the point-of-view ablative: jātitopi anussarati, nāmatopi anussarati, gottatopi anussarati is Kaccāyana’s -to construction, used as Chapter 3 taught it. The enclitic pi welded onto each word has removed the bare form from a plain search: every occurrence in the passage is printed gottatopi, not gottato, so a search for the citation form alone will find it apparently unattested: the same caution Chapter 12 states generally about enclitics that are almost never printed as separate words.

    3. The stem is eta(d)-, and the source is Oberlies, whose §42(3) has the two stems “inflect in the same way”. That lets Chapter 17 give a paradigm for the relative pronoun at all, since Appendix A carries no ya- table of its own and the chapter’s table is therefore checked against the corpus instead of the appendix. The chain starts in Chapter 5, on the pronouns, and it runs one link further than the question implies: eta(d)- has no table of its own there either, because it “declines exactly as ta(d) does” and needs none. So the paradigm printed in Chapter 5 is the distal ta(d)-’s, and the relative pronoun reaches it at two removes: ya(d)- like eta(d)-, eta(d)- like ta(d)-. Know the distal paradigm cold and you have most of the relative pronoun’s as well.

    4. Chapter 4 gives sādhukaṁ ‘well’, niccaṁ ‘constantly’ and ciraṁ ‘for a long time’: the accusative singular of an adjective used adverbially. Chapter 12 gives tikkhattuṁ ‘three times’ among its own derived-by-suffix adverbs, and then explicitly returns to the promise: “the adverbial accusative was introduced in the chapter on gender and adjectives, which promised that this one would return to it; it has.” On top of the single accusative case Chapter 12 builds the larger table of case-marked adverbs across all six oblique cases (accusative for manner and extent, instrumental for manner and means, dative for purpose, ablative for cause and position, genitive for time elapsed, locative for place and time), of which the Chapter 4 accusative turns out to be only the first row.

    5. R3 called the conditional tense “the rarest finite form in the language”, and in Chapter 16 it finally gets its one regular job: it is “the one place in the language where the conditional tense … has a settled job”, carrying the false premise of an unreal condition. The standing form of that job is abhavissa, ‘were to be’, the form worked in Ud 8.3 above (item 36), where the false premise that nothing unconditioned exists is stated in the conditional and then answered, yasmā … tasmā, by the true state of affairs in the plain indicative.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. Tirokuṭṭesu, an avyayībhāva inflected as a locative plural. Gharāvāso, a locative tappurisa, “dwelling in a house”. Rajopatho, a kammadhāraya, “a path that is dust”.

      The one you could not have built from its members is the third. Given raja and patha, the form a reader would write is rajapatho, and the canon does not have it.

      Rajapatho has 0 root-text occurrences and rajopatho 4. But it is not an oddity, and to call it one would be the easy answer to the wrong question. The first member is an -as stem, DPD’s rajas ‘dust, dirt’, of the same declension as manas and cetas, and an -as stem regularly gives -o- inside a compound. Chapter 13’s table of what a stem does in composition names this word, rajas → rajo- in rajopatho, beside vacas → vacī-, the same rule refusing to be tidy.

      So what you could not have supplied is the declension, not the compound. The question hands you raja, and raja is the adjective; the noun behind it is rajas, and the members as given do not show it. The classes show how the two words stand to each other and are silent about the form either takes.

      That is the cost of a compound to a reader. It gives the reciter a great deal: a five-member dvanda is one word to hold instead of five, one ending instead of five, and the whole of dependent origination becomes twelve short phrases instead of a paragraph. The cost is that the joins are invisible. A compound gives no signal about which class it is, which relation its members stand in, or what form a member takes inside it, and resolving one by reversing the rules gives rajapatho, abbhokāso split into abbha and okāsa, and a plausible analysis of a word that never had one. The classes show what to look for, the declension the form to look for it in, and the dictionary what is there.
