    Exercises: Case Syntax

    This chapter’s claim is that an ending narrows the possibilities and something else decides among them. So the set makes you decide instead of rewarding you for reciting a list of uses. Most items give you a real sentence and ask what the case means there, and several give you a form whose ending is truly ambiguous and ask how you would settle it.

    Stage 1 is four passages with every case already named for you, because hearing what an ending means is a different skill from working it out, and it comes first. Five of the later items contain a trap. They are not marked. Where you find one, say what the trap is as well as what the answer is. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — case syntax

    Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss, its case labels and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear the endings doing different jobs.

    1. Atha kho bhagavā kismiñcideva pakaraṇe bhikkhusaṅghaṁ paṇāmetvā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya kapilavatthuṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi. (SN 22.80 — S iii 91)

      atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgkismiñcidevafor some or othern loc sgpakaraṇeaffairn loc sgbhikkhusaṅghaṁthe Saṅgha of monksm acc sg, objectpaṇāmetvāhaving dismissedabspubbaṇhasamayaṁin the morningm acc sg, adverbialnivāsetvāhaving dressedabspattacīvaraṁbowl and roben acc sg, objectādāyahaving takenabskapilavatthuṁKapilavatthun acc sg, goalpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sg, purposepāvisihe entered3 sg aor

      “Then the Blessed One, having dismissed the Saṅgha of monks over some matter or other, dressed in the morning, took his bowl and robe, and entered Kapilavatthu for alms.”

      Say it aloud slowly, and hear that four words in this sentence carry the accusative ending with different functions. Name the four, say what each one does, and then say which of the four is not a participant in the action at all. This is the sentence the chapter opens with, and items 14 and 15 will come back to two of its words cold.

    2. Puttā matthi dhanaṁ matthi, iti bālo vihaññati; (Dhp 62 — Dhp p. 9)

      puttāsonsm nom plmatthi< me of me 1 gen./dat. sg + atthi there isdhanaṁwealthn nom sgitithusbālothe foolm nom sgvihaññatiis tormented

      “‘I have sons, I have wealth’ — so the fool torments himself.”

      Say it aloud. Pāli has no verb ‘to have’, and this line shows what it does instead. Give the literal structure of the first half, and which case carries the possessor. Then two things about matthi: say what has happened at the join, and say why a reader looking for the possessor at the front of the word will not find it. The case of me here cannot be decided, and item 37 will ask you what the tradition does about that.

    3. Ajjeva rattiyā pacchime yāme tathāgatassa parinibbānaṁ bhavissati. (DN 16 — D ii 139)

      ajjatodayevajustrattiyāof the nightf gen sg, partitivepacchimelastm loc sgyāmein the watchm loc sg, time whentathāgatassaof the Tathāgatam gen sgparinibbānaṁfinal extinguishmentn nom sgbhavissatiwill be

      “This very day, in the last watch of the night, the Tathāgata’s final extinguishment will take place.”

      Say it aloud. There is a genitive inside the locative phrase, in the most ordinary use a genitive has. Name it and say what that use is. Then say why rattiyā, alone in the line, has an ending that settles nothing on its own, and what settles it.

    4. Ayaṁ, deva, pūraṇo kassapo saṅghī ceva gaṇī ca gaṇācariyo ca ñāto yasassī titthakaro sādhusammato bahujanassa rattaññū cirapabbajito addhagato vayoanuppatto. (DN 2 — D i 47)

      ayaṁthism nom sgdevayour majestym voc sgpūraṇo kassapoPūraṇa Kassapam nom sgsaṅghīhaving a communitym nom sgceva … caboth … andgaṇīhaving a followinggaṇācariyoa teacher of a followingñātowell knownyasassīrenownedtitthakaroa founder of a sectsādhusammatoheld in high regardbahujanassaby many peoplem gen sg, agent with a past participlerattaññūof long standingcirapabbajitolong gone forthaddhagatoadvanced in yearsvayoanuppattocome to the last stage of life

      “This Pūraṇa Kassapa, your majesty, has a community and a following, is a teacher of a following, well known and renowned, a founder of a sect, held in high regard by many people, of long standing, long gone forth, advanced in years and come to the last stage of life.”

      Say it aloud. Eleven words in a row in one case, and then one word that is not. Find it, and say which case would ordinarily stand there, and why this one stands there instead. Then say what holds the eleven together, given that no verb governs any of them.

    Vacasā paricitā — case syntax

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

    1. Musāvādā veramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ samādiyāmi. (Kp 2)

      musāvādāfrom false speechm abl sgveramaṇīsikkhāpadaṁ< veramaṇī abstaining + sikkhāpadaṁ training rule — the training rule of abstaining n acc sgsamādiyāmiI undertake

      “I undertake the training rule of abstaining from false speech.”

      Many readers of this book will have said this line, or one of its four fellows, more often than any other sentence in Pāli. Recite it and rebuild it from the gloss. Then answer the question that reciting it does not: the ablative is not governed by the verb. Say what does govern it, and where that word is. Then say what the answer implies about whether the phrase could survive being compounded, as it has been.

    2. Etaṁ nibbidāya virāgāya nirodhāya upasamāya abhiññāya sambodhāya nibbānāya saṁvattati. (DN 9 — D i 189)

      etaṁthisn nom sgnibbidāyato disenchantmentf dat sgvirāgāyato dispassionm dat sgnirodhāyato cessationm dat sgupasamāyato peacem dat sgabhiññāyato direct knowledgef dat sgsambodhāyato awakeningm dat sgnibbānāyato extinguishmentn dat sgsaṁvattatileads

      “This leads to disenchantment, to dispassion, to cessation, to peace, to direct knowledge, to awakening, to extinguishment.”

      Rebuild it from the gloss. Seven datives in a row: now check the claim that all seven are equally readable as datives. Sort them by stem class, count how many carry an ending that can be nothing but a dative, and say what the remainder are. Then say what makes the remainder readable anyway, and set item 25’s rule beside your answer: the two should agree, and in this set they once did not.

    3. Evaṁ passaṁ, bhikkhave, sutavā ariyasāvako rūpasmimpi nibbindati, vedanāyapi nibbindati, saññāyapi nibbindati, saṅkhāresupi nibbindati, viññāṇasmimpi nibbindati. (SN 22.59 — S iii 68)

      evaṁthuspassaṁseeingm nom sg, pres partbhikkhavemonksvoc plsutavālearnedm nom sgariyasāvakonoble disciplem nom sgrūpasmiṁregarding formn loc sgpialsonibbindatibecomes disenchantedvedanāyaregarding feelingf loc sgsaññāyaregarding perceptionf loc sgsaṅkhāresuregarding volitional formationsm loc plviññāṇasmiṁregarding consciousnessn loc sg

      “Seeing thus, monks, a learned noble disciple becomes disenchanted with form, with feeling, with perception, with volitional formations, with consciousness.”

      Rebuild it from the gloss. The chapter says this sentence is the whole chapter in one line. Say why: how many of the five endings can only be locative, how many could be five cases each, and what the first group does for the second. Item 36 will ask you to do the same work on one of these words with the sentence taken away.

    4. Tiṭṭhatu, bhante, bhagavā kappaṁ, tiṭṭhatu sugato kappaṁ bahujanahitāya bahujanasukhāya lokānukampāya atthāya hitāya sukhāya devamanussānaṁ. (DN 16 — D ii 103)

      tiṭṭhatulet him remain3 sg impbhantesirvocbhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgkappaṁfor an aeonm acc sg, extent of timesugatothe Fortunate Onem nom sgbahujanahitāyafor the welfare of many peoplen dat sgbahujanasukhāyafor the happiness of many peoplen dat sglokānukampāyaout of compassion for the worldf dat sgatthāyafor the benefitm dat sghitāyafor the welfaren dat sgsukhāyafor the happinessn dat sgdevamanussānaṁof gods and humansm gen pl

      “Let the Blessed One remain for an aeon, sir; let the Fortunate One remain for an aeon, for the welfare of many people, for the happiness of many people, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit and welfare and happiness of gods and humans.”

      Rebuild it from the gloss. One accusative among the datives, and it is not an object. Name its function. Then take the genitive at the end and say how many of the datives it reaches back over, and how you know where to stop.

    Manasānupekkhitā — case syntax

    Recall. Answer from memory, in a phrase, before opening the chapter.

    1. The tradition counts eight of one thing and six of another. Name both, name the two of the eight that are not among the six, and give the tradition’s reason for leaving them out.

    2. Two pairs of cases have partly merged in form. Name both pairs, say where each pair still stays apart, and give the consequence for reading.

    3. You meet a word whose ending could be five different cases. Name the two things you should look at, in order.

    4. When do the nominative and the agent come apart, and what case does the agent take when they do?

    5. Pāli has no verb corresponding to English ‘have’. What does it use instead, and what is the literal shape of the resulting sentence?

    Name the case and its job. For each bolded word, name the case and say which use of it this is. The sentences are all in the chapter.

    2. kapilavatthuṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi

    4. svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — case syntax

    Choose the case. Produce the answer before checking it.

    1. ‘For the welfare of many people, for the happiness of many people.’ Which case, and what is the ending on an a-stem? Then say why you would not be able to identify that case with the same confidence if the nouns were feminine ā-stems.

    2. A verb of fearing or trembling takes its complement in one particular case, which is not the one an English speaker expects. Name the case, and give two such verbs.

    3. Pāli has two ways of forming an ablative and one of them is unmistakable. Name it, say why Wijesekera declines to call it a proper case ending at all, and give three examples of it from this chapter.

    4. A reputable grammar lists appamaññati ‘thinks lightly of’ among the verbs governing the genitive and cites Dhammapada 121 for it. Look the verse up. Say what the canon actually prints, what the verb actually is, and what has happened at the join.

    5. Itthattāya: name the case, give the stem it is built on and the suffix, and say why the bare word is almost never found in the texts.

    Read the sentence. Give the three layers: the line as printed, a word-by-word gloss naming the case of every inflected word, and your own rendering.

    1. Seyyathāpi, bho gotama, puriso purisaṁ narakapapātaṁ patantaṁ kesesu gahetvā uddharitvā thale patiṭṭhapeyya. (DN 12 — D i 234)

    Put it back into Pāli. The answer is a particular line of canon, and getting close to it is the exercise. A well-formed sentence that is not the one in the book is a good result and is not this exercise.

    1. ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived out, what was to be done has been done, there is no further coming to this state of being.’ The last four words are three: a negative, a comparative adverb, and one noun in one case.

    2. ‘All tremble at the rod, all fear death.’ Two verbs, two nouns, and the nouns are not in the case an English speaker would put them in.

    Read at length.

    1. Read Ud 1.10, the Bāhiya sutta, twice through without stopping to parse anything. It is short, it is one of the best-known passages in the canon, and the instruction at its centre is almost entirely a matter of case syntax. Only then answer:
    • Diṭṭhe diṭṭhamattaṁ bhavissati, sute sutamattaṁ bhavissati … Four words in the same case open the four clauses. Name the case, and say which of the tradition’s four kinds of locative you think it is, and why the question is harder than it looks.
    • Each of those four clauses has a second inflected word, and it is not in the same case as the first. Name that case and say what it does there.
    • Bāhiya asks the Buddha to teach him and is refused twice. Find the sentence in which he presses his request and name the case of every inflected word in it. One of them is open between two cases; say which, and why the chapter would not force a choice.
    • The sutta’s closing verse turns on yattha and tattha. Are these case forms? Say what they are built on and where in this book each half of the answer is treated.

    Reaching back.

    1. Chapter 12. Saddhiṁ and saha govern the instrumental, and aññatra and vinā govern it too. Say in one sentence why ‘with’ and ‘without’ should govern the same case, using the tradition’s name for that case’s fundamental sense.

    2. Chapter 13. A tappurisa compound is a case relation with the ending taken off. Resolve bhikkhusaṅgho and kāmataṇhā into two words each, and say why the recovered cases are different. Then say which fact from this chapter is what actually decides the second one.

    3. Chapter 3. The feminine ā-stem singular ending -āya does five jobs. List them. Then take vedanāya from item 7 and say, in the chapter’s terms, what settles which of the five it is there.

    4. Chapter 5. Evaṁ me sutaṁ. Say what Buddhaghosa does with the me, and what this chapter recommends a reader do when the tradition itself declines to choose.

    A chain of genitives. The chapter takes the first half of this definition. Here is the second, which differs from it in one word.

    1. Aparikkhittassa gāmassa gharūpacāre ṭhitassa majjhimassa purisassa leḍḍupāto. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 46) Gloss it. Then say which noun each genitive hangs on, and how you know, since the endings cannot show it.

    Say it aloud.

    1. Read these five verses aloud, slowly, twice through. They are consecutive in the Dhammapada and they are the same sentence five times. Then say which two of the five bolded words carry an ending that could be more than one case, and what makes them readable anyway.

    Piyato jāyatī soko, piyato jāyatī bhayaṁ; (Dhp 212 — Dhp p. 32)
    Pemato jāyatī soko, pemato jāyatī bhayaṁ; (Dhp 213 — Dhp p. 32)
    Ratiyā jāyatī soko, ratiyā jāyatī bhayaṁ; (Dhp 214 — Dhp p. 32)
    Kāmato jāyatī soko, kāmato jāyatī bhayaṁ; (Dhp 215 — Dhp p. 32)
    Taṇhāya jāyatī soko, taṇhāya jāyatī bhayaṁ. (Dhp 216 — Dhp p. 32)

    Coming back — case syntax

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 14–24. Naming a case is the operation this chapter exists to make automatic, and eleven bolded words are the whole of it in miniature. The five that carry a trap are the five to check yourself hardest on.

    Day 10. Items 25–29, and item 30. Choosing a case is the harder direction, and repeat item 28 even when the rest come back: a reader is least prepared to find a source wrong.

    Day 30. Items 1 and 7 aloud, and item 39. If vedanāya still settles itself out of the company it keeps, the chapter’s claim has been understood, not merely agreed with: the ending narrows and the sentence decides.
