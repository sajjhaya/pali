    Review: The Verb

    A review is more than a recap. The exercises after each chapter test that chapter, and they are the right instrument for that. This set tests what only becomes visible once several chapters are in place: where they overlap, where a form belongs to both, and where the habit one chapter builds is the habit the next one breaks. Nothing here can be answered from a single chapter.

    It covers the whole of Part III: The Present System, The Past and the Future, and Passive, Causative and Denominative. All three have been through the verification passes, which is the condition for a chapter appearing in a review at all.

    Much of what follows is drawn from one sutta, the Brahmāyācana at SN 6.1, because it is the natural text for this part of the book. The whole discourse turns on mood, not tense: what the Buddha would teach and what others might fail to understand, Brahmā’s let him teach, the flat future there will be those who understand. If you can follow the moods you can follow the argument; if you cannot, you will get the events right and the sense wrong.

    The four stages run as they do in the chapter sets, with the review’s own constraint on the first two: every line in stages 1 and 2 has already been worked in an earlier set, and is here to be asked a question that set could not ask.

    Sotānugata — the verb

    Nothing is withheld. Each line is given with its gloss. Say it aloud, then answer.

    1. Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno; Attānaṁ upamaṁ katvā, na haneyya na ghātaye. (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19)

      sabbeallm nom pltasantitremble3 pl presdaṇḍassaat the rodm gen sgbhāyantifear3 pl presmaccunodeathm gen sgattānaṁoneselfm acc sgupamaṁas the comparisonf acc sgkatvāhaving madeabsnanothaneyyashould kill3 sg optghātayeshould have [another] kill3 sg opt

      “All tremble at the rod, all fear death; making oneself the comparison, one should not kill nor have another kill.”

      Say it aloud. Four finite verbs, and between them the whole of this part. Name the chapter each belongs to. Then the item: haneyya and ghātaye are the same mood, the same person and the same number, and they are not the same verb. Say what separates them, in one morpheme, and say what that morpheme does to what is being forbidden.

      Then say why taking the two as elegant variation misreads a precept and not merely a line.

    2. Gahakāraka diṭṭhosi, puna gehaṁ na kāhasi; Sabbā te phāsukā bhaggā, gahakūṭaṁ visaṅkhataṁ; Visaṅkhāragataṁ cittaṁ, taṇhānaṁ khayamajjhagā. (Dhp 154 — Dhp p. 23)

      gahakārakahouse-builderm voc sgdiṭṭho·si< diṭṭho seen m nom sg, pp + asi you are 2 sg prespunaagaingehaṁhousen acc sgnanotkāhasiyou will build2 sg futsabbāallf nom plteyour2 sg genphāsukāraftersf nom plbhaggābrokenf nom pl, ppgahakūṭaṁroof-peakn nom sgvisaṅkhataṁdemolishedn nom sgvisaṅkhāragataṁgone to the unconditionedn nom sgcittaṁmindn nom sgtaṇhānaṁof cravingsf gen plkhayam·ajjhagā< khayaṁ the ending m acc sg + ajjhagā has reached 3 sg

      “House-builder, you are seen! You will not build a house again. All your rafters are broken, the roof-peak demolished. The mind has gone to the unconditioned, it has reached the ending of cravings.”

      Say it aloud. Three of the four verbal ideas here are not carried by a finite verb at all, and the past-and-future set asked you only about the two that are. Find the three, say what is carrying each, and say what supplies the tense in each case. Something does, and it is not an ending.

      Then kāhasi, which is the trap. It is a future, and it is not built the way the chapter’s rule builds one. Say what is missing from it, and say what a reader should do on meeting a future they cannot cut into a stem and an affix.

    3. Idhānanda, bhikkhu sabbasaṅkhāresu aṭṭīyati harāyati jigucchati. (AN 10.60 — A v 111)

      idhahereānandaĀnandam voc sgbhikkhua monkm nom sgsabbasaṅkhāresuin all conditioned thingsm loc plaṭṭīyatiis troubled3 sg presharāyatiis ashamed3 sg pres denomjigucchatiis disgusted3 sg pres desid

      “Here, Ānanda, a monk is troubled, ashamed and disgusted by all conditioned things.”

      Say it aloud. Three verbs in a row, three different formations, and one ending. Say which formation each is. Then the question this review exists for, and the one to carry out of this part: say which chapter supplied the ending and which supplied the stem, and state in a sentence what a reader can read off a Pāli verb’s ending and what they can never read off it.

    Vacasā paricitā — the verb

    Recite each until it is fluent, then rebuild it with the Pāli covered.

    1. Pare ca na vijānanti, mayamettha yamāmase; Ye ca tattha vijānanti, tato sammanti medhagā. (Dhp 6 — Dhp p. 2)

      pareothersm nom plcaandnanotvijānantiunderstand3 pl presmayaṁwenom pletthahereyamāmaselet us restrain ourselves1 pl imp middleyethose whom nom pltatthatheretatothereuponsammantiare appeased3 pl presmedhagāquarrelsm nom pl

      “Others do not understand that here we must restrain ourselves; but those who do understand — thereupon their quarrels are appeased.”

      Rebuild it from the gloss, aloud. Three of the four finite verbs take one set of endings and the fourth takes another. Name both sets, say which verb is the odd one, and say what the ending does there that the ordinary set could not. This depends on the distinction of item 8, which costs sentences when it is run together with the other thing “voice” names.

    2. Apārutā tesaṁ amatassa dvārā, / ye sotavanto pamuñcantu saddhaṁ; / vihiṁsasaññī paguṇaṁ na bhāsiṁ, / dhammaṁ paṇītaṁ manujesu brahme. (SN 6.1 — S i 138) — Read it aloud five times, slowly, holding the long vowels for two beats and sounding both halves of every doubled consonant. Then answer: pamuñcantu and na bhāsiṁ stand in consecutive lines and belong to different chapters of this part. Parse both. One of them is missing something that Chapter 8 says its type “generally” has — say what, and say what decides it here.

    Manasānupekkhitā — the verb

    Recall. Answer from memory, in a phrase. If a question needs the book open, that is the answer to it.

    1. What is the present stem, how do you get it from a dictionary, and which three forms of the verb are built on it? What are the rest built on, and why does that one division decide which chapter a form is treated in?

    2. Pāli has a set of primary personal endings and a set of secondary ones. Say which tense or mood takes which, across the whole of Part III. One of them takes both: name it.

    3. The word “voice” names two different distinctions in this part, and running them together will cost you sentences. Name both, say what carries each, and say which of the two the personal endings can never tell you.

    4. Mā and na both come out as “don’t” in English. Name the construction each of them takes, and say what the difference is in what they forbid.

    5. There are four aorists and one of them is still alive. Which, what makes that fact useful and not merely historical, and what does it turn the other three into?

    6. Of the five derived stems, three are productive and two are not. Name them either way, and say what productivity licenses you to do with a verb no grammar lists, and what it forbids you to do.

    Say what the form is, and what would settle it. Each of these is either ambiguous or belongs to more than one chapter at once. Give the analysis, and name what in a sentence would decide it; that is the point of the group.

    1. desesi

    2. assa

    3. khomhi

    4. avoca

    5. dissati

    6. sādiyati

    7. labbhetha

    Build the form. Give the form, and show the steps that produce it.

    1. The third person singular conditional of bhavati, and then the third person plural. Name the three separate things that go into the form before the ending does.

    2. The third person singular optative of atthi: both of the forms the canon actually uses, and the two different endings they come from.

    3. The second person singular imperative of gacchati, in both of its shapes. Then say which stems can take the shorter shape and why the others cannot.

    4. The third person singular present passive of vadati ‘says’. Then say why the aorist of this verb gives you no help at all in getting there.

    5. The causative of bhavati, third person plural present; then the future of that same causative, third person singular. The second one is where the trap is, so show the stem you are attaching -ssa- to.

    6. “Monks, do not fear”: second person plural, in the construction Chapter 8 requires, not the one Chapter 7 would offer.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — the verb

    Read the sentence. Give a word-by-word gloss and an idiomatic rendering, and account for every finite verb in it. Each of these needs at least two of the three chapters.

    1. Rūpañca hidaṁ, bhikkhave, attā abhavissa, nayidaṁ rūpaṁ ābādhāya saṁvatteyya, labbhetha ca rūpe … (SN 22.59 — S iii 66)

      • Three finite verbs, three different things going on. Take them in order.
      • Which clause is the protasis and which the apodosis, and does this sentence follow the normal division of labour between the two moods?

    2. No cetaṁ padaheyya, nayidaṁ saccamanupāpuṇeyya. (MN 95 — M ii 174)

      • This is a conditional sentence with no conditional verb in it. What is marking the condition, what mood are both verbs in, and what does the pair 25–26 together tell you about how often you will meet the form you built in item 19?

    3. Atha kho bhagavā bhikkhū āmantesi: “handa dāni, bhikkhave, āmantayāmi vo, vayadhammā saṅkhārā, appamādena sampādetha.” (DN 16 — D ii 120)

      • Āmantesi and āmantayāmi are two lines apart and are one stem. What is the relation between the two shapes, and which chapter’s table has both of them in a single row?
      • Sampādetha closes the Buddha’s last words. What formation is it, what is it built on, and what is the mood?

    4. Evameva kho, bhikkhave, bhavissanti bhikkhū anāgatamaddhānaṁ, ye te suttantā tathāgatabhāsitā gambhīrā gambhīratthā lokuttarā suññatappaṭisaṁyuttā, tesu bhaññamānesu na sussūsissanti na sotaṁ odahissanti na aññā cittaṁ upaṭṭhāpessanti na ca te dhamme uggahetabbaṁ pariyāpuṇitabbaṁ maññissanti. (SN 20.7 — S ii 267)

      • Five of the verbs here are in the same tense. What is it, and how is it built?
      • Now look at what it is built on. Four different kinds of stem are carrying that one tense: name the kind in each case. This sentence is Chapter 9’s whole argument demonstrated in Chapter 8’s tense.
      • Maññissanti has the doubled consonant Chapter 9 warns about. Is it a passive?

    5. Uṭṭhehi vīra vijitasaṅgāma, satthavāha anaṇa vicara loke; desassu bhagavā dhammaṁ, aññātāro bhavissantī”ti. (SN 6.1 — S i 137)

      • Uṭṭhehi and vicara are the same mood in the same person and number. Why do they not look alike?
      • Desassu is the form to stop on. Parse it fully: formation of the stem, mood, person, number and voice.
      • The Syāmese, Cambodian and PTS editions print desetu here where the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints desassu. Parse that one too, and say what changes about who is being addressed.

    6. ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā uruvelāyaṁ viharati najjā nerañjarāya tīre ajapālanigrodhamūle paṭhamābhisambuddho. Atha kho bhagavato rahogatassa paṭisallīnassa evaṁ cetaso parivitakko udapādi … (SN 6.1 — S i 136)

      • Two consecutive sentences about the same afternoon, and the verbs are in two different tenses. Name them, and say why this is not an inconsistency.

    Put it back into Pāli. Each of these renders a real sentence. Reconstruct the Pāli. The words you need are given; what you have to supply is the stem and the ending. Do not settle for a sentence that merely works; the answer is a particular line of canon, and getting close to it is the exercise.

    1. “And if I were to teach this Dhamma, others would not understand me.”: ahaṁ, ca, eva, kho pana, dhamma, para, na, the enclitic for ‘me’, and the verbs ‘teach’ and ‘understand’ (ā-jān-), both in the optative.

    2. “Monks, do not fear merit.”: bhikkhave, puñña in the genitive plural, and the verb ‘fear’ in the construction of item 24.

    3. “Monks, I will teach you the burden and the bearer of the burden and the taking up of the burden and the laying down of the burden.”: bhāra, bhārahāra, bhārādāna, bhāranikkhepana, ca, bhikkhave, the enclitic for ‘to you’ (plural), and the verb in the future.

    Read at length. This is not a drill. Read the following passage through, twice, without stopping to parse: the first time for the build of the sentences, the second for the sense. Then go back and answer the questions under it.

    1. Atha kho brahmuno sahampatissa bhagavato cetasā cetoparivitakkamaññāya etadahosi: “nassati vata bho loko, vinassati vata bho loko, yatra hi nāma tathāgatassa arahato sammāsambuddhassa appossukkatāya cittaṁ namati, no dhammadesanāyā”ti. Atha kho brahmā sahampati—seyyathāpi nāma balavā puriso samiñjitaṁ vā bāhaṁ pasāreyya, pasāritaṁ vā bāhaṁ samiñjeyya; evameva—brahmaloke antarahito bhagavato purato pāturahosi. Atha kho brahmā sahampati ekaṁsaṁ uttarāsaṅgaṁ karitvā dakkhiṇajāṇumaṇḍalaṁ pathaviyaṁ nihantvā yena bhagavā tenañjaliṁ paṇāmetvā bhagavantaṁ etadavoca: “desetu, bhante, bhagavā dhammaṁ, desetu sugato dhammaṁ. Santi sattā apparajakkhajātikā, assavanatā dhammassa parihāyanti. Bhavissanti dhammassa aññātāro”ti. Idamavoca brahmā sahampati, idaṁ vatvā athāparaṁ etadavoca: (SN 6.1 — S i 137)

      • Brahmā’s speech runs present, imperative, future in three consecutive sentences: parihāyanti, desetu, bhavissanti. Set out what each tense or mood contributes to his argument. He is trying to change someone’s mind, and the sequence is how he does it.
      • The simile of the outstretched arm has two optatives in it, pasāreyya and samiñjeyya. Nobody is wishing for anything and no condition is being stated. What is the optative doing here?
      • Nassati and vinassati stand together, and Bodhi renders them “the world is lost!” and “the world is to perish”, neither of them a plain present. Which use of the present tense is that, and which chapter treats it?
      • Five of the finite verbs in this passage are aorists, and there are only two verbs between them. Find them, and say why a narrative in Pāli should be so heavily built out of two.

    Reaching back. Four items reaching into Parts I and II. Each is a place where what you learnt there and what you learnt here have to be held together.

    1. Chapter 2. Resolve the junctions in etadavoca, idamavoca, tenañjaliṁ and khomhi. One of the four is the reason a perfectly ordinary verb form cannot be found as a word at all. Say which, and say why looking for it inside a longer word does not rescue it either.

    2. Chapter 5. In item 27 the Buddha says āmantayāmi vo, and in item 31 he says pare ca me na ājāneyyuṁ. Neither vo nor me is the form a table would give you first. Say what they are, why they are preferred here, and what one rule of position they both obey.

    3. Chapter 6. At DN 16 — D ii 102 the Buddha says: Yassa kassaci, ānanda, cattāro iddhipādā bhāvitā bahulīkatā … so ākaṅkhamāno kappaṁ vā tiṭṭheyya kappāvasesaṁ vā. Which of the four constructions of Chapter 6 joins cattāro to what it counts? What mood is tiṭṭheyya, and what does that mood contribute to a claim about the Buddha’s lifespan that an indicative would not?

    4. Chapters 4 and 9. At DN 16 — D ii 120 the Buddha says: Ime kho te, bhikkhave, dhammā mayā abhiññā desitā. There is no finite verb in it. Say how the sentence works without one, what mayā contributes and why it is in the case it is in, and what desitā has to agree with.

    Coming back — the verb

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 1 to 5 aloud. Between them they hold the whole of the part: three chapters in one verse of Dhammapada, three participles doing a finite verb’s work, three formations under one ending, a middle where you expect an active, and the verse the Brahmāyācana was built towards. If these five come back, most of the recall group will answer itself.

    Day 10. Items 12 to 24. The ambiguous forms and the built ones together, and the reason to take them as one block is that they are the same skill in the two directions: item 13 asks what assa could be and item 20 asks you to make it. A form you can build and cannot recognise is a form you have not learnt, and so is the reverse.

    Day 30. Items 31 to 33, then 34, then 38. The three production items first, because they are the only place in the set where a wrong answer is unmistakable; then the long passage, read straight through; and then the last of the reaching-back items, which is a sentence with no finite verb in it at all. It is the right thing to close on, because by then you have spent a month on finite verbs and the canon keeps writing sentences without them.
