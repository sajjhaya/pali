    Exercises: Metre

    Scansion is the only part of Pāli grammar that can be done wholly by rule, and the fastest way to learn it is to do a great deal of it. So this set is weighted differently from the rest of the workbook: the recall group is short, and most of the items ask you to mark up a line and say what you find. The four stages still order it, but here the first stage hands you the marks instead of the gloss, because for this chapter the marks are the gloss.

    Write the marks above the line, syllable by syllable, before reading any further. ⏑ for a light syllable, — for a heavy one, × for the last syllable of a line, and ¦ where the line divides.

    Every line below is real canon, cited, and every one has been checked. Seven of the items contain a trap. They are not marked. Where you find one, say what the trap is as well as what the answer is. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — metre

    Nothing is withheld. The four lines of this stage are the Buddha’s first utterance after the awakening, standing at the head of the Vinaya, and they are given here already scanned. Recite each aloud against its marks until the marks stop being a diagram and start being the sound you are making.

    1. Yadā have pātubhavanti dhammā, (Kd 1 — Vin i 2)

      ⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×

      Say it aloud, slowly, letting each heavy syllable take its time. Then: five of the first ten syllables are heavy, and three different things have made them so. Sort the five into the three, and name the thing in each case. The one that catches people is the fourth.

    2. Ātāpino jhāyato brāhmaṇassa; (Kd 1 — Vin i 2)

      ——⏑—¦—⏑—¦—⏑—×

      Say it aloud. Every heavy syllable in this line but one is heavy for the same reason. Name that reason, name the exception and the reason for it, and say which two syllables would have caught you out if you had been marking from the shape of the letters. Then compare the marks with item 1’s: they differ in two places. Find both, and say which of the two matters and which does not.

    3. Athassa kaṅkhā vapayanti sabbā, (Kd 1 — Vin i 2)

      ⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×

      Say it aloud, then say item 1 aloud after it. The two lines have the same eleven marks and not one word in common. Say what that identity is evidence of, and (the harder half) what it is not evidence of. Line four of the verse has the same eleven marks again.

    4. Yato pajānāti sahetudhamman”ti. (Kd 1 — Vin i 2)

      ⏑—⏑—¦—⏑⏑¦—⏑—×

      Say it aloud. There are eleven marks and the line as printed has twelve syllables. Say which syllable has gone unmarked and why. Then, from the four lines together: how many syllables has each, are they all the same, and what does that settle about which of the four families this verse belongs to?

    Vacasā paricitā — metre

    Recite each line until it is fluent: four or five times, aloud, not silently. Then cover the letters and mark it from the sound alone, and only afterwards check your marks against the spelling. Say how many syllables you get.

    1. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    2. manoseṭṭhā manomayā (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

    3. Na hi verena verāni (Dhp 5 — Dhp p. 2)

    4. esa dhammo sanantano (Dhp 5 — Dhp p. 2)

    Manasānupekkhitā — metre

    Recall. Answer from memory, in a phrase, before opening the chapter.

    1. Four Pāli terms are in play and it is fatal to collapse them. Give the two that describe a vowel and the two that describe a syllable, and say which pair is about length and which about weight.

    2. State the three conditions that make a syllable heavy. Then state, in one clause, what makes a syllable light.

    3. E and o are the awkward vowels. Say what is variable about them, and say whether a syllable containing one is ever light by rule.

    4. Copy out the Vuttodaya’s two words for the two marks, and say why a Pāli grammar printed in Devanāgarī may look as though it has scanned every line backwards.

    5. What is a yati? Give the tradition’s gloss on the term, and say what it is not.

    6. The canon names a metre only once. Give the sutta, the word, and the sense the commentary assigns it.

    7. Name the four families of metre in Pāli, with the Pāli term for each, and say in a phrase what fixes the length of a line in each.

    8. What is the pādantagaru rule, and in which family of metres does it change the arithmetic?

    Scan the line. Mark each by the four rules alone, text uncovered but no other aid to hand. Do not apply any exception yet. Say how many syllables you get.

    1. Kālena dhammassavanaṁ (Kp 5)

    2. Sabbattha sotthiṁ gacchanti (Kp 5)

    3. Appaṁ vata jīvitaṁ idaṁ (Snp 4.6 — Sn p. 158)

    4. Satto guhāyaṁ bahunābhichanno (Snp 4.2 — Sn p. 151)

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — metre

    Now say what disturbed the count. Each of these gives the wrong number of syllables, or the wrong shape, when the four rules are applied as written. Say which accommodation is needed, and scan it again.

    1. Dānañca dhammacariyā ca (Kp 5)

    2. Nibbānasacchikiriyā ca (Kp 5)

    3. Tapo ca brahmacariyañca (Kp 5)

    4. ariyasaccāna dassanaṁ (Kp 5)

    5. Patirūpadesavāso ca (Kp 5)

    6. Taṁ tesaṁ maṅgalamuttamanti (Kp 5). Compare it with the refrain etaṁ maṅgalamuttamaṁ, which stands ten times in the same sutta.

    7. Tiṭṭhaṁ naro mohanasmiṁ pagāḷho (Snp 4.2 — Sn p. 151). This one scans to the right number. Something else about it is irregular; say what, and say whether it is a fault.

    Metre as the cause.

    1. Dhp 183 — Dhp p. 27 reads etaṁ buddhāna sāsanaṁ. Write out the line as it would stand with the genitive plural in full, scan both versions, and say what the missing niggahīta buys.

    2. Ānandajoti gives two examples of a form chosen “according to the needs of the metre”: daṭṭhu for disvā, and ghātvā for ghāyitvā. One of the two should not be taught. Say which, and which check settles it.

    3. A poet needs the final syllable of a word to be heavy and it is light. List the three kinds of change available, and say which of the three is the commonest in the texts and why it is nevertheless the hardest to demonstrate.

    4. Explain, in two sentences, why upekhā and upekkhā scan alike although the e is pronounced differently in each.

    Name the pattern.

    1. Here is the profile of the Siloka’s two lines:

      Pattern
      Odd (prior) line [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓ ⏓ ⏓ ¦ ⏑ — — ×
      Even (posterior) line [⏑/—/⏑⏑] ⏓ ⏓ ⏓ ¦ ⏑ — ⏑ ×

      One syllable distinguishes them. Say which, and say what would be lost if the two lines were identical.

    2. Scan Kp 5, Ākaṅkhamānā sotthānaṁ, and Kp 5, Sabbattha sotthiṁ gacchanti. Both depart from the pathyā in the same way. Name the variation, and give the further feature both lines have that the chapter says this variation normally takes.

    3. Scan Kp 5, Kālena dhammassavanaṁ. Name the variation. Then say why someone who had learnt only the pathyā would have concluded that three lines of the Maṅgalasutta were corrupt.

    4. These two lines stand next to each other in the same verse (Kp 6 and 6:8.3):

      Yathindakhīlo pathavissito siyā
      Tathūpamaṁ sappurisaṁ vadāmi

      Scan both. They are in different metres. Name each, say what the difference between them amounts to, and say whether the change is a fault.

    5. A line runs ⏓—⏑—⏑⏑⏑—×. Two different analyses fit the marks. Name both, and say what single piece of evidence, not in the marks, decides between them.

    What the evidence licenses. Four items that hand you the evidence and ask what follows from it. Each answer is a judgement, not a lookup.

    1. Ānandajoti scans Kp 6 with abhabbŏ, the final -o read light against its nature. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana prints Chaccābhiṭhānāni abhabba kātuṁ, and its apparatus records abhabbo against the siglum sī., the Sinhalese edition; the Buddha Jayanti and the Pali Text Society both print abhabbo. Say what happens to the licence, and then what the case shows about claims of metrical licence in general.

    2. The metre this book calls the Siloka has another name. In the Vuttodaya vatta stands six times, and the treatise’s section on the metre is headed Vattappabhedo, “the varieties of Vatta”; the two apparent occurrences of siloka are both Visiloka, which is a different metre. Say which name the tradition uses. Then say why this book uses the other one anyway.

    3. Tuṭṭhubha does not occur anywhere in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, not even inside a longer word, so its absence is not an artefact of the form it was looked up in. The Vuttodaya, meanwhile, heads a section Tiṭṭhubhā (v. 74). Explain the two findings together, and say what they mean about the name in general use.

    4. You scanned esa dhammo sanantano at item 8, from the Dhammapada. The same line stands in the Vinaya, at the close of the Kosambī quarrel, and there it is printed as one word fewer:

      Averena ca sammanti, esadhammo sanantano. (Kd 10 — Vin i 349)

      Scan the second half of it as the Vinaya prints it, and compare your marks with item 8’s. Then answer the question the comparison raises: can the metre decide between the two divisions? Say what it can settle and what it cannot, and name the one feature of a line where a word division does become metrical evidence. Then say which way that feature falls here.

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. This is the reverse of everything above: instead of taking a line apart, make one. The second line of the Kosambī verse is a Siloka even line of eight syllables, and you met the whole verse in the present-system set, where it was read for its verbs.

      Take the two words sammanti, “they are appeased”, and idha, “here”, and put them before kudācanaṁ. Written out plainly that is nine syllables and will not scan. Say what single operation reduces it to eight, write out the line as it stands in the canon, and check yourself against Kd 10 — Vin i 349. Then decide: is the operation one of the three kinds of metrical change you listed at item 30, or is it something else? Answer that, and say what the answer implies about how much of a verse’s form the metre is responsible for.

    The reading passage.

    1. Scan the whole of Dhp 1 (six lines), marking the caesura where you hear one.

      Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā,
      manoseṭṭhā manomayā;
      Manasā ce paduṭṭhena,
      bhāsati vā karoti vā;
      Tato naṁ dukkhamanveti,
      cakkaṁva vahato padaṁ. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)

      Then answer three questions about it. How many syllables has each line, and what does that tell you about the metre? Which lines are odd lines and which even, and how do you know from the marks alone, not from the position? And a Siloka verse normally has four lines — say what has happened here, and whether the chapter licenses it.

    Coming back — metre

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 5–8 and 17–20. Eight lines is enough scanning to make the four rules automatic, and until they are automatic nothing in stage 4 can be attempted. Four of the eight carry a trap, so a clean second pass means the rules have displaced the rules of thumb.

    Day 10. Items 21–27, and item 34. This is the whole of what disturbs a count, and the reason to come back to it as a block is that the accommodations are only learnable by contrast: sarabhatti, resolution, a lost niggahīta and a quotation marker outside the metre all look identical from the syllable count alone.

    Day 30. Items 1–4 aloud, then items 40 and 41. If the first udāna still comes back whole, with its marks, then a Tuṭṭhubha is in the ear and not merely on the page, and the two Vinaya items test whether you can now use the instrument on a question it does not settle as well as on one it does.
