    Front matter

    Pāli: A Workbook

    Version 1.2 · 17 November 2026, for use with the same version of the grammar.

    This is the companion volume to Pāli: A Reference Grammar. It carries the drills and their worked answers, one set per chapter, in the grammar’s order. Nothing here introduces a rule that the grammar does not already state; if an exercise seems to require something you have not met, the chapter it belongs to is where to look.

    The two volumes are numbered alike: the exercises for chapter 6 of the grammar are “Exercises: Numerals” here, and the worked answers for them are “Answers: Numerals” at the back. Nothing needs cross-referencing beyond the chapter’s name.

    Read the answer only after attempting the exercise. The point is practical, not moral: the answers are discursive on purpose, and several of them turn on a distinction that only becomes visible once you have got it wrong. An answer read cold teaches a fact. An answer read after an attempt teaches the reason the fact is worth having.

    Every Pāli sentence in this volume is canonical, and cited. None has been composed for the exercise. That constraint has a cost, since a drill made of real sentences cannot isolate one grammatical point as cleanly as an invented one, and it is worth paying, because a sentence you parse here is a sentence of the Buddha’s word that you have read, not a demonstration of one.

    The answers give a word-by-word gloss, an idiomatic rendering, and then a short discussion naming the classes at work. In print the gloss is set as the grammar sets it, each Pāli word above its English and its grammar tag; the grammar’s “How to Use This Book” explains the layout. Where an exercise reuses a sentence from the grammar’s exposition, the answer says so: meeting a sentence again with no surrounding prose to lean on is different work from meeting it the first time, and it is deliberate.

    How a set is arranged

    Each set runs in four stages, and the four come from the canon. A monk masters the teaching, and four things are then said of what he has mastered: it is sotānugata, “followed by ear”; vacasā paricita, “rehearsed aloud”; manasānupekkhita, “gone over in the mind”; and diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdha, “well penetrated by view” (AN 4.191 — A ii 185). These are four operations, not four intensities of one: exposure, recitation, recall without the text, and analysis, and the order is theirs, not mine. Understanding comes fourth. The grammar’s “How to study a classical language” prints the passage in full with its gloss and argues the point; what the formula does here is decide what a set asks first and what it asks last.

    Stage What its items ask Difficulty
    Sotānugata — met by ear The line is given, with its gloss. Read it aloud, mark the long vowels and the doubled consonants, and say what the marked form is. easy
    Vacasā paricitā — rehearsed aloud Recite from the Pāli; then rebuild the line from the gloss alone. easy to moderate
    Manasānupekkhitā — gone over in the mind Text covered. Recall the cell, the form, the rule — including from chapters already finished. moderate
    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — penetrated by view Adjudicate an ambiguity, split a compound, decide what the evidence licenses, put English back into Pāli. difficult

    Nothing is withheld in stage 1, so an item there cannot be got wrong for want of information, by design: the first meeting with a sentence is a meeting and not a test. Stage 2 is aloud throughout. Vowel length and the doubled consonants are half the morphology of this language, and on the page they are a single mark. Bhikkhunā and bhikkhūnaṁ are the instrumental singular and the genitive plural of one word, they each turn up a few hundred times in the four Nikāyas, and what separates them is one long vowel. Said aloud, they are not the same word at all.

    Stage 3 holds the short form-drills, the ones that give an ending and ask what it is, and also the items reaching back to earlier chapters. In stage 4 the set stops being a drill. At least one of its items asks you to produce Pāli instead of parsing it, a harder task and a different one.

    The sub-headings inside the stages (Recall, Identify, Split the compound, Read at length) say what each small group of items does. They are the same group names the mature sets have always used.

    The difficulty ordering is measured, not felt. A drill is hard or easy largely by how often its reader has already met the words it turns on, and that is a countable thing: across the four Nikāyas, the five hundred commonest word-forms account for half of all running words, and the ten thousand commonest for a little under seven-eighths. Stage 1 works with forms from the head of that curve, stage 4 from well down it. The count cannot show whether the rule an item drills is difficult, and that stays a judgement.

    Coming back

    Every set ends with a short Coming back list, naming which of its items to re-attempt after three days, after ten, and after thirty.

    The reason is the best-attested finding in the literature on how adults study, and it contradicts what most people actually do. Of ten study techniques reviewed by Dunlosky and colleagues, only two were rated of high utility: testing yourself, and spreading the sessions out. A later synthesis of 242 studies and 169,179 participants ranked the same two first again, and found that the techniques learners actually prefer, rereading and highlighting, are among the least effective of the ten.1 Retrieval beats re-study by about half a standard deviation.2 So a set worked through once on the day the chapter was read has been enjoyed, not learned.

    The three intervals are a practical choice and not a finding. What the evidence supports is that the sessions be separated at all, with the gaps lengthening as the material holds; three, ten and thirty days is one workable implementation of that and there is nothing sacred in the numbers. Missing one is not a failure. Doing the whole set again in one sitting the night before you want it is.

    The tradition arrived here first, and by a different road. Sajjhāya, “recitation, rehearsal”, was the technique that carried this canon for the centuries before it was written down, and it consists of recall from memory, daily, sustained over years: retrieval and spacing at once. The grammar makes the same observation and adds the caution that goes with it, which holds here too: nobody in that tradition was doing cognitive psychology, and that the practice turns out to be well designed is a happy accident, not an argument.

    What the method rests on

    A workbook that grades its exercises and schedules their review owes the reader an account of where the grading and the schedule came from. This is that account, briefly; the grammar’s chapter carries the evidence in full.

    Four considerations shaped this volume. The stages are canonical: AN 4.191, quoted above, read as four operations in a fixed order. Retrieval and spacing are why the answers sit in a separate half of the book, not beneath each question, and why every set schedules its own review.3 4 Production is why each set now ends by asking for Pāli, not about it: Swain’s work on French-immersion learners found that thousands of hours of comprehensible input still left persistent gaps in grammar, which is a finding about what mere reading does not do.5 And volume of reading is why the drills are not the destination. Two meta-analyses of extensive reading report medium to large gains in comprehension, rate and vocabulary, with adults gaining more than children; the practical consequence is that Appendix B’s reading pathway should be begun when Part IV is done and not when the book is finished.6 Nation’s threshold of about 98% of running words known is why that pathway is graded, and why the sets here are too.7

    None of this is imported wholesale. The four-stage spine is the canon’s, the measurements behind the grading are of the canon, and the modern literature does one job: it says which of the several defensible ways of arranging a workbook is likely to reward the hours you put into it.
