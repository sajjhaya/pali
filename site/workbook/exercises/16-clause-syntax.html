    Exercise · Chapter 16

    Exercises: Clause and Sentence Syntax

    This chapter’s claim is that Pāli subordinates by case and by participle, not by conjunction, and that the reader has to supply the relation the language leaves unstated. So the set is arranged around the one decision that governs everything else, whose action this is, and only then asks what the relation is.

    Every sentence below is real canon, and cited. Stage 1 is four passages in which the subordination is already named for you, and the first three are chosen so that one thing changes at a time. Three of the later items contain a trap. They are not marked. Where you find one, say what the trap is as well as what the answer is. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — clause and sentence syntax

    Nothing is withheld. Each passage comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear where one clause stops and the next begins.

    1. Sīho, bhikkhave, migarājā sāyanhasamayaṁ āsayā nikkhamati. Āsayā nikkhamitvā vijambhati. Vijambhitvā samantā catuddisā anuviloketi. Samantā catuddisā anuviloketvā tikkhattuṁ sīhanādaṁ nadati. (AN 4.33 — A ii 33)

      sīhothe lionbhikkhavemonksmigarājāking of beastssāyanhasamayaṁin the eveningm acc sg, adverbialāsayāfrom his lairm abl sgnikkhamaticomes outnikkhamitvāhaving come outvijambhatihe stretchesvijambhitvāhaving stretchedsamantā catuddisāin all four directionsanuviloketihe surveysanuviloketvāhaving surveyedtikkhattuṁthree timessīhanādaṁa lion’s roarm acc sgnadatihe roars

      “The lion, monks, the king of beasts, comes out from his lair in the evening. Having come out from his lair, he stretches. Having stretched, he surveys the four directions all around. Having surveyed the four directions all around, he roars a lion’s roar three times.”

      Say it aloud, all four sentences, without stopping between them. Each one opens by repeating in the absolutive what the sentence before ended with as a finite verb. Say what single condition licenses the absolutive throughout, and what would have had to change in the Pāli if the lion had not been the one doing all four things. Then say what the pattern is for. It has a purpose, and the purpose has more to do with how a passage was carried than with how it was parsed.

    2. Parinibbute bhagavati saha parinibbānā mahābhūmicālo ahosi bhiṁsanako salomahaṁso. (DN 16 — D ii 156)

      parinibbutefully extinguishedm loc sg, past participlebhagavatithe Blessed Onem loc sgsaha parinibbānātogether with the full extinguishmentmahābhūmicāloa great earthquakem nom sgahosithere was3 sg aorbhiṁsanakoterrifyingsalomahaṁsohair-raising

      “When the Blessed One had attained final Nibbāna, there was, together with the final Nibbāna, a great earthquake, terrifying and hair-raising.”

      Say it aloud. No word in this sentence means ‘when’. Say what carries it. Then say what the tense of the participle settles about the order of the two events, and what would have been settled differently had the participle been a present one. The next item is that sentence.

    3. Imasmiñca pana veyyākaraṇasmiṁ bhaññamāne āyasmato koṇḍaññassa virajaṁ vītamalaṁ dhammacakkhuṁ udapādi. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

      imasmiṁthisn loc sgveyyākaraṇasmiṁexpositionn loc sgbhaññamānebeing spokenn loc sg, present participle of the passive bhaññati, from bhaṇatiāyasmato koṇḍaññassaof the venerable Koṇḍaññam gen sgvirajaṁdust-freevītamalaṁstainlessdhammacakkhuṁthe eye of the Dhamman nom sgudapādiarose3 sg aor

      “And while this exposition was being spoken, the dust-free, stainless eye of the Dhamma arose in the venerable Koṇḍañña.”

      Say it aloud. This is item 2’s construction with one thing changed. Name the thing, and say what it changes about the timing of the two events. Then say what makes the pair worth learning together, not separately: between them they carry a rule the chapter states in one line and never has to repeat.

    4. Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

      pavattiteset rollingn loc sg, past partbhagavatāby the Blessed Onem ins sgdhammacakkethe wheel of Dhamman loc sgbhummā devāthe earth-dwelling godsm nom plsaddaṁa crym acc sganussāvesuṁraised3 pl aor

      “And when the wheel of Dhamma had been set rolling by the Blessed One, the earth-dwelling gods raised a cry.”

      Say it aloud, immediately after item 3; the two stand one sentence apart in the same sutta. This is item 2’s construction again, with one word added. Name the word and its case. Then say why the grammatical subject of the subordinate clause and its logical subject are standing in two different cases, and which of the two a reader should follow to find out who did the thing.

    Vacasā paricitā — clause and sentence syntax

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

    1. Yā pana bhikkhunī lasuṇaṁ khādeyya, pācittiyaṁ. (Bi Pc 1 — Vin iv 259)

      yāwhateverf nom sgpana(filler)bhikkhunīnunf nom sglasuṇaṁgarlicn acc sgkhādeyyashould eat3 sg optpācittiyaṁan offence requiring confessionn nom sg

      “Whatever nun should eat garlic: an offence requiring confession.”

      Six words, and most of the Pātimokkha is built on this frame. Recite it and rebuild it from the gloss. Then: there is no sace, no ce and no yadi, and the second half has no verb in it. Say what is carrying the ‘if’ and what is carrying the ‘then’. Then say what yā pana bhikkhunī gives the rule that a name or a plural would not, and which chapter of this book that word belongs to.

    2. No cetaṁ, bhikkhave, abhavissa ajātaṁ abhūtaṁ akataṁ asaṅkhataṁ, nayidha jātassa bhūtassa katassa saṅkhatassa nissaraṇaṁ paññāyetha. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 80)

      no ceif notetaṁthisn nom sgabhavissawere to be3 sg condajātaṁunbornabhūtaṁunbecomeakataṁunmadeasaṅkhataṁunconditionedall n nom sgna idhanot herejātassaof the bornn gen sgnissaraṇaṁescapen nom sgpaññāyethawould be discerned3 sg opt mid of the passive paññāyati

      “If there were no unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, no escape from the born, become, made and conditioned would be discerned here.”

      Recite it until it is fluent, then rebuild it from the gloss. Name the type of conditional and give the mood of each verb. Here, and almost nowhere else in the language, a reader regularly meets the conditional tense, and the reason has to be stated: say what the sentence would be asserting if the moods were indicative instead, and why that would be the opposite of the doctrine.

    3. The sentence above is followed immediately by another. Say what that second sentence does to the first, and what construction it uses to do it. (Ud 8.3 — Ud p. 81)

    Manasānupekkhitā — clause and sentence syntax

    Recall. Answer from memory, in a phrase, before opening the chapter.

    1. You meet a chain of actions with only one finite verb at the end. What form are the other verbs in, and what one condition has to hold for the language to be allowed to do that?

    2. Give the rule that decides between an absolutive and a locative absolute. One sentence.

    3. Which two participles can stand as the predicate of an absolute construction, and which two cannot? Name the exception the chapter records to that rule.

    4. The tradition has no word for ‘absolute construction’. What does it have instead, and which two case-endings does its rule mention?

    5. Name the three types of conditional sentence by the moods they use, and say which of the three is the only regular home of the conditional tense.

    6. A sentence has no verb in it. Name the two verbs that are being left out, and say what happens to gender concord when a noun stands where the verb would be.

    Whose action is it? For each sentence, say whether the bolded form belongs to the subject of the main verb or to somebody else, and name the construction accordingly.

    1. Atha kho bhagavā pubbaṇhasamayaṁ nivāsetvā pattacīvaramādāya sāvatthiṁ piṇḍāya pāvisi. (MN 26 — M i 160)

    2. Atha kho māro pāpimā acirapakkante āyasmante ānande yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami. (DN 16 — D ii 104)

    3. So kho ahaṁ … akāmakānaṁ mātāpitūnaṁ assumukhānaṁ rudantānaṁ kesamassuṁ ohāretvā … agārasmā anagāriyaṁ pabbajiṁ. (MN 26 — M i 163)

    4. Addasā kho nigrodho paribbājako sandhānaṁ gahapatiṁ dūratova āgacchantaṁ. (DN 25 — D iii 37)

    5. So kho ahaṁ, bhikkhave, aparena samayena daharova samāno susukāḷakeso … pabbajiṁ. (MN 26 — M i 163)

    6. Te jātisaṁvattanikepi saṅkhāre abhisaṅkharitvā … jātipapātampi papatanti. (SN 56.42 — S v 449)

    Name the construction and its sense. Say what the construction is, and which of its senses is at work here: time, attendant circumstance, cause, condition, hypothesis or concession.

    1. Jātiyā sati jarāmaraṇaṁ hotī ti. (SN 12.49 — S ii 78)

    2. Deve vassante deve gaḷagaḷāyante vijjullatāsu niccharantīsu asaniyā phalantiyā neva passeyya, na pana saddaṁ suṇeyya. (DN 16 — D ii 131)

    3. Atha kho, bhikkhave, rājā khattiyo muddhābhisitto dibbe cakkaratane antarahite anattamano ahosi. (DN 26)

    4. Atha kho brāhmaṇo pokkharasāti … yāne āropetvā ukkāsu dhāriyamānāsu ukkaṭṭhāya niyyāsi. (DN 3 — D i 108)

    5. Dassane, bhagavā, sati kathaṁ paṭipajjitabban ti? (DN 16 — D ii 141)

    6. Āturakāyassa me sato cittaṁ anāturaṁ bhavissatī ti. (SN 22.1 — S iii 1)

    7. Seyyathāpi, ānanda, gahapatissa vā gahapatiputtassa vā manuññaṁ bhojanaṁ bhuttāvissa bhattasammado hoti. (DN 17 — D ii 195)

    8. Hatthesu, bhikkhave, sati ādānanikkhepanaṁ paññāyati; pādesu sati abhikkamapaṭikkamo paññāyati. (SN 35.236 — S iv 171)

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — clause and sentence syntax

    The sentence types.

    1. Sace maṁ bhagavā anujāneyya, āgaccheyyāhaṁ imaṁ ambavanaṁ padhānāya. (AN 9.3 — A iv 355) — which type, and how do you know?

    2. Āditte, bhikkhave, cele vā sīse vā kimassa karaṇīyan ti? (SN 56.34 — S v 440) — there is no sace and no ce here. What is carrying the condition, and would you translate it ‘when’ or ‘if’?

    3. Appamādo amatapadaṁ, pamādo maccuno padaṁ. (Dhp 21 — Dhp p. 4) — what is missing, and what has happened to the concord?

    4. ‘Pasaṁsā me āgacchatū’ ti sīlaṁ rakkheyya paṇḍito. (Iti 76 — It p. 67) — the sentence ends a quotation with ti and nobody is speaking. What is the ti for?

    5. Seyyathāpi, bhante, purisassa agāraṁ ekādasadvāraṁ, so tasmiṁ agāre āditte ekamekenapi dvārena sakkuṇeyya attānaṁ sotthiṁ kātuṁ. (MN 52 — M i 353) — name the three constructions in this one sentence and say why the verb is optative.

    6. Atthi nu kho, bhante, añño koci mayā upaṭṭhākataro ti? (MN 81 — M ii 51) — two things mark this as a question. Name both, and say which of them a reader should trust.

    Adjudicate.

    1. Santaṁ yeva kho pana paraṁ lokaṁ ‘natthi paro loko’ ti saṅkappeti. (MN 60 — M i 402) — name the construction, and say what evidence the chapter offers that the tradition recognised it.

    2. Santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ byāpādaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ byāpādo’ ti pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 60) — this looks like the item above and two good grammars read it differently. Give both readings, and say what would settle it.

    3. The Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana and the Buddha Jayanti print Saṁvaṭṭamāne loke yebhuyyena sattā ābhassarasaṁvattanikā honti at DN 1 — D i 17; the Pali Text Society prints Saṁvaṭṭamāno loko at the same place. Three grammars build a grammatical category on the second reading. What is the category, and what should a reader conclude?

    4. Kaccāyana’s vutti on the anādara rule gives two sentences side by side: Rudato dārakassa pabbaji, rudantasmiṁ dārake pabbaji. Say what each is, why they are printed together, and one reason a reader should not take either as an example of canonical Pāli.

    Read the sentence. Translate, then say what each construction is contributing.

    1. Yatra hi nāma tathāgato atīte buddhe parinibbute … jātitopi anussarissati, nāmatopi anussarissati, gottatopi anussarissati. (DN 14 — D ii 8)

    The case law. The Vibhaṅga’s case reports are short, and each packs several of this chapter’s constructions into one sentence because it has to say who did what and in what order before it can decide anything.

    1. Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu saṅghassa odane bhājīyamāne—“aparassa bhāgaṁ dehī”ti amūlakaṁ aggahesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 59) — Gloss it and render it. Three of this chapter’s constructions are here. Name them, and say which of the three tells you that the monk was not the one distributing the rice.

    The reading passage. Ānanda asks the Buddha, shortly before the parinibbāna, how monks are to conduct themselves towards women. The exchange is short and turns entirely on this chapter’s constructions.

    1. Read DN 16 — D ii 141 and translate it. Then answer three questions about it in prose, not in a list: which construction carries the second and third questions and what its sense is there; why sati has the ending it has, given the case of the word it goes with; and what the whole exchange demonstrates about how much of a Pāli sentence can be left unsaid.

    Coming back — clause and sentence syntax

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 14–19. Whose action is it is the decision this chapter exists to make automatic, and it is prior to every other question in the set: get it wrong and the sense of the construction cannot be right by accident. Two of the six carry a trap.

    Day 10. Items 20–27, and item 39. In naming the sense of an absolute a reader stops reading grammar and starts reading the passage. Nothing in the words says cause or concession, and in item 39 getting it right decides a case at law.

    Day 30. Items 1 to 4 aloud, and item 40. If the three locative absolutes still sort themselves by the tense of the participle without your going back to the rule, the chapter’s central claim has stopped being a rule and become the way you read.
