    Exercise · Chapter 11

    Exercises: Absolutives and Infinitives

    Both formations in this chapter are indeclinable, and that one fact generates most of the work below. An absolutive cannot agree with anything, so it cannot tell you whose action it names, and the reader supplies that from the main verb, except in the one construction where the default fails. An infinitive cannot decline either, so Pāli reaches for a dative noun where English would reach for “to”.

    The set drills two things in parallel: recognising the forms, which is mechanical, and deciding what they attach to, which is not. Stage 1 is four lines in which an absolutive or an infinitive carries the sentence, and in stage 4 the agent question gets decided, not assumed. Every Pāli line below is canonical and cited; none has been composed. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — absolutives and infinitives

    Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear what the non-finite form is attached to.

    1. Idhāvuso, bhikkhu satthāraṁ upanissāya viharati aññataraṁ vā garuṭṭhāniyaṁ sabrahmacāriṁ, yatthassa tibbaṁ hirottappaṁ paccupaṭṭhitaṁ hoti pemañca gāravo ca. (DN 34 — D iii 284)

      idhahereāvusofriendvocbhikkhua monkm nom sgsatthāraṁthe Teacherm acc sgupanissāyadepending on, close toabs as postposition, + accviharatidwellsaññataraṁ vā garuṭṭhāniyaṁor some other one standing in a teacher’s placem acc sgsabrahmacāriṁa fellow monkm acc sgyattha·assain whom, for himtibbaṁkeenn acc sghirottappaṁconscience and shamen acc sgpaccupaṭṭhitaṁestablishedn acc sg, pphotiispemañca gāravo caand affection and respect

      “Here, friend, a monk lives depending on the Teacher, or on some other fellow monk standing in a teacher’s place, in whom keen conscience and shame are present, and affection and respect.”

      Say it aloud. One word here is an absolutive that has stopped behaving like one. Find it, say what verb it comes from, and then say what it governs, and why that is not what an absolutive normally does.

    2. Kicchena me adhigataṁ, halaṁ dāni pakāsituṁ; Rāgadosaparetehi, nāyaṁ dhammo susambudho. (SN 6.1 — S i 136)

      kicchenawith difficultyadv, ins sgmeby me1 sg insadhigataṁattainedn nom sg, ppha·laṁ< hi indeed + alaṁ enough, no needdāninowpakāsituṁto revealinfrāga·dosa·paretehiby those overwhelmed by greed and hatem ins plna·ayaṁnot thism nom sgdhammoteachingm nom sgsusambudhoeasily understoodm nom sg

      “This that I have attained with difficulty — there is no need to reveal it now. This teaching is not easily understood by those overwhelmed by greed and hate.”

      The Buddha’s hesitation after the awakening, and the reason the Brahmā request in set 09 had to be made. Say it aloud. The second line rests on its infinitive, and the infinitive has no expressed agent at all. Say what the form is and what verb it comes from. Then say what English has to supply that the Pāli does without, and notice that adhigataṁ in the line above does have its agent, in a case set 09 will have taught you to expect.

    3. Atha kho te bhikkhū sāvatthiyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkantā yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkamiṁsu; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavantaṁ abhivādetvā ekamantaṁ nisīdiṁsu. (AN 10.27 — A v 49)

      atha khothentethosem nom plbhikkhūmonksm nom plsāvatthiyaṁin Sāvatthīf loc sgpiṇḍāyafor almsm dat sgcaritvāhaving wanderedabspacchābhattaṁafter the mealpiṇḍapātapaṭikkantāreturned from the almsroundm nom plyena … tenato wherebhagavāthe Blessed Onem nom sgupasaṅkamiṁsuthey approachedupasaṅkamitvāhaving approachedabsbhagavantaṁthe Blessed Onem acc sgabhivādetvāhaving paid homageabsekamantaṁto one sidenisīdiṁsuthey sat down

      “Then those monks, having wandered for alms in Sāvatthī and returned from the almsround after the meal, approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to him, they sat down to one side.”

      Set 08 took this sentence for its aorists. Here it is for the three words between them. Say it aloud. Three absolutives and two finite verbs, and all five have the same subject. That makes the sentence readable at speed, and it is the default the chapter states. Say which word gives you the subject, and how many times it is expressed.

    4. Na dāni sukaraṁ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke pariccajituṁ. (MN 76 — M i 524)

      nanotdāninowsukaraṁeasyn nom sgamhehiby us1 pl inslābha·sakkāra·silokegain, honour and renownm acc plpariccajituṁto give upinf

      “It is not easy for us now to give up gain, honour and renown.”

      Say it aloud. Name the case of the infinitive’s agent, and say why it is not the nominative. The sentence has no grammatical subject at all, which is the point, and the case that steps in is the one set 09’s passives use for theirs.

    Vacasā paricitā — absolutives and infinitives

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

    1. Sakkā bhoge ca bhuñjituṁ puññāni ca kātuṁ. (AN 4.122 — A ii 125)

      Yannūnāhaṁ sikkhaṁ paccakkhāya hīnāyāvattitvā bhoge ca bhuñjeyyaṁ puññāni ca kareyyanti. (AN 4.122)

      sakkāit is possibleindbhogepossessionsm acc plcaandbhuñjituṁto enjoyinfpuññānimeritorious deedsn acc plkātuṁto doinfyaṁ nūna ahaṁwhat if Isikkhaṁthe trainingf acc sgpaccakkhāyahaving renouncedabshīnāyato the lower lifem dat sgāvattitvāhaving turned backabsbhuñjeyyaṁI might enjoy1 sg optkareyyaṁI might do1 sg optti(quotation)

      “It is possible to enjoy possessions and to make merit. What if I were to renounce the training, return to the lower life, and enjoy possessions and make merit?”

      Two consecutive sentences, and both are one man talking himself out of the robe. Recite them in order, then rebuild them from the gloss. The same two verbs, bhuñjati and karoti, stand in both sentences and never once in the same form. Say what form each takes in each line, and then say what changes between the first sentence and the second when the verbs change: the facts do not move, and something else does.

    2. Bhagavāpi, bhante, etarahi arahaṁ sammāsambuddho pañca nīvaraṇe pahāya cetaso upakkilese paññāya dubbalīkaraṇe catūsu satipaṭṭhānesu supatiṭṭhitacitto satta bojjhaṅge yathābhūtaṁ bhāvetvā anuttaraṁ sammāsambodhiṁ abhisambuddho’ti. (DN 16 — D ii 83)

      bhagavā·pithe Blessed One toobhantevenerable sirvocetarahinowarahaṁ sammāsambuddhothe worthy one, the fully awakened onem nom sgpañca nīvaraṇethe five hindrancesm acc plpahāyahaving abandonedabscetaso upakkilesethe defilements of the mindm acc plpaññāya dubbalīkaraṇethat weaken wisdomm acc plcatūsu satipaṭṭhānesuin the four applications of mindfulnessn loc plsupatiṭṭhitacittowith mind well establishedm nom sgsatta bojjhaṅgethe seven awakening factorsm acc plyathābhūtaṁas they really arebhāvetvāhaving developedabsanuttaraṁ sammāsambodhiṁunsurpassed full awakeningf acc sgabhisambuddhohas fully awakenedm nom sg, ppti(quotation)

      “And the Blessed One too, venerable sir, who is at present the worthy one, the fully awakened one — having abandoned the five hindrances, the defilements of the mind that weaken wisdom, with mind well established in the four applications of mindfulness, having truly developed the seven awakening factors — has fully awakened to unsurpassed full awakening.”

      One sentence of Sāriputta’s declaration of faith, and item 27 is the whole of it. Rebuild it from the gloss; it is long, formulaic, and much easier the fourth time. Two absolutives, and the predicate at the far end is not a finite verb at all. Find all three, and say what holds the sentence together in the absence of a finite verb.

    Manasānupekkhitā — absolutives and infinitives

    Cover the chapter. These are answered from memory or not at all.

    Recall.

    1. Absolutives take no case ending. What part of speech does this make them, grammatically, and what does it mean for how they agree (or fail to agree) with the noun they might seem to describe?

    2. State the default rule for whose action an absolutive names, and then state the one construction the chapter documents where that default does not hold.

    3. Name the absolutive’s two main suffix types, and the sound change that regularly turns the second of them into -cca.

    4. The gerund and the gerundive differ by one letter and mean entirely different things. What is each, and which chapter teaches the other?

    5. Pāli reaches for the infinitive less often than English reaches for “to.” Why?

    Identify the formation.

    1. gantvā
    2. paccakkhāya
    3. jahetvā — one of hitvā, jahitvā and jahetvā is not actually printed anywhere in the root texts. Which, and how would you check?
    4. desetuṁ
    5. cassa, in paññāya cassa disvā āsavā parikkhīṇā honti
    6. amhehi, in na dāni sukaraṁ amhehi lābhasakkārasiloke pariccajituṁ
    7. nissāya
    8. sakkā

    Build the form.

    1. The -tvā absolutive of bhāveti ‘develops’.
    2. The infinitive of bhāveti.
    3. The -ya absolutive of pajahati ‘abandons’ (already met once in this chapter; name it here from the rule, not from memory).
    4. The infinitive of suṇāti ‘hears’.
    5. The -tvā absolutive of dadāti ‘gives’.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — absolutives and infinitives

    Put it back into Pāli.

    1. “It is not easy to teach the Dhamma to others.”

    2. “Having heard that teaching, he gains faith in the Realized One.”

    Read at length.

    1. DN 16 — D ii 83, the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta’s account of Sāriputta’s declaration of faith, all three sentences together:

      Ye te, bhante, ahesuṁ atītamaddhānaṁ arahanto sammāsambuddhā, sabbe te bhagavanto pañca nīvaraṇe pahāya cetaso upakkilese paññāya dubbalīkaraṇe catūsu satipaṭṭhānesu supatiṭṭhitacittā sattabojjhaṅge yathābhūtaṁ bhāvetvā anuttaraṁ sammāsambodhiṁ abhisambujjhiṁsu. Yepi te, bhante, bhavissanti anāgatamaddhānaṁ arahanto sammāsambuddhā, sabbe te bhagavanto pañca nīvaraṇe pahāya cetaso upakkilese paññāya dubbalīkaraṇe catūsu satipaṭṭhānesu supatiṭṭhitacittā satta bojjhaṅge yathābhūtaṁ bhāvetvā anuttaraṁ sammāsambodhiṁ abhisambujjhissanti. Bhagavāpi, bhante, etarahi arahaṁ sammāsambuddho pañca nīvaraṇe pahāya cetaso upakkilese paññāya dubbalīkaraṇe catūsu satipaṭṭhānesu supatiṭṭhitacitto satta bojjhaṅge yathābhūtaṁ bhāvetvā anuttaraṁ sammāsambodhiṁ abhisambuddho’ti.

      Translate the passage, then answer:

      • The same two absolutives, pahāya and bhāvetvā, appear in all three sentences, governed each time by a different finite verb. Name the three finite verbs and their tenses.
      • What single grammatical fact makes it possible to write one long formula three times with only the finite verb (and its supporting participle) changing?
      • Supatiṭṭhitacittā is a compound built partly on participial material of the kind Chapter 10 taught. Which element in it is that, and what does it contribute?
      • Why might a sutta choose to say this three times (past buddhas, future buddhas, this Buddha) instead of once?

    Whose action is it. The chapter says an absolutive’s agent is ordinarily the subject of the main verb, and then documents the place where that fails. Both sentences are now in front of you.

    1. Set item 3 beside this:

      Paññāya cassa disvā āsavā parikkhīṇā honti. (MN 25 — M i 160)

      In item 3, whose action is caritvā, and how do you know? In this sentence, whose action is disvā? Say what in the second sentence makes the default reading impossible. The test is a question about what the nominative subject could plausibly have done, not a rule about word order. Then say which word supplies the real agent, and why its case cannot be settled from its form.

    Reaching back.

    1. Chapter 8 taught the optative as the ordinary vehicle for a conditional apodosis. Mañhi, bhante, aññatitthiyā sāvakaṁ labhitvā kevalakappaṁ nāḷandaṁ paṭākaṁ parihareyyuṁ (MN 56 — M i 379) is a conditional sentence with no conditional-mood verb in it at all. What serves as the protasis instead, and what carries the apodosis?

    2. Chapter 10 gave the transitive/intransitive rule for the past participle’s voice. Vatvāna, this chapter’s absolutive of vadati ‘says’, and vutta ‘said’, the past participle Pāli uses for that verb, both mean ‘saying’ done and over, though DPD builds the first on √vad and the second on √vac. Which of the two carries a voice distinction (active vs passive) and which does not, and why does that difference follow directly from what each formation is?

    Two subordinations in one sentence. The Vinaya’s case law puts an absolutive and a locative absolute side by side more often than the suttas do, because a legal narrative has to keep track of who did what.

    1. Tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu saṅghassa cīvare bhājīyamāne theyyacitto kusaṁ saṅkāmetvā cīvaraṁ aggahesi. (Bu Pj 2 — Vin iii 58) — Gloss it and render it. Two subordinate constructions stand here. Name each, say whose action each reports, and show that the rule of Chapter 16 decides which one the sentence had to use in each place. One of the two also happens to be a causative.

    Coming back — absolutives and infinitives

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 7–11 and 12–19. The default agent rule and its one exception go first, because every reading question after them depends on it.

    Day 10. Items 20–24 and 28. If item 28’s two sentences still separate without the chapter open, the exception has been understood, not memorised.

    Day 30. Items 5 and 6 aloud, and 25–26 and 31. If item 31’s two constructions still sort themselves, the absolutive and the locative absolute have stopped being interchangeable.
