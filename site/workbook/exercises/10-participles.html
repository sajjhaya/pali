    Exercise · Chapter 10

    Exercises: Participles

    The chapter’s one rule to carry into every sentence you meet from here on is that a past participle’s voice depends on the verb it is built from, not on the participle itself. Everything else in this set (the past active participle, the gerundive’s true name, the bare--a type) is less a new rule than a new place to look.

    A participle is the commonest thing in Pāli that a reader half-parses and moves past, so stage 1 gives four lines in which the participle is the whole sentence. Stage 4 tests the voice rule. Every Pāli line below is canonical and cited; none has been composed. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — participles

    Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear which participle is carrying the sentence.

    1. Idha, mahāli, bhikkhu tiṇṇaṁ saṁyojanānaṁ parikkhayā sotāpanno hoti avinipātadhammo niyato sambodhiparāyaṇo. (DN 6 — D i 156)

      idhaheremahāliMahālim voc sgbhikkhua monkm nom sgtiṇṇaṁ saṁyojanānaṁof the three fettersn gen plparikkhayāthrough the complete endingm abl sgsotāpannoa stream-entererm nom sg, pphotiisavinipātadhammonot liable to fall to a lower realmm nom sgniyatobound, destinedm nom sg, ppsambodhiparāyaṇoheaded for awakeningm nom sg

      “Here, Mahāli, through the complete ending of the three fetters a monk is a stream-enterer, not liable to fall to a lower realm, bound for and headed toward awakening.”

      Say it aloud. The most consequential word in the sentence is a past participle being used as a noun. Say which one, and say what verb it is built on. Then say which of the other words in the predicate are participles and which only look like them.

    2. Tiṭṭhaṁ caraṁ nisinno va, Sayāno yāvatāssa vitamiddho; (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

      tiṭṭhaṁstandingm nom sg, pres partcaraṁwalkingm nom sg, pres partnisinnoseatedm nom sg, ppvaorsayānolying downm nom sg, pres partyāvatā·assa< yāvatā as long as + assa he isvitamiddhofree of drowsinessm nom sg

      “Standing, walking, seated or lying down, as long as he is free of drowsiness …”

      Say it aloud. Four postures, and three different participle endings inside them. Set 08 took the second half of this same verse for its perfect; here the first half is wanted for something else. Name the ending on each of the four words, and say which one belongs to the older type that only a handful of verbs keep; the answer to that is also the answer to item 11.

    3. The first sermon turns each of the four truths three times, and the second and third turns are this chapter’s two participles set against each other.

      Taṁ kho panidaṁ dukkhaṁ ariyasaccaṁ pariññeyyan’ti. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)

      Taṁ kho panidaṁ dukkhaṁ ariyasaccaṁ pariññātan’ti. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)

      Taṁ kho panidaṁ dukkhasamudayaṁ ariyasaccaṁ pahātabban’ti. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)

      Taṁ kho panidaṁ dukkhasamudayaṁ ariyasaccaṁ pahīnan’ti. (SN 56.11 — S v 422)

      taṁ kho panidaṁnow thisdukkhaṁ ariyasaccaṁthe noble truth of sufferingn nom sgpariññeyyaṁis to be fully understoodn nom sg, gerundivepariññātaṁhas been fully understoodn nom sg, ppdukkhasamudayaṁ ariyasaccaṁthe noble truth of the origin of sufferingn nom sgpahātabbaṁis to be abandonedn nom sg, gerundivepahīnaṁhas been abandonedn nom sg, ppti(quotation)

      “‘Now this noble truth of suffering is to be fully understood.’ … ‘Now this noble truth of suffering has been fully understood.’ … ‘Now this noble truth of the origin of suffering is to be abandoned.’ … ‘Now this noble truth of the origin of suffering has been abandoned.’”

      Say all four aloud, in order. The third and fourth truths do the same thing with sacchikātabbaṁ against sacchikataṁ and bhāvetabbaṁ against bhāvitaṁ. Eight words, four verbs, two participles apiece, and between them they carry the whole doctrinal architecture of the sermon. Say what the difference between the two members of each pair is, in one sentence, without using the words “gerundive” or “past participle”.

    4. Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 57)

      yathā yathā vāin whatever waypana·assanow, for himkāyothe bodym nom sgpaṇihitopositioned, disposedm nom sg, pphotiistathā tathāin just that waynaṁitm acc sgpajānātihe understands

      “And however his body is positioned, that is how he understands it.”

      This follows directly on the four-postures passage the chapter quotes at 6.1, and it sums that passage up. Say it aloud. One participle, and it does what four finite verbs did in the sentence before it. Say what paṇihito is built on, and why a generalising sentence of this kind needs a participle, not a verb.

    Vacasā paricitā — participles

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud. Both are formulas, and a formula in the mouth is several participles learnt at once.

    1. ’Khīṇā jāti, vusitaṁ brahmacariyaṁ, kataṁ karaṇīyaṁ, nāparaṁ itthattāyā’ti pajānāti. (SN 22.79 — S iii 90)

      khīṇāexhaustedf nom sg, ppjātibirthf nom sgvusitaṁlivedn nom sg, ppbrahmacariyaṁthe spiritual lifen nom sgkataṁdonen nom sg, ppkaraṇīyaṁwhat had to be donen nom sg, gerundivenanotaparaṁfurthern nom sgitthattāyafor this state of beingm dat sgti(quotation)pajānātihe understands

      “He understands: ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no further coming to any state of being.’”

      The arahant’s declaration, fixed word for word across the canon. Rebuild it from the gloss. Four participles in eight words, and one of them is not a past participle at all. Find it, and say why it stands next to the past participle of the very same verb.

    2. Adinnaṁ nāma yaṁ adinnaṁ anissaṭṭhaṁ apariccattaṁ rakkhitaṁ gopitaṁ mamāyitaṁ parapariggahitaṁ. Etaṁ adinnaṁ nāma. (Bu Pj 2)

      adinnaṁ nāma“what is not given” meansyaṁwhichn nom sgadinnaṁnot givenanissaṭṭhaṁnot let go ofapariccattaṁnot relinquishedrakkhitaṁguardedgopitaṁprotectedmamāyitaṁtreated as one’s ownparapariggahitaṁpossessed by anotheretaṁthisn nom sg

      “‘What is not given’ means: what is not given, not let go of, not relinquished; what is guarded, protected, treated as one’s own, possessed by another. That is what ‘what is not given’ means.”

      The Vinaya defining theft’s object by piling up past participles until nothing is left out. Rebuild the list from the gloss. It is harder than it looks, and the order is not arbitrary. Every word between yaṁ and the full stop is a past participle. Say what each is from, sort them into the ones carrying the negative prefix and the ones not, and say what gender and number they are all in and why.

    Manasānupekkhitā — participles

    Cover the chapter. These are answered from memory or not at all.

    Recall. Answer these in a phrase, before opening anything.

    1. What does a participle inherit from a verb, and what from an adjective?
    2. State in one sentence which verbs give the past participle a passive sense and which give it an active sense.
    3. Name the two participle types no coursebook consulted for this chapter taught on its own, and say what each is built from.
    4. Why is “future participle” a misleading name for the gerundive?
    5. Which verb keeps the older, bare -āna present-participle ending on the plain root, with no -m- at all?

    Identify the formation. For each: which participle is it, what voice does it carry, and what is it built from? Two of these are traps; say what the trap is.

    1. gato
    2. desito
    3. kātabbaṁ
    4. vusitavā
    5. karonto
    6. dukkaraṁ
    7. asantaṁ
    8. sotāpanno

    Build the form. Give the form, and show the step that produces it.

    1. The past participle of dadāti ‘gives’.
    2. The gerundive, -tabba type, of pajahati ‘abandons’.
    3. The past active participle of the verb behind vijitāvī’s own root sense, ‘has conquered’.
    4. The present participle (-(n)t type, nom. sg. m.) of jānāti ‘knows’.
    5. The present participle (-māna type) of vedeti ‘feels’.

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — participles

    Put it back into Pāli. Each of these is the rendering of a real sentence. Reconstruct the Pāli. The words you need are given; what you have to supply is the participle. Do not settle for a sentence that merely works; the answer is a particular line of canon, and getting close to it is the exercise.

    1. “House-builder, you are seen!”: gahakāraka (vocative), the past participle of dassati, and the second person of atthi, fused onto it.

    2. “A living being is not to be killed.”: pāṇa-, na, the -tabba gerundive of hanati ‘kills’.

    Read at length. Read the following passage through, twice, without stopping to parse: the first time for the build of the sentences, the second for the sense. Then go back and answer the questions under it.

    1. “Bhojanaṁ, suppavāse, dentī ariyasāvikā paṭiggāhakānaṁ cattāri ṭhānāni deti. Katamāni cattāri? Āyuṁ deti, vaṇṇaṁ deti, sukhaṁ deti, balaṁ deti. Āyuṁ kho pana datvā āyussa bhāginī hoti dibbassa vā mānusassa vā. Vaṇṇaṁ datvā vaṇṇassa bhāginī hoti dibbassa vā mānusassa vā. Sukhaṁ datvā sukhassa bhāginī hoti dibbassa vā mānusassa vā. Balaṁ datvā balassa bhāginī hoti dibbassa vā mānusassa vā. Bhojanaṁ, suppavāse, dentī ariyasāvikā paṭiggāhakānaṁ imāni cattāri ṭhānāni detīti. (AN 4.57 — A ii 63)

      • Dentī opens the passage and closes it, framing everything between. What kind of participle is it, and what does its feminine ending tell you about who is speaking?
      • Datvā, repeated four times, is not a participle this chapter has taught. What is it, and which chapter will give it its own section?
      • Bhāginī ‘sharer’ looks like it could be a participle and is not. What is it actually, and what does the resemblance tell you about how the possessive adjectives in -in and the past active participle in -in were built?
      • Nothing in this passage is a gerundive, although the whole passage is about an obligation of sorts: giving food well. Why would a writer choose four plain finite verbs (deti) over four gerundives here?

    Test the voice rule. The chapter’s central claim is that a past participle’s voice comes from the verb underneath it. This set has now printed enough past participles to check the claim, not take it on trust.

    1. Take sotāpanno from item 1, pariññātaṁ and pahīnaṁ from item 3, and khīṇā, vusitaṁ and kataṁ from item 5. Sort all six into the ones that mean “having done” and the ones that mean “having been done”. Then, for each, name the verb it is built on and say whether that verb is transitive. If the rule holds, the two sortings agree. Say whether they do, and say what you would have to conclude about any that did not.

    Reaching back. Two items reaching over earlier chapters.

    1. Chapter 9 taught kāreti as the causative of karoti. Chapter 10 corrected a source’s kārayamāna, given as the plain present middle participle of karoti, to kurumāno/kubbamāno, while noting that kārayamāna is itself a real word meaning something else. What does it actually mean, which chapter’s material shows you that, and why would looking it up as kārayamāno have suggested it did not exist?

    2. Chapter 7 gave atthi’s irregular present as asmi, amhi, amha. Chapter 10’s santo is the very same verb’s present participle. Which paradigm does it inflect by, and why is that not the surprise it first looks like?

    Say it aloud. The verse that gives this chapter its most famous line, and which set 08 took for its tenses.

    1. Gahakāraka diṭṭhosi, puna gehaṁ na kāhasi; sabbā te phāsukā bhaggā, gahakūṭaṁ visaṅkhataṁ; visaṅkhāragataṁ cittaṁ, taṇhānaṁ khayamajjhagā. (Dhp 154 — Dhp p. 23) — Read it aloud several times, holding the long vowels for two beats and the doubled consonants for the full length of both letters. Then answer: two words in the second half share a root that means ‘construct, put together’ — visaṅkhataṁ and visaṅkhāragataṁ. The first describes the roof-peak; the second describes the mind. What does the verse achieve by using the same root twice, once literally and once figuratively?

    Coming back — participles

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 7–11 and 12–19. The voice rule and the two under-taught types go before anything else, because every item after them is an application.

    Day 10. Items 20–24 and 28. If the two sortings in 28 still agree without the chapter open, the rule has been understood, not memorised.

    Day 30. Items 3, 5 and 6 aloud, and 25–26. If item 6’s seven participles come back in the right order, the Vinaya’s way of defining a thing by exhausting it has become legible.
