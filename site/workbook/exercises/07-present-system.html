    Exercises: The Present System

    The chapter’s thesis is that the reader works from the present stem, not the root, and that three of Pāli’s moods are built on it. So the third stage asks you to get back to the stem, and then to deal with the consequence: because the three moods share one stem, several forms belong to more than one of them, and only the sentence decides.

    The first two stages come before that and are arranged around the moods, not around the verbs, because once you have heard the imperative and the optative in real lines you will not confuse them later. Every Pāli line below is canonical and cited; none has been composed. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — the present system

    Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear which mood is speaking.

    1. Pare ca na vijānanti, mayamettha yamāmase; Ye ca tattha vijānanti, tato sammanti medhagā. (Dhp 6 — Dhp p. 2)

      pareothersm nom plcaandnanotvijānantiunderstand3 pl presmayaṁwenom pletthahereyamāmaselet us restrain ourselves1 pl imp middleyethose whom nom pltatthatheretatothereuponsammantiare appeased3 pl presmedhagāquarrelsm nom pl

      “Others do not understand that here we must restrain ourselves; but those who do understand — thereupon their quarrels are appeased.”

      The chapter quotes the first two lines of this from MN 128, where the Buddha says them to the quarrelling monks of Kosambī. Say the whole verse aloud. Three of its four finite verbs are ordinary present indicatives and the fourth is not, and the fourth is the only middle form most readers will ever meet. Say which it is, and say what in its ending marks it off from the other three.

    2. Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno; Attānaṁ upamaṁ katvā, na haneyya na ghātaye. (Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19)

      sabbeallm nom pltasantitremble3 pl presdaṇḍassaat the rodm gen sgbhāyantifear3 pl presmaccunodeathm gen sgattānaṁoneselfm acc sgupamaṁas the comparisonf acc sgkatvāhaving madeabsnanothaneyyashould kill3 sg optghātayeshould have [another] kill3 sg opt

      “All tremble at the rod, all fear death. Taking oneself as the measure, one should neither kill nor have another killed.”

      Say it aloud. The verse turns on its hinge: two present indicatives in the first half, two optatives in the second, and the mood carries the whole argument, because the first half states what is and the second what one ought therefore to do. Then look at the two optatives. They are the same mood, the same person and the same number, and they do not look alike at all. Say what the difference is.

    3. Na paro paraṁ nikubbetha, nātimaññetha katthaci na kañci; Byārosanā paṭighasaññā, nāññamaññassa dukkhamiccheyya. (Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26)

      nanotparoone personm nom sgparaṁanotherm acc sgnikubbethashould deceive3 sg opt middlena·atimaññethashould not despise3 sg opt middlekatthacianywherekañcianyonem acc sgbyārosanāangerfpaṭighasaññāthought of enmityfna·aññamaññassanot for one anotherm gen sgdukkham·iccheyya< dukkhaṁ suffering n acc sg + iccheyya should wish 3 sg opt

      “Let no one deceive another, nor despise anyone anywhere; in anger or in thoughts of enmity, let none wish suffering upon another.”

      From the Mettasutta, which a great many readers will already know by heart, so say it from memory if you can. Three optatives in four lines, and two of them are middle. Say which two, and say what the third does differently. Then say why a verse of this kind is where such forms are found.

    4. Rūpaṁ ’netaṁ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā’ti samanupassati. (MN 22 — M i 136)

      rūpaṁformn acc sgna·etaṁnot thisn nom sgmamamine1 sg genna·eso·ahaṁ·asmi< na not + eso this m nom sg + ahaṁ I nom sg + asmi am 1 sg pres of atthinanotme·esothis of minem nom sgattāselfm nom sgti(quotation)samanupassatiregards3 sg pres

      “One regards form thus: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’”

      Say it aloud, slowly, and count the words. One of them is four words printed as one, and one of the four is the first person singular of the commonest verb in the language. Separate them out. Then say what the chapter warns will happen if you go looking for that verb form on its own in a text.

    Vacasā paricitā — the present system

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud. Producing a mood is the work; recognising one is not.

    1. Handa dāni, bhikkhave, āmantayāmi vo, vayadhammā saṅkhārā appamādena sampādethā’ti. (DN 16 — D ii 156)

      handacome nowdāninowbhikkhavemonksm voc plāmantayāmiI address1 sg presvoyou2 pl accvaya·dhammāsubject to decaym nom plsaṅkhārāconditioned thingsm nom plappamādenawith diligencem ins sgsampādethastrive on2 pl impti(quotation)

      “Come now, monks, I address you: conditioned things are subject to decay; strive on with diligence.”

      The last words the Buddha spoke. Recite them, then rebuild the whole from the gloss. Two finite verbs, one present indicative and one imperative, and both stems belong to the same conjugation class: the eighth, whose affix the chapter’s table gives twice over. Say what the two stems are, and say which of the two forms the affix takes in each. Then say why the second verb needs no pronoun to tell the monks it is addressed to them.

    2. Attanā hi kataṁ pāpaṁ, attanā saṅkilissati; Attanā akataṁ pāpaṁ, attanāva visujjhati; Suddhī asuddhi paccattaṁ, nāñño aññaṁ visodhaye. (Dhp 165 — Dhp p. 24)

      attanāby oneselfm ins sghiindeedkataṁdonen nom sg, pppāpaṁeviln nom sgsaṅkilissatiis defiled3 sg presakataṁnot donen nom sg, ppattanā·evaby oneself alonevisujjhatiis purified3 sg pressuddhīpurityf nom sgasuddhiimpurityf nom sgpaccattaṁindividuallyadvna·aññono otherm nom sgaññaṁanotherm acc sgvisodhayecan purify3 sg opt, causative

      “Evil is done by oneself; by oneself one is defiled. Evil is left undone by oneself; by oneself alone one is purified. Purity and impurity are one’s own; no one purifies another.”

      Rebuild it from the gloss, and expect the last word to be the one that will not come. The verse’s last two verbs are the same verb twice, once plain and once causative, and the chapter’s section on vowel gradation is what explains the difference between them. Say what that difference is before you look it up.

    Manasānupekkhitā — the present system

    Cover the chapter. These are answered from memory or not at all.

    Identify the form. For each, give the dictionary form of the verb, its present stem, and the person, number, tense or mood, and voice of the form as it stands.

    1. suṇāma
    2. karonti
    3. gacchāhi
    4. jāneyyuṁ
    5. bhavantu
    6. yamāmase

    Resolve the ambiguity. Each of these can be parsed in more than one way, or looks like something it is not. Give every reading the form allows, and say what in a sentence would decide between them.

    1. gacchāma
    2. desetha
    3. siyā
    4. vuccati

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — the present system

    Read the verse. Give each a word-by-word gloss and then an idiomatic rendering, resolving any sandhi in the gloss. Account for every finite verb: its stem, its mood, and, where the mood allows more than one sense, which sense the verse needs.

    1. Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā; Manasā ce paduṭṭhena, bhāsati vā karoti vā; Tato naṁ dukkhamanveti, cakkaṁva vahato padaṁ. (Dhp 1 — Dhp p. 1)
    2. Na hi verena verāni, sammantīdha kudācanaṁ; Averena ca sammanti, esa dhammo sanantano. (Dhp 5 — Dhp p. 2)
    3. Hīnaṁ dhammaṁ na seveyya, pamādena na saṁvase; Micchādiṭṭhiṁ na seveyya, na siyā lokavaḍḍhano. (Dhp 167 — Dhp p. 25)

    One imperative, two numbers. The chapter gives the third person singular imperative that opens a formal act. Here is the same formula when the act is done before a group instead of the whole Sangha.

    1. Suṇantu me āyasmantā. (Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 197) — Gloss it and render it. Name the verb’s person, number and mood, and say what me is. Then say what changes and what does not between this and Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho.

    Two verbs for ‘is’. The chapter says the difference between atthi and hoti “is not absolute but it is the way to bet”. Here is each of them doing the job the other could not.

    1. Atthi imasmiṁ kāye kesā lomā nakhā dantā taco … (MN 10 — M i 57)

      Jātassa maraṇaṁ hoti, jāto dukkhāni phussati; (SN 5.6 — S i 132)

      Say which verb each line uses and what it is asserting. Then swap them, and say what each sentence would then mean, or fail to mean. One of the two lines also has its verb disagreeing in number with what follows it; find it, and say whether that is an error.

    Put it back into Pāli. Every item so far has run from a printed form to the mood behind it. Now run it the other way. Each phrase below occurs in a line already in this set. Give the Pāli from memory, naming the mood and the person and number of the verb, and only then look back at the line it came from.

    1. Four phrases, one verb each:

      1. “they fear death” — Dhp 129 — Dhp p. 19

      2. “let none wish suffering upon another” — Snp 1.8 — Sn p. 26

      3. “strive on with diligence” — DN 16 — D ii 156

      4. “let the venerables listen to me” — Bu Np 1 — Vin iii 197

    Coming back — the present system

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 1–4 aloud, and 7–12. The stem goes before everything else, because every form in the chapter is built on it.

    Day 10. Items 13–16, and 20. If the three overlapping cells still come out as overlaps and not as errors, the paradigm has been understood, not memorised.

    Day 30. Items 5, 6 and 22. If (b) and (c) in 22 still come out, the optative and the imperative have separated for good.
