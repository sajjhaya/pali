    Exercises: Relative Clauses

    This chapter’s claim is that Pāli subordinates by pairing, not by embedding, and that the relative clause therefore arrives before there is anything to attach it to. So the set is arranged around the reader’s actual difficulty (what is the answering word, and what does each half of the pair agree with?), and only then asks what kind of clause it is.

    Every sentence below is real canon, and cited. Stage 1 gives you four pairs with both halves already named, and the group that follows in stage 3 is the same exercise on four sentences where the pairing goes wrong in some way. Six of the later items contain a trap. They are not marked. Where you find one, say what the trap is as well as what the answer is. Worked answers are at the back.

    Sotānugata — relative clauses

    Nothing is withheld. Each line comes with its gloss and its rendering, and the work is to say it and to hear the two halves answer each other.

    1. Atha kho bhagavā yena dvārena nikkhami, taṁ gotamadvāraṁ nāma ahosi. (DN 16 — D ii 89)

      atha khothenbhagavāthe Blessed Oneyena dvārenaby which gaten ins sgnikkhamiwent outaortaṁthatn nom sggotamadvāraṁthe Gotama Gaten nom sgnāmaby nameahosibecameaor

      “The gate by which the Blessed One went out came to be called the Gotama Gate.”

      Say it aloud. This is the construction at its plainest: one word opens, one word closes, and they are the same word twice. Name the two, and say what is identical about them and what is not. Then say which half of the concord rule accounts for the sameness and which half for the difference. The rule has two halves and this sentence needs both.

    2. Yo, bhikkhave, rāgakkhayo dosakkhayo mohakkhayo — idaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, asaṅkhataṁ. (SN 43.12 — S iv 362)

      yowhichm nom sgbhikkhavemonksrāgakkhayothe ending of greedm nom sgdosakkhayothe ending of hatemohakkhayothe ending of delusionidaṁthisn nom sgvuccatiis calledasaṅkhataṁthe unconditionedn nom sg

      “The ending of greed, the ending of hate, the ending of delusion: this, monks, is called the unconditioned.”

      Say it aloud. The answering word is not from the same stem as the relative, and it is not even the same gender. Name it, and say what each of the two takes its gender from. If you have learnt yo … so as a fixed pair you will look past this one entirely, so say also what the general rule is that yo … idaṁ obeys.

    3. Yāni kānici, bhikkhave, bhayāni uppajjanti sabbāni tāni bālato uppajjanti, no paṇḍitato. (AN 3.1 — A i 101)

      yāni kāniciwhatevern nom plbhayānidangersn nom pluppajjantiarisesabbāni tāniall thosen nom plbālatofrom the foolm abl sgno paṇḍitatonot from the astute one

      “Whatever dangers arise, monks, all of them arise from the fool, not from the astute one.”

      Say it aloud. The relative has been generalised and so has the word that answers it, and each has taken a second word to do it. Name both additions, and say what each contributes. Then say why a generalised relative needs a generalised correlative at all, when item 1 managed with a bare pair.

    4. Yā kho, bhikkhave, imesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁ saṅgati sannipāto samavāyo, ayaṁ vuccati cakkhusamphasso. (SN 35.93 — S iv 68)

      yāwhichf nom sgimesaṁ tiṇṇaṁ dhammānaṁof these three thingsm gen plsaṅgatithe meetingf nom sgsannipātothe coming togetherm nom sgsamavāyothe concurrencem nom sgayaṁthism nom sgcakkhusamphassoeye-contactm nom sg

      “The meeting, the coming together, the concurrence of these three things is called eye-contact.”

      Say it aloud. The two pronouns point at the same thing and are in different genders, and neither is wrong. Say what each takes its gender from. There are three nouns in the relative clause and they are not all the same gender, so name which one yā has chosen and why. This is the sentence to keep in mind for the four items of stage 3 that ask the same question and get a different answer.

    Vacasā paricitā — relative clauses

    Recite each until it is fluent. Then cover the Pāli and rebuild it from the gloss alone, aloud.

    1. Yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ. (SN 56.11 — S v 423)

      yaṁ kiñciwhatevern nom sgsamudayadhammaṁis of the nature to arisen nom sg, adj compound samudaya + dhammasabbaṁ taṁall thatn nom sgnirodhadhammaṁis of the nature to ceasen nom sg, nirodha + dhamma

      “Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease.”

      This is what Koṇḍañña saw, and it is probably the best-known relative sentence in the canon; most readers will have it already. Recite it and rebuild it from the gloss. Then: there is no verb in it anywhere. Say what holds the sentence together in place of one, and how the two halves bear on each other. Then compare it with item 3 and say what the two constructions have in common.

    2. Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti. (MN 10 — M i 57)

      yathā yathāin whatever wayvā panaor moreoverassahism gen sgkāyobodym nom sgpaṇihitois disposedpphotiistathā tathāin just that waynaṁitm acc sgpajānātihe understands

      “Or however his body is disposed, he understands it accordingly.”

      Recite it and rebuild it from the gloss. Both halves of the pair are doubled, and the doubling is not emphasis. Say what it contributes, and why the instruction would be a weaker one if either word stood singly. Then name the kind of clause the pair opens.

    3. Yasmā ca kho, ānanda, suññaṁ attena vā attaniyena vā tasmā suñño lokoti vuccati. (SN 35.85 — S iv 54)

      yasmābecauseca kho(filler)ānandaĀnandasuññaṁemptyn nom sgattenaof a selfm ins sgvā … vāeither … orattaniyenaof what belongs to a selfn ins sgtasmāthereforesuññoemptym nom sglokothe worldm nom sgtiend of quotationvuccatiis called

      “Because it is empty of a self or of what belongs to a self, Ānanda, it is said that the world is empty.”

      Recite it and rebuild it from the gloss. Name the kind of clause the pair opens. Then note the order: the reason stands first and the conclusion second. Say whether that is the ordinary Pāli order or the exceptional one, and what an English speaker’s habit would have done with the same two clauses. This sentence is the Buddha’s reply to a question, and item 27 is that question.

    Manasānupekkhitā — relative clauses

    Recall. Answer from memory, in a phrase, before opening the chapter.

    1. State the rule that decides the number, gender and case of a relative pronoun. It has two halves; give both, and say which half identifies what the pronoun points at.

    2. The tradition calls the relative pronoun aniyamattha. Translate the term, and say in one sentence why it is a better name for the class than “relative”.

    3. Yad- is in the paradigm and occurs nowhere in the canon as a word. Explain both facts at once.

    4. Warder ranks the relative words by how much each of them means. Name the two he puts at the top of that ranking, and say why the ranking is also a difficulty ranking.

    5. Name Buddhaghosa’s four uses of yaṁ by the cases they correspond to. What single claim about the empty relative do the four of them together amount to?

    6. Two words mean “because” and open a causal clause. Name both, and say which of the two belongs more to the canon and which to the commentaries.

    7. Perniola says a relative clause or phrase can be expressed five ways. Name three of the four that are not the correlative sentence.

    Find the answering word. For each sentence, name the word in the main clause that answers the relative, or say that there is none. Where there is none, say what is standing in for it. These are the three sentences of that group in which something is missing; items 1 to 3 are the three in which nothing is.

    1. Ayaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, bhikkhu phegguṁ aggahesi brahmacariyassa. (MN 29 — M i 196)

    2. Yāvakīvañca, ānanda, vajjī abhiṇhaṁ sannipātā sannipātabahulā bhavissanti, vuddhiyeva, ānanda, vajjīnaṁ pāṭikaṅkhā, no parihāni. (DN 16 — D ii 73)

    3. Ko nu kho, bhante, hetu ko paccayo, yena mātugāmo neva sabhāyaṁ nisīdati …? (AN 4.80 — A ii 82)

    What agrees with what. For each bolded pronoun, say what it takes its gender and number from, and what it takes its case from. Name both sources separately.

    1. Tatra, bhikkhave, yo so satto paṭhamaṁ upapanno tassa evaṁ hoti. (DN 1 — D i 18)

    2. Yāvatakaṁ, bhante, sekhena ñāṇena sekhāya vijjāya pattabbaṁ, anuppattaṁ taṁ mayā. (MN 73 — M i 494)

    3. Vinicchayā yāni pakappitāni, (iti māgaṇḍiyo) Te ve munī brūsi anuggahāya. (Snp 4.9 — Sn p. 164)

    4. Yāvakīvañca, ānanda, vajjī yā tā kulitthiyo kulakumāriyo, tā na okkassa pasayha vāsessanti, vuddhiyeva, ānanda, vajjīnaṁ pāṭikaṅkhā, no parihāni. (DN 16 — D ii 74)

    Supply the case of the empty relative. Each of these carries a yaṁ that does not obviously belong to any noun. Following Buddhaghosa’s method, resolve it: say which case it stands for, and rewrite the phrase with that case made explicit, as he rewrites yaṁ vipassī as yasmiṁ kappe vipassī.

    1. Ito so, bhikkhave, ekanavutikappe yaṁ vipassī bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi. (DN 14 — D ii 2)

    2. Aṭṭhānametaṁ anavakāso yaṁ ekissā lokadhātuyā dve arahanto sammāsambuddhā apubbaṁ acarimaṁ uppajjeyyuṁ, netaṁ ṭhānaṁ vijjati. (DN 28 — D iii 114)

    3. Tasmātihānanda, tuyhevetaṁ dukkaṭaṁ … yaṁ tvaṁ … na tathāgataṁ yāci. (DN 16 — D ii 115)

    4. So, ānanda, samayo yaṁ mahāvātā vāyanti; mahāvātā vāyantā udakaṁ kampenti. (AN 8.70 — A iv 312)

    Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā — relative clauses

    Name the clause and its sense. For each, say what kind of subordinate clause the relative word opens (time, cause, place, manner, generalisation), and give the sense of the pair.

    1. Yato ca kho, bhikkhave, tathāgato loke uppajjati arahaṁ sammāsambuddho, atha mahato ālokassa pātubhāvo hoti mahato obhāsassa. (SN 56.38 — S v 443)

    2. Kittāvatā nu kho, bhante, suñño lokoti vuccatī ti? (SN 35.85 — S iv 54)

    What the counts license. Three items that hand you a count and ask what may be concluded from it. Every figure is a count over the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, given for the root texts, the commentaries, the sub-commentaries, and the literature outside all three.

    1. Warder says yasmā is “a rarely used synonym of yato”. In the root texts yato stands 1,370 times against yasmā’s 374. In the commentaries the order reverses, yasmā 3,317 against yato’s 590, and in the sub-commentaries it reverses again the same way, 1,605 against 327. State what is right about his claim, and what the counts add to it.

    2. The chapter gives yāvataka and yattaka as relative adjectives of the same meaning. In the root texts yāvatak- occurs 117 times and its correlative tāvatak- 78; yattak- occurs 12 times and tattak- 15, of which four are tatta- “heated” inside a compound and not the pronoun at all. Say what you would tell a beginner to learn and what you would tell them to skip. Then say why those counts had to be taken on the stem yāvatak-, not on the dictionary form yāvataka.

    3. Yesānaṁ occurs seven times in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana, and not once in the root texts, the commentaries or the sub-commentaries. Six of the seven are declension runs in Kaccāyana, the Padarūpasiddhi and the Saddanīti, grammars printing a paradigm, and the seventh is a verse in the Thūpavaṁsa, a mediaeval chronicle. What does that license you to say about the form, and what does it not license you to say?

    Adjudicate.

    1. Grammars differ on Buddhaghosa’s third use of yaṁ, the one he calls karaṇavacane. Set out the two readings and the evidence for each, and say which you would follow and why. Do not settle it by counting.

    2. A textbook prints the formula as Yad ’aniccaṁ, taṁ dukkhaṁ, with an apostrophe, on a quotation line. The canon has it solid, as yadaniccaṁ. Say what is wrong with the printed form, why the impulse behind it is nonetheless right, and where in this book’s three-layer example format the resolution belongs.

    3. Perniola illustrates the kammadhāraya alternative to a relative clause with jātapuriso kālaṁ karissati. Look for it in the canon, as a whole sentence and word by word. Then say what the result does and does not tell you about the construction he is illustrating.

    Read the sentence. Give a literal rendering and then an idiomatic one, and say in a phrase what the relative construction does in each.

    1. Sukarāni asādhūni, attano ahitāni ca; Yaṁ ve hitañca sādhuñca, taṁ ve paramadukkaraṁ. (Dhp 163 — Dhp p. 24)

    2. Yassa accantadussīlyaṁ, māluvā sālamivotthataṁ; Karoti so tathattānaṁ, yathā naṁ icchatī diso. (Dhp 162 — Dhp p. 24)

    The relative with nothing to hold on to. The Khandhaka’s minor rules are the shortest sentences in the canon that are still whole sentences. Two of them, differing in one word.

    1. Yo ugghaṁseyya, āpatti dukkaṭassa. (Kd 15 — Vin ii 105) — Render it. Then say what yo agrees with, what answers it, and how a reader knows who is meant.

    2. Yo kārāpeyya, āpatti dukkaṭassa. (Kd 15 — Vin ii 133) — The same sentence with one word changed. Render it, and say what the change does to the person the rule catches.

    The reading passage. DN 16 — D ii 74: three of the seven conditions for the non-decline of the Vajjians, in the form the Buddha states them to Ānanda.

    The passage is chosen because it repeats one construction three times and changes only the gender: ye te … te of the elders, yā tā … tā of the women and girls, yāni tāni … tāni of the shrines. No other passage in the canon lets you watch the concord rule work three times over on the same sentence frame.

    1. Set the three conditions out side by side and mark, in each, the outer relative, the inner relative, and every word that answers either. Two of the three carry an answering word in a case the inner relative is not in; find it, and say why it stands there.

    2. The Buddha’s statement of each condition is in the future tense, and Ānanda’s report of the same fact a sentence earlier is in the present. Account for the difference using the tense rule in the chapter, and say what the future conveys that a translation into English “as long as they honour” leaves out.

    3. Yāvakīvañca is answered by nothing in any of the three. Say what closes the yāva clause instead, and why the construction is not defective.

    Coming back — relative clauses

    The set is not finished on the day it is worked. Re-attempt these, from cold, without reading the answers first.

    Day 3. Items 15–21. Finding the answering word and saying what each half agrees with is the whole of the reading skill, and five of these seven are the sentences where the ordinary expectation fails. If you can say why each fails, the rule is understood, not memorised.

    Day 10. Items 22–25, and items 36 and 37. The empty relative is the hardest thing in the chapter because there is nothing on the page to work from, and the two Khandhaka rules are the same difficulty stripped to six words apiece.

    Day 30. Items 1 to 7 aloud, and item 38. If items 5 and 7 still come back whole (the sentence Koṇḍañña saw and the sentence about the empty world), then the two commonest shapes in the canon are in the memory, and the reading passage is only those shapes repeated with the gender changed.
